The **consciousness-reading machine** is a thought experiment about the limits of the science of
consciousness. Imagine a device that scans a person's brain and displays what they are experiencing
at that moment: the scene they see, the tune running in their head, the ache in their knee, the
worry at the back of their mind. The thought experiment raises three questions. Is such a machine
possible, even in principle? Would it be different from a machine that reads the contents of
short-term memory, or whatever a person is ready to report? And if someone built one, how could we
tell that it really does what it claims?

The best-known philosophical version is the *autocerebroscope*, a device imagined by Herbert Feigl
([1958](../sources/feigl-1958.md)) that shows a person the state of their own brain while they experience,
and developed by Paul Meehl ([1966](../sources/meehl-1966.md)) into a detailed thought experiment.

The questions matter beyond the thought experiment. Everything the science of consciousness knows
about experience, including the [neural-correlates-of-consciousness](../concepts/neural-correlates-of-consciousness.md), has been calibrated
against what subjects say, so the machine is a test of whether that science could ever reach
experience itself rather than its reports.

### Is it possible?

**Existing decoders.** Machines of a limited kind already exist. Trained on a person's brain
recordings, decoders can reconstruct the rough content of what the person is seeing, or the gist of a
story they are hearing or imagining. Tang and colleagues ([2023](../sources/tang-2023.md)) recovered the
meaning of continuous speech from fMRI recordings, though only with a decoder trained at length on
that subject and only with the subject's cooperation. Such decoders are statistical: they learn which
patterns of activity go with which stimuli in a training set, and they guess, often loosely, for new
cases ([Roskies, SEP](../sources/sep-neuroethics.md)).

**The argument from supervenience.** If the contents of experience are fixed by the state of the
brain, as physicalists and most dualists agree, then different experiences go with different brain
states, and in principle a machine could tell which is which. This is the main reason to think a
perfect reader possible. It requires only that experience vary with the brain, not that it be
identical to the brain, so even a property dualist can accept it.

**Obstacles in principle.** Three doubts remain.
- *Individuality.* The same experience may be realized differently in different brains, so a reader
  may need to learn each brain separately, as current decoders do.
- *Content.* Some philosophers hold that what a thought is about depends partly on the thinker's
  environment and history, not only on the brain, so a brain scan would underdetermine it.
- *Character.* Even if the machine could say *that* someone sees red, it is unclear how its output
  could show *what it is like* for them, except by describing it in words or by recreating a similar
  experience in the user. This is the point of the [explanatory-gap](../concepts/explanatory-gap.md): a correlation between
  a brain state and an experience does not make the experience readable from the physical
  description.

### Is it different from reading short-term memory?

A machine that read working memory, or more broadly whatever information is available to report and
reasoning, would read [access-consciousness](../concepts/access-consciousness.md). Whether that is the same as reading experience
depends on the relation between [access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness](../concepts/access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness.md).

**If access is all there is.** On [global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md) views, a content is conscious just in
case it is broadcast for use by memory, reasoning and report ([Dehaene &
Naccache, 2001](../sources/dehaene-naccache-2001.md)). A reader of the workspace would then be a reader of consciousness, and the two
machines would be the same machine.

**If experience overflows access.** Block ([2007](../sources/block-2007.md)) argues that we experience more
than we can hold for report. In Sperling's experiments ([1960](../sources/sperling-1960.md)), subjects
shown a grid of twelve letters for a fraction of a second could report only about four, but could
report any row cued just after the grid disappeared. Block takes this to show that all the letters
were experienced, though only some could be remembered. If he is right, a memory reader would display
a small part of the experience and miss the rest, and a true consciousness reader would show things
the subject could never report.

**If there is no fact of the matter.** Dennett ([1991](../sources/dennett-1991.md)) argues that the line
between experiencing something and remembering it is not sharp. When a later stimulus changes how an
earlier one is reported, nothing settles whether the experience itself was changed or only the memory
of it. On this view the two machines cannot differ, because there is no separate stream of experience
for the consciousness reader to read.

The relation also runs the other way. Short-term memory can hold things that are not now experienced,
such as a phone number one is keeping in mind but not currently thinking about. So a memory reader
would show some contents that are not conscious, as well as possibly missing some that are.

### Could we tell that it works?

**The validation circle.** To check a reader, one compares its output with what subjects say they
experience. But then the machine is validated only as a reader of reports, and if it agreed with
reports in every case it would add nothing to them. The same difficulty arises for no-report
paradigms, which infer experience from eye movements or pupil size: such measures must themselves be
checked against reports before they can be trusted (Overgaard & Fazekas, [2016](../sources/overgaard-fazekas-2016.md)).

**Disagreement with the subject.** The interesting case is a machine that disagrees with what the
subject says. Suppose it shows all twelve letters while the subject insists they saw only some.
Should we believe the machine or the person? Introspection is fallible
([introspection-is-fallible-representation](../claims/introspection-is-fallible-representation.md)), so a well-confirmed theory might give the machine
the better claim, much as a thermometer can correct a person's sense of how warm a room is. Others
hold that a person's sincere judgment about their own present experience has an authority that no
external device could override ([first-person-access](../concepts/first-person-access.md)).

**Meehl's autocerebroscopist.** Meehl ([1966](../sources/meehl-1966.md)) worked this case out in detail. His
subject is a neurophysiologist connected to an autocerebroscope that displays the letter R whenever his
visual cortex is in the state that, in normal people, red light produces and that normally leads to
saying "red", and G for the corresponding green state. The subject attends to the displayed letter and
to its colour at the same moment, a design Meehl chose to avoid the worry that introspection is
"really" short-term retrospection, a report from memory rather than from present experience. One day
the device shows R while the subject is certain that he sees green, and this recurs on about one trial
in ten. Every check finds the apparatus sound, the light red and the visual cortex in the red state.
Meehl's subject eventually traces the fault to an abnormal wiring of the connection between his visual
system and the part of his brain that labels experiences, and concludes that his raw feel was red and
that his labelling of it was wrong. In his words, "sometimes my raw feels seem to be green when they are
in fact red." Meehl's moral is that statements about one's own experience are normally noninferential
and indubitable, but corrigible and occasionally false: no necessary connection holds between
someone's saying "green" and their experiencing green, and this is so for the dualist as much as for
the identity theorist. He adds that the same experiment could have come out against the identity
thesis, which is why he counts the thesis as empirical.

An obvious reply is that the experiment assumes what is at issue. Meehl's subject can be
corrected only because the theory linking the red brain state to red experience is taken as settled,
and that link was itself established by trusting reports. A defender of first-person authority can say
that a case of this kind would show instead that the theory had failed for this subject.

**Indirect validation.** A reader could gain trust without matching reports case by case, as
scientific instruments usually do: by agreeing with a well-tested theory of consciousness, by being
consistent across subjects and conditions, and by predicting new reports and behaviour. The detection
of awareness in a vegetative patient ([Owen et al., 2006](../sources/owen-2006.md)) is accepted on such
grounds, since the patient's brain responded to instructions exactly as healthy brains do. But the
trust is only as good as the theory, and theories of consciousness are contested.

**Beyond humans.** The hardest case is a subject who cannot report at all, or whose brain is unlike
ours: an infant, an octopus, an AI system. A reader calibrated on human brains has no obvious
application to a system built differently, and the question whether it would read anything there is
the question of [substrate-independence](substrate-independence.md).

### In plain terms

Imagine a helmet that shows on a screen whatever you are experiencing right now. Machines that do a
rough version of this exist: trained on someone's brain scans, they can guess what story the person
is listening to. Could a perfect one be built? If experience depends on the brain, perhaps. But would
it show your *experience*, or only what you are able to remember and describe? Some scientists think
these are the same, while others think you experience more than you can report, and some think the
question has no answer. And how would we check the helmet? We could compare it with what people say,
but then it is only as good as what people say. If the helmet and the person disagreed, it is not
obvious which one we should believe.
