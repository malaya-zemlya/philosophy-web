*Is machine superintelligence achievable?* That is: could an engineered system come to exceed
the best human performance by a wide margin across virtually every domain of cognitive
endeavor — science, strategy, social manipulation, engineering, and the rest — rather than
merely matching or beating humans at one narrow task, as calculators and chess engines already
do? The question is the threshold issue for [superintelligence](../concepts/superintelligence.md): everything asked
about the consequences of building such a system presupposes an answer to whether one could
exist at all.

### Three sub-issues bundled under one question

The question as usually posed actually bundles at least three separable disputes.

The first is **physical possibility**: is there some principled barrier — a law of physics, a
limit on computation, an irreducible role for biological substrate — that caps how far
engineered cognition can go, short of matching, let alone exceeding, the best humans across the
board? Skeptics of a hard ceiling point to the steady historical trend of machines matching and
then surpassing narrow human abilities; skeptics of unbounded growth point to diminishing
returns, energy and data constraints, and the possibility that some cognitive feats depend on
embodiment or biological detail that silicon cannot cheaply reproduce.

The second is **coherence**: does "exceeding human intelligence across all domains" even
presuppose a single underlying scale on which minds can be ranked, high to low? [intelligence](../concepts/intelligence.md)
catalogues competing senses of "intelligence" — a narrow psychometric sense, a broad
means-end-rationality sense, an economically-flavored optimization-power sense — and on some of
those senses a single unified ranking may not exist: a system could be vastly superhuman on one
axis (raw calculation, or persuasion) while being ordinary or worse on another (long-horizon
planning, embodied common sense). If intelligence is not one scale but a bundle of loosely
related capacities, "superintelligence" may need reformulating before the feasibility question
can even be given a determinate answer.

The third is **trajectory**, sometimes called *takeoff*: supposing superintelligence is both
physically possible and a coherent target, does it arrive gradually, through incremental
improvements spread over decades, or could it arrive through a runaway *intelligence
explosion* — a self-amplifying feedback loop in which a system that is good enough to improve
its own design produces a successor that is better still, compressing what might otherwise take
generations into a short and possibly uncontrollable interval? See [intelligence-explosion](../concepts/intelligence-explosion.md).
The pace of takeoff matters enormously for what, if anything, can be done to prepare: a slow
takeoff leaves time for course correction and institutional response, while a fast one may not.
This sub-issue is large enough, and sufficiently independent of the first two, that it may
eventually warrant a question node of its own rather than living as a clause inside this one.

### Why the bundling matters

Treating feasibility as a single yes-or-no question invites talking past one another: a
critic who denies a hard physical ceiling may still deny that "intelligence" is coherent enough
to admit of the required ordering, while a critic who grants coherence may still deny that any
realistic trajectory is fast enough to matter practically. Answers to this question should say
which of the three sub-issues they are actually addressing.
