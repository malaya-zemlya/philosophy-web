The **onset of consciousness** is the question of when, in the development of an individual human or
animal, there first comes to be something it is like to be that creature. A fertilized egg presumably
experiences nothing, and an awake two-year-old plainly does. Somewhere between them experience begins:
in the womb, at birth, in early infancy, or later still. The question concerns *ontogeny*, the
development of the individual, and is distinct from the [origin-of-consciousness](origin-of-consciousness.md), which
asks when consciousness first appeared in the history of life. It matters well beyond theory, since
answers have shaped whether newborns are given anaesthesia for surgery, how fetal pain is weighed in
medicine and law, and which animals and embryos are protected in research.

### Why the question is hard

Consciousness is usually detected by report: a person tells us what they see or feel. Fetuses and
infants cannot report, and neither can animals, so every answer rests on indirect markers, such as
behaviour, physiological responses to stimuli and the maturity of the brain structures thought to support
experience in adults. Which markers count depends on which theory of consciousness one holds. A theory on
which experience needs the cerebral cortex and its connections to the thalamus implies a fairly late
onset; a theory on which the brainstem suffices implies an early one; a theory on which experience needs
higher-order thought about one's own states implies a very late one. The question therefore inherits all
the disagreement about the [neural-correlates-of-consciousness](../concepts/neural-correlates-of-consciousness.md), at the point where the
correlations are least securely established.

Several distinctions also need to be kept apart. *Phenomenal consciousness*, the bare having of
experience, is different from *self-consciousness*, the awareness of oneself as a subject, and from
*reflective* or reportable consciousness. A newborn might have the first without the others. Being
conscious at a time, such as being awake and aware now, is also different from having the *capacity* for
consciousness, which a sleeping adult retains.

### Candidate answers

Answers range across the whole span of development.

*Early in gestation.* Some researchers hold that structures below the cortex may suffice for experience.
Björn Merker argues that the upper brainstem is the core of conscious experience, pointing to children
born with little or no cerebral cortex (hydranencephaly) who nonetheless show emotion, attention and
preferences [merker-2007](../sources/merker-2007.md). If he is right, experience, and pain in particular, might be
possible well before the cortex is functional. Stuart Derbyshire, who had earlier argued that fetal pain
requires cortical processing and probably later development still [derbyshire-2006](../sources/derbyshire-2006.md), now argues
with John Bockmann that the cortex may not be necessary for pain. Projections from the thalamus into the
*subplate*, a transient layer beneath the developing cortex present from about 12 weeks of gestation,
might support a rudimentary, immediate experience of pain, so fetal pain cannot be ruled out before 24
weeks [derbyshire-bockmann-2020](../sources/derbyshire-bockmann-2020.md).

*Mid to late gestation.* The most common view in medicine ties the possibility of experience to the
arrival of nerve fibres from the thalamus in the developing cortex, at around 23 to 24 weeks of
gestation. Before this, it is argued, the cortex has no access to sensory information, and so there can
be no experience of it. A working party of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists concluded
that the fetus cannot be aware of pain before 24 weeks [rcog-2010](../sources/rcog-2010.md), and a multidisciplinary review
in *JAMA* placed the capacity for conscious pain perception unlikely before the third trimester
[lee-2005](../sources/lee-2005.md). Brain recordings from premature infants show that around 35 to 37
weeks of gestation the brain begins to respond differently to touch and to painful stimuli
[fabrizi-2011](../sources/fabrizi-2011.md).

*At birth.* David Mellor and colleagues argue that capacity is not enough, and that the fetus is never
actually conscious in the womb [mellor-2005](../sources/mellor-2005.md). The intrauterine environment keeps it in a
sleep-like state by means of inhibitory substances, such as adenosine and certain neurosteroids produced
with the placenta, together with warmth and cushioning. Birth removes these inhibitors and floods the
newborn with stimulating hormones, so that awareness begins when the infant first breathes and wakes.
The same working party accepted that the fetus is probably kept in a continuous sleep-like state in
utero [rcog-2010](../sources/rcog-2010.md). Hugo Lagercrantz and Jean-Pierre Changeux combine the two views: the
thalamocortical connections needed for conscious perception form from about 24 weeks, but because the
fetus is sedated, a minimal consciousness emerges only at birth, when a surge of stress hormones and
catecholamines arouses the newborn, with richer and reflective forms following in infancy
[lagercrantz-changeux-2009](../sources/lagercrantz-changeux-2009.md).

*In infancy.* Some evidence suggests that the adult signature of conscious perception appears in an
immature form in the first months. Sid Kouider and colleagues found that infants of 5, 12 and 15 months
show the same two-stage brain response to briefly flashed faces that marks conscious perception in adults,
only slower and weaker in the youngest group [kouider-2013](../sources/kouider-2013.md). Studies of the infant brain also find
adult-like networks, such as a left-lateralized language network at three months
[dehaene-lambertz-2002](../sources/dehaene-lambertz-2002.md). In a recent review of behavioural, neural and theoretical markers, Tim
Bayne and colleagues conclude that there is reasonable evidence that consciousness is present by
birth, and that it may emerge late in gestation, while stressing the uncertainty [bayne-2023](../sources/bayne-2023.md); Claudia Passos-Ferreira argues likewise that
there is good reason to regard newborns as conscious [passos-ferreira-2023](../sources/passos-ferreira-2023.md).

*Later still.* On some higher-order theories, a state is phenomenally conscious only if the subject can
represent it as its own experience, which requires concepts of mental states. Peter Carruthers drew the
consequence that young children, before they acquire a theory of mind at around three or four years, may
lack phenomenal consciousness altogether, as most animals would [carruthers-2000](../sources/carruthers-2000.md). Most
philosophers regard this as a reason to reject the theory rather than to accept the conclusion.
Self-consciousness, by contrast, is generally agreed to come late. Children begin to recognize themselves
in a mirror at around 18 to 24 months [amsterdam-1972](../sources/amsterdam-1972.md), and the capacity for reflective
self-awareness develops over the following years.

### What the theories predict

The major theories of consciousness give different verdicts. [global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md) ties
consciousness to the broadcasting of information across long-range networks linking frontal and parietal
cortex, which are immature at birth but present in rudimentary form, so it suggests a gradual emergence
across late gestation and infancy. [integrated-information-theory](../positions/integrated-information-theory.md) makes consciousness a
matter of degree, measured by integrated information, and so implies that a fetal brain has some small
amount of it as soon as its parts are integrated, rising as it develops. [higher-order-theories](../positions/higher-order-theories.md)
in their stronger forms imply the late onset described above. Brainstem-centred views imply the early
onset. The question is therefore one place where theories of consciousness could in principle be tested,
if independent markers could be agreed.

Alison Gopnik offers a different perspective on infant experience [gopnik-2009](../sources/gopnik-2009.md). Adult
consciousness, she suggests, is like a *spotlight*, narrowly focused by attention on what matters to
current goals. Infant consciousness is more like a *lantern*, diffusely illuminating everything at once,
because the infant brain is less inhibited and learning about everything in its surroundings. On this
view infants may be, in a sense, *more* conscious than adults, not less.

### Gradual or sudden?

Whatever the timing, it is unclear whether consciousness begins at a moment or comes in by degrees. The
brain develops gradually, and there seems to be no single step in its construction at which a switch could
plausibly be thrown, which suggests a gradual onset (compare the
[continuity-argument-schema](../concepts/continuity-argument-schema.md), and [no-nonarbitrary-threshold-for-experience](../claims/no-nonarbitrary-threshold-for-experience.md)). Against
this, many hold that whether there is something it is like to be a creature admits of no halfway cases
([phenomenal-determinacy](../claims/phenomenal-determinacy.md)), so that there must be a first moment of experience even if we cannot
locate it. One way to reconcile the two is to distinguish the *existence* of experience, which may begin
abruptly, from its *richness*, which certainly grows by degrees.

### Animals

The same question arises for every animal species, and the answer seems to differ between species born at
different stages of maturity. *Precocial* animals, such as lambs, foals and chicks, are born or hatch with
a well-developed brain and are active within minutes; *altricial* animals, such as rats, kittens and
humans to some degree, are born immature. Mellor and Kirsten Diesch argue that in precocial species
awareness begins at birth or hatching, with the same neuroinhibition operating before it, while in
altricial species it may begin only some days or weeks after birth, as the brain matures
[mellor-diesch-2007](../sources/mellor-diesch-2007.md). European law reflects the uncertainty by extending protection to the fetal
forms of mammals from the last third of their normal development, on the ground that they may be capable
of suffering by then [eu-directive-2010-63](../sources/eu-directive-2010-63.md). Jonathan Birch argues that where the evidence is this
uncertain, the right response is precaution proportionate to the realistic possibility of sentience,
rather than waiting for the question to be settled, and applies this to human fetuses and embryos as well
as animals [birch-2024](../sources/birch-2024.md), [sep-consciousness-animal](../sources/sep-consciousness-animal.md).

### The practical stakes

The history of neonatal medicine shows what is at stake. Until the 1980s, many surgeons operated on
newborn infants with little or no anaesthesia, on the assumption that their nervous systems were too
immature for pain to be experienced. Kanwaljeet Anand and Paul Hickey's review of the evidence that
newborns mount strong physiological responses to surgical stress, and do better when anaesthetized, helped
change practice [anand-hickey-1987](../sources/anand-hickey-1987.md). The fetal-pain debate has similar consequences for fetal
surgery, where analgesia may be given to the fetus, and it figures in debates about abortion law, where
estimates of the onset of pain have been used to support time limits. The scientific question is
independent of the moral and legal ones, but they make it unusually consequential.

### In plain terms

When does a baby first start to experience anything? Before birth, at birth, or some time after? Nobody
can simply ask, because fetuses and newborns cannot tell us, so scientists look for signs in behaviour and
in the developing brain, and the signs they trust depend on their theory of what consciousness needs.
Some think the deep, ancient parts of the brain are enough, which would mean experience starts early in
pregnancy. The commonest medical view links it to wiring that reaches the brain's outer layer at about 24
weeks. Others argue the fetus is kept asleep by chemicals in the womb, so that it "wakes up" at birth. A
few theories even suggest young toddlers lack real experience, which most people take as a sign the theory
is wrong. The question matters: doctors once operated on newborns without anaesthesia because they assumed
babies could not feel pain. The same puzzle arises for animals, where newborn lambs seem far more awake
than newborn kittens.
