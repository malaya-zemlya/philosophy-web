The **origin of consciousness** is the question of whether [consciousness](../concepts/consciousness.md) has always
existed, and, if it has not, when and how it first came into the world. On the picture given by
modern cosmology, the early universe was a hot, nearly uniform plasma with no atoms, let alone cells
or brains. Today it contains at least some beings for whom there is something it is like to be them.
If experience requires anything like a nervous system, then for most of cosmic history there was no
experience at all, and at some point there began to be. The question asks whether that story is
right, and if it is, what kind of event the first appearance of experience was.

The question is a historical face of the [mind-body-problem](../concepts/mind-body-problem.md). Asking how
consciousness relates to matter now leads naturally to asking how it arose from matter then, and the
answers given to the one constrain the answers available to the other.

### Three sub-questions

The issue divides into three parts, which are often run together.

- **Whether.** Was there ever a time without consciousness? Most physicalists and emergentists say
  yes. Panpsychists, on whom experience belongs to the basic constituents of matter, say no, and so do
  views on which a mind (for instance God) exists independently of the physical universe.
- **When.** If consciousness began, at what point? This is partly empirical, a matter of finding the
  earliest organisms that were conscious, but the empirical work cannot start without a theory of
  which signs indicate consciousness, and that theory is disputed.
- **How.** What sort of transition was it? Did experience switch on abruptly, or shade in by degrees
  through borderline cases? And how could any arrangement of non-experiencing matter give rise to
  experience at all? The last part is the [hard-problem](../concepts/hard-problem.md) in historical form.

### Answers to "when"

**Early evolutionary emergence.** Many biologists and philosophers locate the first experience in
the Cambrian period, more than 500 million years ago, when animals with complex bodies, eyes and
centralized nervous systems appeared. Feinberg and Mallatt ([2016](../sources/feinberg-mallatt-2016.md))
argue from neuroanatomy that sensory consciousness arose then in vertebrates, arthropods and
cephalopods. Ginsburg and Jablonka ([2019](../sources/ginsburg-jablonka-2019.md)) reach a similar date by a
different route, treating *unlimited associative learning*, the open-ended ability to learn about
new compound stimuli and chains of events, as the marker of minimal experience. Godfrey-Smith
([2016](../sources/godfrey-smith-2016.md)) uses the octopus, whose mind evolved separately from ours, to argue
that experience may have arisen more than once. These proposals leave open whether consciousness
evolved once, in a common ancestor of the three lineages, or several times independently
([Trestman, Birch & Allen, SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness-animal.md)).

**Narrower and later datings.** Views that tie consciousness to more demanding capacities date it
later and restrict it more. On some [higher-order-theories](../positions/higher-order-theories.md), a state is
conscious only if the subject represents itself as being in it, which may confine experience to
mammals, or even to humans. The most extreme dating is that of the psychologist Julian Jaynes
([1976](../sources/jaynes-1976.md)), who held that consciousness arose only about three thousand years ago,
when an earlier mentality, in which people heard their own deliberations as the voices of gods,
broke down. Jaynes's thesis concerns introspective self-awareness, however, not raw feeling, and it
shows how much the answer to "when" depends on which sense of the word is meant. A creature can feel
pain without being able to reflect on the fact that it does.

**Never: experience was always present.** [Panpsychism](../concepts/panpsychism.md) denies that there was a
time without consciousness. On this view, rudimentary experience belongs to the fundamental
constituents of matter, so it was present in the early plasma, and what appeared later was only the
complex, unified kind of experience that brains have. William James ([1890](../sources/james-1890.md), ch. 6)
put the reasoning in evolutionary terms: if evolution is continuous, consciousness in some form must
have been present at the very origin of things, since nothing in a continuous process marks a point
where it could have entered. [Cosmopsychism](../positions/cosmopsychism.md) holds instead that the universe as a
whole has always been a subject, and that individual minds are aspects of it. Theistic views and
[idealism](../positions/idealism.md) also deny that consciousness began, though for a different reason: on
them a mind is prior to the physical world rather than part of it.

### Answers to "how"

**Sudden or gradual.** If consciousness began, it either began abruptly, with a first organism that
had experience whose parents had none, or gradually, through creatures of which it is neither
clearly true nor clearly false that they experience anything. A sudden "lights on" moment is hard to
square with the gradualness of evolution, which favours the second option. But many people find
borderline consciousness hard to understand: it is not obvious what it would be for there to be
only indeterminately something it is like to be a creature. Schwitzgebel
([2023](../sources/schwitzgebel-2023.md)) argues that such cases are possible, and gradualists like
Godfrey-Smith accept them as the price of avoiding a sharp threshold.

**Emergence or no emergence.** A deeper question is whether experience can arise from wholly
non-experiencing matter at all. Emergentists hold that it can: past a certain level of organization,
matter gives rise to experience as a genuinely new feature
([strong-emergence-is-acceptable](../claims/strong-emergence-is-acceptable.md)). Their opponents deny that this
is intelligible ([no-radical-emergence](../claims/no-radical-emergence.md)), since nothing about mass, charge or
spatial arrangement seems to explain why experience should begin. If they are right, consciousness
cannot have appeared from nothing, and the history must have been one of experience growing more
complex rather than of experience beginning. This is the core of the
[genetic-panpsychism](../arguments/genetic-panpsychism.md) argument, Nagel's ([1979](../sources/nagel-1979.md))
inference from the absence of brute emergence to panpsychism, and Strawson
([2006](../sources/strawson-2006.md)) presses the same point. The companion
[continuity argument](../arguments/no-arbitrary-line-panpsychism.md) adds that even if emergence were possible, no
non-arbitrary place to locate it is in view ([no-nonarbitrary-threshold-for-experience](../claims/no-nonarbitrary-threshold-for-experience.md)).
Panpsychism avoids the problem of emergence but faces its own version of the "how" question, the
*combination problem*: how simple experiences in particles add up to the unified experience of an
animal.

**Why it evolved.** If consciousness arose by natural selection, it presumably did something useful,
since selection favours traits only through their effects. James ([1890](../sources/james-1890.md), ch. 5)
used this point against the view of Huxley ([1874](../sources/huxley-1874.md)) that consciousness is a
causally idle by-product of the brain ([epiphenomenalism](../positions/epiphenomenalism.md)): a feeling with no
effects could not have been selected, and its close fit with what is good or bad for an organism
(pain with harm, pleasure with benefit) would then be a coincidence. Epiphenomenalists reply that
consciousness could have come along with the brain processes that were selected without itself
being selected for. Either way, the history of consciousness bears on the question of
[mental-causation](mental-causation.md).

### Illusionist reframing

[Illusionism](../positions/illusionism.md) changes the question. If phenomenal consciousness in the
philosopher's sense does not exist, then it never appeared, and what needs a history is the
evolution of creatures that represent themselves as having it. That is a question about the
evolution of certain cognitive capacities, and it raises no special difficulty about how experience
arose from matter. Critics reply that this answers the historical question only by denying what it
was asking about.

### In plain terms

Shortly after the Big Bang, the universe was a hot soup of particles: no planets, no life, no
brains. Today it contains creatures that feel pain, see colours and think. So did feeling switch on
at some point? Most scientists who study the question think it began over 500 million years ago,
when the first animals with eyes and real brains appeared. Some think it came much later, or only
with humans. Others say it never "began" at all: some faint trace of experience has always been part
of matter (the view called panpsychism), or a mind such as God existed before the universe. The
hardest part is the "how". Even if we knew the date, we would still not understand how lifeless
matter could ever come to feel anything, which is why some thinkers prefer to say that it never had
to.
