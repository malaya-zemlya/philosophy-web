Mental states seem to cause physical events all the time: a decision moves a hand, a pang of thirst
sends one to the tap, a belief prompts a sentence. *Mental causation* is the question of how that is
possible, how something mental can make a difference in the physical world. It is one of the strands
of the [mind-body-problem](../concepts/mind-body-problem.md), and it matters far beyond metaphysics. If mental states made no
difference to what we do, we would not act *because of* our reasons, our beliefs would never explain
our behaviour, and the feeling that we are agents would be an illusion. Most philosophers therefore
treat the reality of mental causation as a fixed point that any theory of mind must preserve
([mental-events-have-physical-effects](../claims/mental-events-have-physical-effects.md)).

The problem takes different forms depending on what one thinks the mind is. For the substance dualist
it is the problem of *interaction*. For the physicalist who identifies mental events with physical
ones but denies that mental properties reduce, it is the problem of whether mental properties do any
of the causing. For anyone who holds that the content of a thought depends on things outside the
head, it is the problem of how content can matter to what the head does. The survey by Robb, Heil and
Gibb ([SEP](../sources/sep-mental-causation.md)) organizes the subject in this way, and this entry follows
it.

### The problem of interaction

The modern problem begins with René Descartes, for whom mind and body are different substances: the
body is extended and moves by contact, while the mind is unextended and thinks. Princess Elisabeth of
Bohemia ([1643](../sources/elisabeth-1643.md)) asked him how a thinking thing with no extension could set a
body in motion, when every motion she understood required contact and a surface to push. Descartes's
answer, that the soul acts on the body through the pineal gland, located the interaction without
explaining it.

Two later versions sharpen the problem.

- **Conservation.** If an immaterial mind moved matter, energy would appear in the brain from
  nowhere, violating the conservation laws of physics. Dualists reply that conservation laws hold for
  closed systems and that the claim that the brain is closed in this sense is what is in question.
- **Pairing.** Jaegwon Kim ([2005](../sources/kim-2005.md)) asks what makes a particular soul interact with a
  particular body. Physical causes are paired with their effects partly by their positions in space.
  Two exactly similar souls with no location would have nothing to tie each to its own body rather
  than to the other's.

The deeper form of the objection is the [causal-closure](../concepts/causal-closure.md) of the physical: the principle,
supported by the history of physics and physiology, that every physical event that has a cause has a
sufficient physical cause ([physical-domain-complete](../claims/physical-domain-complete.md); Papineau, [2002](../sources/papineau-2002.md)).
If closure holds, there are no gaps in the physical causal chain for an immaterial mind to fill.

### Anomalous monism and the problem of causal relevance

One way to keep mental causes is to make them physical. Donald Davidson's
[anomalous-monism](../positions/anomalous-monism.md) ([1970](../sources/davidson-1970.md)) holds that every mental event is
identical to some physical event, so it can cause physical effects in the ordinary way, but that
there are no strict laws linking mental and physical *types*, so the mental cannot be reduced to the
physical.

Critics replied that this saves mental *events* as causes but not mental *properties*. An event
causes its effects in virtue of some of its properties and not others. Ernest Sosa
([1984](../sources/sosa-1984.md)) gave the example of a gunshot that kills: the shot is also loud, but it is
not the loudness that kills. On Davidson's view, the critics argued, a mental event causes behaviour
in virtue of its physical properties, and its being a belief or a desire is like the loudness of the
shot, real but causally irrelevant. Terence Horgan ([1989](../sources/horgan-1989.md)) called this the
problem of *quausation*: whether a mental event causes its effects *qua* mental. The charge is that
anomalous monism amounts to *property* epiphenomenalism. Davidson ([1993](../sources/davidson-1993.md))
and his defenders reply that the charge relies on a picture of causation as a relation between
properties that his view rejects.

### The exclusion problem

Kim's [causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md) generalizes the problem to every non-reductive physicalism, the
view that mental properties are realized by physical properties but are not identical to them. Its
premises are the closure principle and the claim that the effects of mental causes are not
systematically overdetermined ([no-systematic-overdetermination](../claims/no-systematic-overdetermination.md)): an arm does not rise
because of two independent complete causes, as a man shot by two firing squad bullets at once might
die. Suppose a mental property M causes behaviour B. B has a sufficient physical cause P, the
realizer of M. Since B is not overdetermined, P leaves nothing for M to do, and M is excluded
([irreducible-mental-causally-excluded](../claims/irreducible-mental-causally-excluded.md)). The only way for M to be causally efficacious is to
be identical to P, so the argument concludes that mental causation requires reduction.

The responses fall into four groups.

**Accepting reduction.** Kim himself concluded that functional, intentional mental properties can be
reduced and so keep their causal powers, while the qualitative character of experience may not be
reducible, and so may be epiphenomenal ([Kim, 2005](../sources/kim-2005.md)). Identity theorists
([type-identity](../positions/type-identity.md)) accept reduction for experience too.

**Rejecting exclusion.** Several authors deny that mental and physical causes compete. Stephen Yablo
([1992](../sources/yablo-1992.md)) compares the relation of a mental property to its realizer with that of a
determinable to a determinate, *red* to *scarlet*. A pigeon trained to peck at red things pecks
because the target is red, not because it is scarlet, and a cause should be *proportional* to its
effect. On this view the mental property is often the better candidate for being the cause. Karen
Bennett ([2003](../sources/bennett-2003.md)) argues that the overdetermination is harmless, because the
counterfactual test for genuine overdetermination cannot be met when one cause necessitates the other.
Interventionist accounts of causation (Woodward, [2003](../sources/woodward-2003.md); List & Menzies,
[2009](../sources/list-menzies-2009.md)) treat a cause as a difference-maker and argue that, by that
standard, the mental property often makes the difference and its specific realizer does not
([mental-causes-are-difference-makers](../claims/mental-causes-are-difference-makers.md)).

**Turning the argument back.** Ned Block ([2003](../sources/block-2003.md)) argues that if the exclusion
argument works against the mental, it works equally against chemistry, biology and every other
science above fundamental physics, so that causal powers would drain away to the bottom level
([exclusion-generalizes](../arguments/exclusion-generalizes.md)). Since that conclusion is unacceptable, something in the argument
must be wrong.

**Denying closure.** Interactionist dualists, and some emergentists who accept *downward causation*,
reject the closure premise and hold that mental causes fill real gaps in the physical chain. The cost
is a bet against the way physics and physiology have developed, and this is where the problem meets
the question of what counts as [physical](../concepts/physical.md), since a closure principle is only as definite as
its key term.

### Epiphenomenalism

[epiphenomenalism](../positions/epiphenomenalism.md) accepts the conclusion that its rivals try to avoid. On this view mental
states, or at least experiences, are caused by brain processes but cause nothing in turn
([mental-states-causally-inert](../claims/mental-states-causally-inert.md)). Thomas Huxley ([1874](../sources/huxley-1874.md)) compared
consciousness to the whistle of a steam engine, which accompanies the engine's work without
contributing to it. Frank Jackson ([1982](../sources/jackson-1982.md)) defended a version restricted to
qualia, the felt qualities of experience, as the price of accepting his knowledge argument.

Epiphenomenalism is usually regarded as a position of last resort. It faces three main objections.
- *Evolution.* A trait with no effects cannot be selected, so the close fit between experience and an
  organism's needs, with pain going with harm, would be a coincidence. William James
  ([1890](../sources/james-1890.md)) pressed this point against Huxley. The reply is that experience
  could have come along with selected brain processes without itself being selected for.
- *Knowledge.* If experience causes nothing, it cannot cause our beliefs and statements about it, and
  it is unclear how those beliefs could be knowledge. This is the [paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment](../concepts/paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment.md).
- *Agency.* If our thoughts and feelings do nothing, our conviction that we act because of them is
  false in every case.

The exclusion problem threatens to push every non-reductive view toward epiphenomenalism, which is
why the problem of mental causation is often treated as the strongest argument for physicalism
([causal-argument-for-physicalism](../arguments/causal-argument-for-physicalism.md)).

### Content and externalism

A further version of the problem concerns the *content* of thoughts. On *externalist* views, what a
thought is about depends partly on the thinker's environment and history, so two people with
identical brains could have thoughts with different contents. But a brain's causal powers seem to
depend only on its internal state. Content would then seem irrelevant to what the brain does, and the
fact that a belief is about water would make no difference to whether one reaches for the glass. Fred
Dretske ([1988](../sources/dretske-1988.md)) replied that content is a *structuring* cause: it explains not
why a particular movement happens now, but why the brain came, through learning, to be wired so that
states carrying that content produce movements of that kind.

### In plain terms

You decide to raise your arm, and it goes up: your *thought* seems to move your *body*. But physics
seems complete on its own terms. The arm went up because nerves fired, because of earlier physical
events, in a chain that never needs to mention a decision. So if your decision is not just another
physical event, what did it actually do? There are a few options. The decision might really *be* the
brain activity. The brain activity might do everything, with the decision along for the ride, which
is the view called epiphenomenalism. Or the decision and the brain activity might be two ways of
describing one cause, as being red and being scarlet are two ways of describing one colour. Each
option has a price, and saying how a mind can really move a body, without either reducing it to
brain chemistry or leaving it idle, is the problem of mental causation.
