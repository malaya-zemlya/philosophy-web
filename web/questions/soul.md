Is there, in a human being, a *soul*: a self that thinks, feels and is aware, and that is something more
than the living body and what it does? The question is among the oldest in philosophy and is central to
nearly every religious tradition, because the answer settles what, if anything, could survive death. It
is distinct from the [hard problem of consciousness](hard-problem.md), which asks how conscious experience fits into the
physical world. One can hold that consciousness is non-physical and still deny that there is a soul, a
persisting *self* that has the experiences; and one can believe in a soul while holding that its life
depends on the body. It is distinct, too, from the [question of personal
identity](personal-identity.md), which asks what makes a person the same over time, though the two are intertwined, since the
soul has traditionally been offered as the answer to both.

The main answers fall into six families, surveyed in the entry [soul](../concepts/soul.md). The soul may be an
immaterial substance, as for Plato, Descartes and Avicenna ([soul-is-immaterial-substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md)). It
may be pure, inactive consciousness set over against a material mind, as in Sāṃkhya. It may be the form of
a living body, as for Aristotle and Aquinas ([soul-is-form-of-body](../claims/soul-is-form-of-body.md)). There may be no enduring
self at all, as in Buddhism ([no-enduring-self](../claims/no-enduring-self.md)). The self may be one universal consciousness, as
in Advaita Vedānta ([self-is-brahman](../claims/self-is-brahman.md)). Or there may be no soul, the person being wholly physical,
as for secular physicalists and Christian physicalists alike. The positions that answer the question are
listed in the index.
