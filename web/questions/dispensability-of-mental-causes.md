The **dispensability of mental causes** is the question of whether, in a world where physics gives a
complete causal account of every physical event, we still *need* to cite mental states to explain
what people do. If the movement of your arm can be traced, cell by cell, to earlier events in your
brain, and those to earlier physical events, then "she raised her arm because she wanted water"
seems to add nothing that the physical story lacks. Either it is a loose way of saying what the
physical story says precisely, or it is idle.

The question is related to, but distinct from, the exclusion problem. Jaegwon Kim's
[causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md) asks whether irreducible mental properties can be *causes* at all, given
that their physical realizers already suffice. The dispensability question asks about *explanation*:
whether mental-level explanations do work that physical explanations cannot, whatever we conclude
about causal efficacy. The two can come apart. A mental explanation might be indispensable even if
the mental property it cites is not a cause in the strictest sense, and a mental property might be a
genuine cause and still be dispensable, if a physical description of the same cause would explain
everything as well. The general problem of which both are parts is [mental-causation](mental-causation.md).

### The case for dispensability

The argument for dispensing with mental causes is an argument from parsimony. Kim's *principle of
explanatory exclusion* ([1989](../sources/kim-1989.md)) holds that a single event cannot have two complete
and independent explanations. If a neurophysiological explanation of an action is complete, a reasons
explanation of the same action must either be reducible to it or be in competition with it. On a
reductive view, the mental explanation is kept, but only because it *is* a physical explanation under
another description. On an eliminative view ([eliminative-materialism](../positions/eliminative-materialism.md)), it is not kept at
all: Paul Churchland ([1981](../sources/churchland-1981.md)) argues that belief-desire psychology is a folk
theory with a poor record that a mature neuroscience will replace, as chemistry replaced talk of
phlogiston.

### The case for indispensability

Most responses argue that mental explanations capture something that physical explanations of the
same events miss. The arguments take several forms.

**Relevance.** Hilary Putnam ([1975](../sources/putnam-1975.md)) asked why a square peg one inch across will
not pass through a round hole one inch in diameter. The answer is geometric: the diagonal of the peg
is longer than the hole. One could in principle deduce the same result from the positions and forces
of all the particles in the peg and the board, but the deduction would not *explain* it, because it
would not show which features matter. Any peg of that shape would fail, whatever it is made of. The
psychological case is parallel: "she wanted water" picks out what is relevant to her reaching, while
a complete neural description is buried in details that would have been different had she reached in
the same way for the same reason.

**Proportionality.** Stephen Yablo ([1992](../sources/yablo-1992.md)) argues that a good cause is
*proportional* to its effect, neither too specific nor too general. A pigeon trained to peck at red
things pecks at a scarlet target because it is red, not because it is scarlet, since it would have
pecked at a crimson one too. Mental properties often stand to their neural realizers as red stands
to scarlet, and are then the proportional causes, while the neural property is too specific.

**Program explanation.** Frank Jackson and Philip Pettit ([1990](../sources/jackson-pettit-1990.md)) grant
that the work of producing an effect is done at the lowest level, but argue that a higher-level
property can still be explanatorily relevant by *programming* for it. When boiling water cracks a
flask, it is some particular molecule that strikes the glass. Citing the boiling explains more than
citing that molecule, because it shows that the cracking did not depend on which molecule it was.

**Difference-making.** Interventionist theories of causation (Woodward, [2003](../sources/woodward-2003.md);
List & Menzies, [2009](../sources/list-menzies-2009.md)) define a cause as a variable whose manipulation
would change the effect. By this test the desire for water often makes the difference to whether one
reaches for the glass, while its exact neural realizer does not, since a slightly different realizer
of the same desire would have produced the same reach ([mental-causes-are-difference-makers](../claims/mental-causes-are-difference-makers.md)).

**Patterns and autonomy.** Daniel Dennett argues that the patterns described by the
[intentional-stance](../positions/intentional-stance.md), the practice of predicting a system by attributing beliefs and desires
to it, are real even though the system is physically determined ([1987](../sources/dennett-1987.md)).
A creature that could predict everything from physics would still miss them, since it would not see
what the many different physical routes to the same action have in common. In "Real
patterns" ([1991](../sources/dennett-1991b.md)) he compares mental states to the gliders in Conway's Game of
Life, which are fully fixed by the cell-level rules yet are the only practical way to describe what
the system does. Jerry Fodor ([1974](../sources/fodor-1974.md)) makes the general case for the *special
sciences*: their kinds are realized by physically diverse mechanisms, so their generalizations
correspond to no single physical law and would be lost if translated into physics.

**Two explanations of one machine.** Douglas Hofstadter ([2007](../sources/hofstadter-2007.md), ch. 3)
makes the point with a computer built from dominoes, which he calls a *domino chainium*. Each domino
is spring-loaded, so that after being knocked over it stands back up, and chains of them can branch,
merge and loop, which is enough to build a program. This one tests whether its input is prime. The
input, 641, is entered as a stretch of 641 dominoes, and one particular stretch, the "divisor
stretch", falls only if a divisor is found. An observer who does not know what the machine is for
watches it run and asks why a certain domino in that stretch never falls. One answer is that the
domino before it never falls. That is true, but it only moves the question back one domino. The other
answer is that 641 is prime. It mentions nothing physical, and it is the answer that actually explains
the outcome, both for this domino and for any other run of the machine on a prime input.

Hofstadter is explicit that no extra force is at work: the laws of physics settle every domino's fall
by themselves, and the primality of 641 acts only through the arrangement of the dominoes. He still
counts the primality as a cause. His comparison is with the temperature of a gas, which we cite as the
cause of its pressure although the pressure is produced by countless molecular collisions. The best
and most illuminating explanation of what the chainium does depends on 641 being prime, and for
Hofstadter that is enough for a causal role. He applies the same reasoning to the brain, where
thoughts, meanings and the self play the role of primality and neurons the role of dominoes, and he
calls the result *downward causality* (ch. 12). The example is close to Putnam's peg, with the
difference that the explaining property belongs to a computation rather than to a shape. It also takes
a side in the dispute described in the next section, since it treats being the best explanation as
sufficient for being a cause.

### Explanation and causation

The indispensability arguments establish, at most, that mental explanations are not *redundant*. Kim
and others reply that this is a fact about explanation, about what is useful or illuminating for us,
and that it leaves untouched the metaphysical question of which property does the causing. Whether
that reply works depends on one's theory of causation. If causation is the production of effects by
the transfer of energy or the operation of physical laws, the work is done at the bottom level and
the higher-level explanations are useful summaries. If causation is difference-making, the
indispensability of mental explanations is itself evidence that mental properties are causes, since
being a difference-maker is what being a cause consists in.

A further objection is the one Ned Block ([2003](../sources/block-2003.md)) presses against the exclusion
argument ([exclusion-generalizes](../arguments/exclusion-generalizes.md)). If physical completeness made mental causes dispensable,
it would make every cause above fundamental physics dispensable too, including the causes cited in
chemistry, biology and geology. Few accept that conclusion, so either the argument for dispensability
proves too much, or higher-level explanation in general is dispensable in the same way.

### In plain terms

Suppose scientists could trace every nerve signal that led your arm to reach for a glass of water. Do
we still need to say you reached *because you were thirsty*? Some philosophers say no: the brain
story is complete, so the "thirsty" story is either a rough version of it or nothing at all. Most say
yes, because the brain story contains too much. If your neurons had fired a little differently, you
would still have reached for the glass, since you would still have been thirsty. "Thirsty" picks out
what actually mattered, and the exact neural details did not. The same holds in other sciences: a
square peg will not go through a round hole of the same width, and the reason is its shape, not the
behaviour of its atoms. Or picture a computer made of dominoes, checking whether 641 is a prime
number, where one domino stays standing. You could say "the dominoes before it fell the wrong way to
hit it", but the real reason is that 641 is prime. Whether being the useful explanation also makes thirst a real *cause* is the
part that is still disputed.
