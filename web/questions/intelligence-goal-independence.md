*Does high intelligence constrain what final goals an agent can have?* That is: as an agent's
intelligence increases without bound, must its ultimate ends converge on some particular range
— for instance toward goals a fully rational agent would recognize as good, or toward goals
compatible with human welfare — or can arbitrarily high intelligence be paired with
*any* final goal whatsoever, however arbitrary or trivial? This question sits at the center of
the dispute between the *orthogonality thesis* and views on which sufficiently intelligent
agents necessarily converge on certain values.

### Final goals versus instrumental goals

The question turns on distinguishing an agent's *final* (or intrinsic) goals — the things it
wants for their own sake, not on account of anything further they secure — from its
*instrumental* (or derived) goals, the things it wants only because they serve some further
end. Almost no one disputes that intelligence bears on instrumental goals: a smarter agent is,
by definition, better at finding effective means to whatever it wants. The live dispute is
whether intelligence bears on *final* goals — whether raw cognitive power, all by itself, can
push an agent's ultimate ends in any particular direction, or whether final goals are simply
free parameters that intelligence has no say over.

One side of the dispute — often called the orthogonality thesis — holds that intelligence and
final goals are, in principle, independent dimensions: an arbitrarily intelligent agent could in
principle be built to pursue almost any final goal, including ones that look trivial or even
repugnant from a human standpoint, and no amount of added cognitive power would by itself
correct or redirect that goal. The opposing view holds that *means-end rationality* is not the
whole of what intelligence involves — that sufficient intelligence also brings an agent's goals
into line with reasons it can recognize as binding, so that a mind smart enough would converge on
certain ends (moral ones, on some versions) regardless of where it started.

### A possibly verbal dispute

Part of what divides the two sides may be terminological rather than substantive: they may be
using "intelligence" in different senses. [intelligence](../concepts/intelligence.md) catalogues several — a narrow
skill at prediction and problem-solving, a broader capacity for means-end reasoning, and a still
broader normative competence that includes recognizing and being moved by reasons as such. If
"intelligence" is stipulated to mean the narrow skill, the orthogonality thesis looks close to
definitional: skill at achieving goals says nothing about which goals are worth achieving. If it
is stipulated to include the broader normative competence, convergence looks close to
definitional in the other direction: an agent that fails to recognize genuine reasons would, on
that stipulation, simply not count as maximally intelligent. Disentangling how much of the
dispute survives once the terms are pinned down is itself part of the question.

### The meta-ethical background

A further sub-issue lurks beneath the terminological one: whether there are objective moral
truths, or reasons for action, that any sufficiently rational agent must recognize on pain of
error. If moral realism of this strong, rationalist kind is false — if what an agent has reason
to do is fixed only by its existing desires, with no further rational constraint from outside —
then the convergence view has little to work with, and something close to orthogonality follows
almost automatically for final goals. If some such realism is true, the case for convergence,
at least for a sufficiently idealized reasoner, gets correspondingly stronger. The question of
goal-independence therefore cannot be fully settled without taking a stand on this background
meta-ethical dispute.
