**Epistemic access to experience** is the question of how anyone comes to know about conscious
experience: how you know what you are experiencing, and how, if at all, anyone else can know it. It
combines two puzzles. The first is about *communication*. You can tell someone the time, the route to
the station or the proof of a theorem, and they then know what you know. But you cannot, it seems, tell
someone what the colour red looks like to you, or what coffee tastes like, so that they know it as you
do. The second is about *self-knowledge*. We know about the world mainly by perceiving it, through eyes
and ears that put us in causal contact with things. We do not perceive our own experiences in that way:
there is no organ that looks at a sensation of red, and the sensation does not itself look like
anything. Yet we seem to know our experiences better than we know anything else. How?

The two puzzles have a common root. Experience is given in a special way to exactly one subject, the one
who has it (see [first-person-access](../concepts/first-person-access.md)), and to everyone else, if at all, only through what
that subject says and does. This makes experience, in one sense, [unobservable
in principle](../concepts/unobservability-in-principle.md) to everyone but its owner, and raises the question of what kind of access the owner has.

### What can and cannot be communicated

Much about experience can be communicated. One can report that one sees red rather than green, that the
red is brighter than it was, that it is more like orange than like blue, that a pain is sharp rather than
dull and getting worse. One can describe the whole structure of colour similarities and differences, and
others can confirm that their colour judgments have the same structure. What seems to resist
communication is the *quality itself*, what it is like to see that red, which philosophers call its
*qualitative character* or its *qualia* (see [qualia](../concepts/qualia.md)).

Three classic thought experiments sharpen the point. John Locke asked us to imagine that the colour one
person sees when looking at a violet is the colour another sees when looking at a marigold, and the
reverse [locke-1689](../sources/locke-1689.md). Since both would call violets "blue" and marigolds "yellow," and sort
colours in the same way, nothing they said or did would reveal the difference. This *inverted spectrum*
suggests that the quality of experience can vary while everything communicable stays fixed, a possibility
defended in detail by Sydney Shoemaker [shoemaker-1982](../sources/shoemaker-1982.md), [sep-qualia-inverted](../sources/sep-qualia-inverted.md). Thomas
Nagel argued that however much we learn about the bat's echolocation, we cannot know what it is like to
be a bat, because that knowledge requires taking up its point of view [nagel-1974](../sources/nagel-1974.md). And Frank
Jackson's Mary, who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never seen colour, seems to
learn something new when she first sees red, something that no description had conveyed
[jackson-1982](../sources/jackson-1982.md) (see [knowledge-argument](../concepts/knowledge-argument.md)).

Why should this be so? Answers fall into three groups.

*Structure is all that communication carries.* Moritz Schlick argued that all communication, and all
science, conveys only *form*, the pattern of relations among things, never *content*, the intrinsic
quality of what stands in those relations [schlick-1979](../sources/schlick-1979.md). A description tells you how things
are related; it cannot tell you what it is like to have the relata in experience. Bertrand Russell drew a
similar conclusion about physics, which describes only the structure of matter
([intrinsic-structural-distinction](../concepts/intrinsic-structural-distinction.md), [functional-description-structural-dynamical-only](../claims/functional-description-structural-dynamical-only.md)).
On this view experience is incommunicable in principle, because what is distinctive about it is
intrinsic and language transmits only structure.

*The hearer lacks the concept.* A second answer locates the barrier in the hearer rather than in the
fact. To grasp what red looks like, one needs a *phenomenal concept* of red, and such concepts can be
acquired only by having the experience. David Chalmers calls them *direct phenomenal concepts*, concepts
partly constituted by the experience they refer to [chalmers-2003b](../sources/chalmers-2003b.md). On this view
communication fails with Mary or the congenitally blind because they lack the concept, but succeeds, to a
degree, between people who have had similar experiences: two coffee drinkers can talk meaningfully about
the taste of a particular coffee, though neither can be certain the other's taste is the same.

*Nothing private is missing.* A third group of answers denies that there is anything private to
communicate. Ludwig Wittgenstein's image of the *beetle in a box* imagines everyone owning a box with
something in it called a "beetle," which only its owner can look into [wittgenstein-1953](../sources/wittgenstein-1953.md),
[sep-private-language](../sources/sep-private-language.md). The word "beetle" can still have a public use, but whatever is actually
in the box "drops out of consideration as irrelevant." What words for experience mean is fixed by their
public use, not by private objects. Daniel Dennett argued that the apparent ineffability of experience is
only practical: experiences are so rich in fine discriminations that no feasible description captures
them, as no description could capture a particular face, but nothing in them is unspeakable in principle
[dennett-1988](../sources/dennett-1988.md). Illusionists go further and hold that the sense of an ineffable private quality
is itself an introspective illusion [frankish-2016](../sources/frankish-2016.md).

### How we know our own experience

The second puzzle is how the subject knows its own experience. Ordinary perception has a familiar
structure: a sense organ, a causal channel from the object to the organ, an appearance that may or may
not match the object, and therefore the possibility of error. Self-knowledge of experience seems to lack
all of this. There is no inner eye, and the experience does not appear *through* anything. Five main
accounts have been offered.

*Inner sense.* The oldest account models self-knowledge on perception after all. Locke spoke of
*reflection*, the mind's perception of its own operations [locke-1689](../sources/locke-1689.md). Modern versions by
David Armstrong and William Lycan posit an internal monitoring mechanism that scans first-order mental
states and produces beliefs about them, as the senses scan the world [armstrong-1968](../sources/armstrong-1968.md),
[lycan-1996](../sources/lycan-1996.md). On this view self-knowledge is causal, reliable and fallible, like perception.
Shoemaker's influential critique is that introspection lacks the marks of perception: there is no organ,
no distinctive sensory character to introspecting, and no way of "looking more closely" that is
independent of the state looked at. He argued that a rational creature who was *self-blind*, having
experiences and beliefs but no inner-sense access to them, is not coherently conceivable, which suggests
that self-knowledge is partly constitutive of having the states rather than a separate faculty that
detects them [shoemaker-1994](../sources/shoemaker-1994.md).

*Acquaintance.* A second account holds that the subject is directly *acquainted* with its own
experience, in Russell's sense of being presented with it rather than knowing truths about it
[russell-1911](../sources/russell-1911.md). Acquaintance is not a channel between the knower and the known; the experience
itself is present to the subject, and a belief formed by attending to it can be partly constituted by the
experience, as with Chalmers's direct phenomenal concepts [chalmers-2003b](../sources/chalmers-2003b.md). This explains why
such knowledge seems both direct and especially secure, and why Descartes could treat it as certain even
while doubting the existence of the external world [descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md). Richard Rorty
proposed that *incorrigibility*, the impossibility of being overruled by others, is the very mark of the
mental [rorty-1970](../sources/rorty-1970.md). Whether acquaintance is a genuine datum or itself a fallible
representation is a live dispute in this web ([acquaintance-datum-or-representation](acquaintance-datum-or-representation.md);
compare [introspective-acquaintance-is-datum](../claims/introspective-acquaintance-is-datum.md) and
[introspection-is-fallible-representation](../claims/introspection-is-fallible-representation.md)).

*Transparency.* A third account notes that when we try to attend to our experience, we seem to find only
the world. G. E. Moore observed that consciousness is "transparent" or "diaphanous": in trying to fix
attention on the sensation of blue, one sees only the blue [moore-1903](../sources/moore-1903.md). Gilbert Harman and
Michael Tye made this the basis of representationalism, on which the character of experience is
exhausted by what it represents [harman-1990](../sources/harman-1990.md), [tye-2000](../sources/tye-2000.md). Gareth Evans proposed that
we answer the question "Do I believe there will be a third world war?" by considering whether there will
be one, directing attention outward rather than inward [evans-1982](../sources/evans-1982.md). Alex Byrne extends the
idea to perception: one knows that one sees a red thing by attending to the red thing and inferring that
one sees it [byrne-2018](../sources/byrne-2018.md). On this view, self-knowledge of experience is achieved by looking
*through* experience at the world, which explains why there is no inner organ to be found.

*Interpretation.* A fourth account holds that we know our minds largely as we know other people's, by
interpreting the available evidence. Peter Carruthers's *interpretive sensory-access* theory holds that
we have direct access only to sensory states, including inner speech and imagery, and infer our beliefs,
desires and decisions from them, often wrongly [carruthers-2011](../sources/carruthers-2011.md). Classic experiments by
Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson found that people confidently cite reasons for their choices that
demonstrably played no role [nisbett-wilson-1977](../sources/nisbett-wilson-1977.md) (see [introspective-confabulation](../arguments/introspective-confabulation.md)).
Eric Schwitzgebel argues that even reports of current experience are unreliable: people disagree, and
are often wrong, about how sharp their visual periphery is, what their emotions feel like, and whether
thought has a distinctive experiential character [schwitzgebel-2008](../sources/schwitzgebel-2008.md).

*Constitution.* A fifth account, associated with Shoemaker and others, holds that in a rational creature
having certain mental states and believing that one has them are not two independent facts, so that
self-knowledge is not detection at all but part of what it is to have a mind of a certain kind
[shoemaker-1994](../sources/shoemaker-1994.md), [gertler-2011](../sources/gertler-2011.md).

These accounts are not exclusive. A common view is that knowledge of one's current sensory experience is
the best candidate for acquaintance or something like it, while knowledge of one's beliefs, motives and
character relies far more on transparency and interpretation, and is correspondingly fallible
[sep-introspection](../sources/sep-introspection.md), [sep-self-knowledge](../sources/sep-self-knowledge.md).

### How the puzzles connect

The two puzzles explain each other. If self-knowledge of experience is acquaintance, then it is a form
of knowing that only the subject can have, and communication can transmit at most the structure of what
is known, leaving the quality to be supplied by the hearer's own experiences, if they have similar ones.
If instead self-knowledge is inner sense or interpretation, it is a third-person-style process directed at
one's own states, and the privacy of experience becomes a contingent fact about access rather than a
deep feature of experience itself. If, finally, the illusionists are right, both the apparent privacy
and the apparent certainty are products of the brain's model of itself. A related puzzle is that our
reports of experience are produced by brain processes that would occur, and produce the same reports,
whatever the truth about experience, which raises the question of how such reports could be evidence for
it ([paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment](../concepts/paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment.md)).

### In plain terms

You can tell a friend the time, or the way to the station, and then they know what you know. But you
cannot tell them what red looks like to you, or what your coffee tastes like, so that they know it the
way you do. Philosophers have long wondered why. Maybe language can only carry patterns and
relationships, never the raw feel itself. Maybe your friend can understand only if they have had a
similar experience. Or maybe there is nothing hidden to share at all. There is a second puzzle. You learn
about the world by seeing and hearing it, but you do not *see* your own experiences, since there is no
inner eye. So how do you know what you are feeling? Some say there is a kind of inner sense after all;
some say experience is simply present to you; some say you find out by looking outward at the world; and
some say you largely work it out, often getting it wrong.
