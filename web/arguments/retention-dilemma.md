The [knowledge-argument](knowledge-argument.md) concludes that Mary the colour scientist gains, on first seeing
red, knowledge of something non-physical. This argument asks where that knowledge is kept afterwards.
Knowledge, unlike a passing experience, is retained and used, and the only thing that persists in Mary
from her release to her later uses of what she learned is the lasting state of her brain. Comparing
Mary with a complete physical duplicate who never saw red then forces a choice on anyone who holds
that her knowledge is phenomenal.

### The two Marys

*Released Mary* leaves her room, sees a red tomato, and is changed by it. A month later her brain is in
some state *S*. *Swamp Mary*, borrowed from Daniel Dennett ([dennett-2005](../sources/dennett-2005.md)), never leaves the
room; a bolt of lightning happens to rearrange her brain into exactly the state *S*. The duplication is
**complete**: it includes every lasting trace in Released Mary, her new recognitional and imaginative
competences and also her memory traces, so that Swamp Mary has an apparent episodic memory of seeing a
red tomato, which in her case is false. Dennett's RoboMary, who installs the same state in herself by
calculation instead of by accident ([write-access-installs-binding](../claims/write-access-installs-binding.md)), would serve equally well.

The completeness matters. A Swamp Mary given only the competences, who "just knows" what red looks
like without remembering any occasion of seeing it, differs physically from Released Mary and raises a
separate question, whether knowing what red is like requires an episodic memory. That question has an
answer independent of this argument: the knowledge survives forgetting the occasion
([phenomenal-knowledge-needs-lasting-vehicle](../claims/phenomenal-knowledge-needs-lasting-vehicle.md)). The dilemma needs duplicates alike in every
lasting respect, so that the only difference between the two Marys is that one of them saw red.

### The argument

The inference form is a dilemma.

1. A first encounter teaches a learner something exactly when it leaves a lasting change beyond what a
   repeat encounter would leave ([snapshot-test-first-encounter-learning](../claims/snapshot-test-first-encounter-learning.md)).
2. Knowledge of what an experience is like persists as a competence guiding later recognition,
   imagination and report, and so needs a lasting vehicle
   ([phenomenal-knowledge-needs-lasting-vehicle](../claims/phenomenal-knowledge-needs-lasting-vehicle.md)).
3. That lasting change can in principle be produced without the experience, by accident or by a learner
   able to write its own representations ([write-access-installs-binding](../claims/write-access-installs-binding.md)), so a complete
   duplicate of Released Mary who never saw red is possible.
4. Either Swamp Mary knows what red is like, or she does not.
5. If she does, the knowledge is carried by the lasting state both Marys share, and it can be had
   without the experience, against the knowledge argument's claim that only the experience delivers it.
6. If she does not, then what separates the knower from the non-knower is not anything in either of
   them now but only the fact that one of them once had the experience.
7. Therefore the phenomenal realist must either locate Mary's new knowledge in the lasting change in
   her brain or make it depend on her history rather than on her present state
   ([phenomenal-knowledge-present-or-historical](../claims/phenomenal-knowledge-present-or-historical.md)).

### The second horn

The second horn is less outlandish than it first sounds, because there is a precedent for it. Donald
Davidson, who introduced the Swampman case, held that a duplicate assembled by chance lacks thoughts and
reference, since these depend on causal history ([davidson-1987](../sources/davidson-1987.md)), and history-based theories of
content take the same line ([swampman](swampman.md) presses the case against them). The realist could
treat phenomenal knowledge in the same way.

The cost is that this makes the knowledge argument's prize extrinsic. On the second horn, Mary's
knowing what red is like is like a banknote's being genuine: a matter of origin that no inspection of
the note can reveal. Neither Mary could tell from the inside whether she knows, both would make the
same judgements with the same confidence, and the knowledge would make no difference to anything either
later thought, said or did. The knowledge argument was meant to show that Mary becomes acquainted with
an intrinsic quality of experience. A knowledge whose presence depends on history and is invisible in
the knower is an odd thing to have won.

### Bearing on Jackson's own view

The dilemma presses hardest on the view Frank Jackson defended alongside the knowledge argument, that
qualia are epiphenomenal ([jackson-1982](../sources/jackson-1982.md)). In §IV of that paper (pp. 133–135) he answers the
objection that epiphenomenal qualia could not be known in *other* people, with his model of two
newspapers reporting the same match: behaviour and qualia are separate effects of a common cause in the
brain. He does not address Mary's knowledge of her *own* qualia, kept and used after release. On his own
common-cause model, everything she later does with that knowledge is caused by the lasting brain trace,
which Swamp Mary shares, and the quale contributes nothing to it. That is the familiar charge of
self-stultification against [epiphenomenalism](../positions/epiphenomenalism.md), in a form specific to Mary.

### The weakest premise

Premise 3 is the most exposed. A realist can deny that the lasting state *S* could arise without the
experience, holding that some part of it can be produced only by actually having the experience. That
would have to be a claim about physics or neurophysiology: that some brain state cannot be produced by
any physical cause other than the visual one. It is hard to see what could make a physical state
uniquely producible in this way. The realist can also accept the first horn and give up the premise that
only experience delivers the knowledge, at the cost of the knowledge argument itself.

### In plain terms

Imagine two Marys with exactly the same brain a month after one of them left the black-and-white room
and saw a red tomato. The other never left: a freak lightning bolt rewired her brain into exactly the
same state, false memories of a tomato included. Do both know what red looks like? If yes, then you can
know it without ever seeing red, which the famous argument denies. If no, then knowing what red looks
like is not anything in your head; it is just a fact about your past that leaves no mark you could ever
notice, like the difference between a real banknote and a perfect counterfeit. Either way the argument
is in trouble. The case is borrowed from Dennett's "Swamp Mary", and it echoes Davidson's "Swampman"
([swampman](swampman.md)).
