Many arguments about consciousness rest, openly or quietly, on the assumption that a subject has
privileged and reliable access to their own qualia: if your experience is a certain way, you can tell,
and if you sincerely judge it to be a certain way, it is. This argument observes that two claims taken
seriously in the literature deny that assumption, and traces what follows for the arguments that need
it. No standard argument pattern fits; the move is an undercutting defeater aimed at a shared
presupposition.

### The argument

The inference form is deductive.

1. A subject can have qualia that are inaccessible to their introspection or report
   ([qualia-can-be-inaccessible](../claims/qualia-can-be-inaccessible.md)).
2. A subject's sincere judgements about their own qualia can be false
   ([subject-can-be-wrong-about-own-qualia](../claims/subject-can-be-wrong-about-own-qualia.md)).
3. Therefore any argument that needs a subject's self-knowledge of qualia to be complete or infallible
   rests on a false premise, and any argument that needs it only to be generally reliable must show that
   it is reliable in the case at hand.

### The arguments affected

- **Introspection as a direct datum.** The view that introspective acquaintance meets the experience
  itself, and so cannot be wrong wholesale ([introspective-acquaintance-is-datum](../claims/introspective-acquaintance-is-datum.md)), is directly
  denied by premise 2.
- **Privileged access.** The thesis that each subject has direct, non-inferential acquaintance with
  its own conscious states ([privileged-first-person-access](../claims/privileged-first-person-access.md)) is denied by premise 1 if read, as
  it usually is, as covering *all* of a subject's conscious states. A direct but partial and fallible
  acquaintance survives; the privileged access these arguments need does not. The general notion is
  set out in [first-person-access](../concepts/first-person-access.md).
- **Appearance is reality.** John Searle argues that for occurrent experience the appearance just is
  the reality, so there is no gap for introspective error to occupy ([no-appearance-reality-gap](no-appearance-reality-gap.md)).
  Premise 2 denies the conclusion, and premise 1 denies the underlying picture: if an experience can
  exist without appearing to its subject at all, its reality is not the same as its appearance.
- **Acquaintance with intrinsic nature.** The [intrinsic-nature](intrinsic-nature.md) needs the premise that
  experience is the one intrinsic nature we know directly ([consciousness-only-known-intrinsic](../claims/consciousness-only-known-intrinsic.md)).
  If acquaintance can miss or misjudge the quality, it is no window onto an intrinsic nature, and the
  route from introspection to panpsychism and [russellian-monism](../positions/russellian-monism.md) loses its starting point.
- **Fading and dancing qualia.** David Chalmers's two thought experiments
  ([fading-qualia](fading-qualia.md), [dancing-qualia](dancing-qualia.md)) argue that if your qualia faded or flipped
  while your functional organisation stayed fixed, you would fail to notice, which is held to be absurd.
  Both treat an *unnoticed* change in one's own qualia as impossible. Given premises 1 and 2, it is not
  absurd at all, and the reductio fails. This point has already been pressed in the web in narrower
  forms: that the reductio presupposes functionalism about introspective judgement
  ([dancing-qualia-presupposes-functional-introspection](../claims/dancing-qualia-presupposes-functional-introspection.md), argued in
  [dancing-qualia-begs-the-question](dancing-qualia-begs-the-question.md)), and that "noticing" a flip needs a memory comparison
  whose earlier term is itself functionally fixed ([dancing-qualia-no-memory-trace](dancing-qualia-no-memory-trace.md)).
- **The knowledge argument and acquaintance replies.** Mary is supposed to know, on release, that she
  has learned something about what red is like ([knowledge-argument](knowledge-argument.md)), and the
  [acquaintance-hypothesis](acquaintance-hypothesis.md) treats her gain as direct acquaintance. Both lean on her access to
  the new quality being reliable, though less heavily than the arguments above.
- **Knowing that one is not a zombie.** Chalmers's reply to the worry that a zombie twin would make all
  the same judgements about its experience is that we, unlike it, are acquainted with our qualia (the
  [paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment](../concepts/paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment.md)). If that acquaintance is fallible, the reply is weakened, and with it part of the case built on
  [zombie-conceivability](zombie-conceivability.md).

### A double edge

The premises do not favour one side of the qualia debate. They undercut arguments *for* intrinsic qualia
that rest on acquaintance, but they also undercut the fading and dancing qualia arguments, which defend
the functionalist-friendly thesis that experience is fixed by functional organisation. They are also the
natural reply available to a defender of intrinsic qualia against [qualia-relational-from-cases](qualia-relational-from-cases.md):
the quality may be real but hidden. That reply carries a cost. A quality that no one, including its
owner, could ever detect even indirectly would be an idle residue ([real-feel-residue-is-idle](real-feel-residue-is-idle.md)).
So the premises help the realist only to the extent that inaccessible or misjudged qualia still leave
some observable trace. The best-known defence against the idleness charge, that the feel is the most
observed thing there is because it is observed from the first person
([feel-is-the-watching-not-an-unwatched-difference](feel-is-the-watching-not-an-unwatched-difference.md)), is unavailable for inaccessible qualia,
which by hypothesis are not watched at all.

### The weakest premise

Premise 1 is the more contested. Defenders of the view that access exhausts consciousness, such as
global workspace theorists ([global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md)), read the Sperling results as showing rich access to fragmentary contents,
not experience without access. Premise 2 is supported by confabulation studies showing that reports about
one's own mind are often theory-driven constructions ([introspective-confabulation](introspective-confabulation.md)), and is
widely accepted for peripheral and fleeting experience, but
defenders of privileged access can restrict their arguments to attended, stable qualia, where error is
least likely.

### In plain terms

A lot of famous arguments about consciousness quietly assume that you always know what your own
experience is like. This argument points out that some philosophers deny that: you might have
experiences you can't notice, or be wrong about the ones you do notice. If they are right, those
famous arguments lose a step. That cuts both ways, since it weakens arguments on both sides of the
debate about whether experience can be explained scientifically.
