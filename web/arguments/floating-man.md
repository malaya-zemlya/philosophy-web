The *floating man* (also called the *flying man*) is a thought experiment devised by the Persian
philosopher Ibn Sīnā, known in Latin as Avicenna (980–1037), to show that the human soul is something
other than the body. It appears twice in the psychology of his great encyclopedia, the *Kitāb
al-Shifāʾ* (*Book of the Cure*), at the beginning and near the end of the treatise on the soul
[avicenna-de-anima](../sources/avicenna-de-anima.md). Avicenna offers it less as a strict proof than as a pointer that would
alert a reflective person to the soul's existence and its independence from the body
[marmura-1986](../sources/marmura-1986.md), [sep-arabic-islamic-mind](../sources/sep-arabic-islamic-mind.md), [sep-ibn-sina](../sources/sep-ibn-sina.md). It anticipates by six centuries the reasoning of Descartes's *Meditations*
[descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md).

### The thought experiment

We are asked to imagine a man created all at once, in the full maturity of his faculties, but with his
sight veiled so that he sees nothing. He is suspended in empty air, or a void, so that he feels no
resistance or contact, and his limbs are spread apart so that no part of his body touches another. He
has no memory of past sensation, since he has only just come into being, and no sensation now. Would he
affirm that he exists? Avicenna holds that he would. He would affirm the existence of his own self, and
yet he would not affirm the existence of any limb, any internal organ, his heart or his brain, or anything
external to him. He might not even know that he has a body.

### The argument

The reasoning has the form of a thought experiment: an imagined case, an intuition about it, and a bridge principle connecting the intuition to a thesis.

1. ([floating-man-affirms-self](../claims/floating-man-affirms-self.md)) A man in the floating condition, with no sensation of anything,
   would still affirm his own existence.
2. In that condition he would not affirm the existence of his body or of any of its parts.
3. ([affirmed-differs-from-unaffirmed](../claims/affirmed-differs-from-unaffirmed.md)) What one affirms while not affirming something else is
   distinct from that other thing.

∴ The self whose existence the man affirms is distinct from his body and all its parts, which supports
[soul-is-immaterial-substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md): the soul is a substance in its own right, not the body or a
feature of it.

### The weak point

The intuition in premise (1) is widely granted, and premise (2) is built into the scenario. The argument
stands or falls with the bridge, premise (3), which raises the thought-experiment pattern's critical
question about whether the intuition really bears on the thesis. Read as a principle about what one
*knows* or *affirms*, premise (3) is invalid: it is an instance of the *masked man fallacy*. A person
can know that her father is in the room while not knowing that the masked man is, even if the masked man
is her father. What one affirms depends on how a thing is presented, so the floating man's failure to
affirm his body shows only that his self is not presented to him *as* a body, not that it is not a body.
The same objection was pressed against Descartes's argument from doubt.

Defenders have read the argument in two more resistant ways. On one, it concerns the *concept* of the
self: since the floating man has a complete grasp of what he is without any bodily concept entering into
it, being bodily cannot belong to the essence of the self. On another, it is not an argument at all but
an exercise in drawing attention to a self-awareness that is direct and non-sensory, prior to all
perception, which later philosophers in the Islamic world made central to their theories of
self-knowledge. Critics reply that the first reading assumes the self's essence is transparent to
introspection, which is the very point at issue, and that the second shows at most that self-awareness is
non-sensory, which a physicalist can accept.

### In plain terms

Imagine you were suddenly created floating in empty space, blindfolded, with your arms and legs spread
so that nothing touches anything, and no memories. You would feel nothing at all, not even your own body.
Would you still know that *you* exist? Avicenna thought obviously yes. But you would not know that you
have a body. So, he concluded, the "you" that you know about must be something other than your body: a
soul. The usual objection is that not knowing something about yourself does not make it untrue. Someone
can know their father is in the room without knowing the masked man is, even though the masked man *is*
their father. Similarly, you might fail to notice that you are a body without thereby being something
other than a body.
