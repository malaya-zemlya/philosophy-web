*RoboDennett* is a robot devised by Michael Beaton in reply to Daniel Dennett's RoboMary
([beaton-2005](../sources/beaton-2005.md)). It knows everything about its own colour vision, as RoboMary does, but has
no abilities beyond those its knowledge entails. Beaton argues that RoboDennett still does not know
what seeing red is like, so that the intuition behind the knowledge argument is correct about Mary,
and that this is fully compatible with physicalism.

### RoboMary

Dennett's RoboMary is a colour-equipped robot fitted from the start with black-and-white cameras
([dennett-2005](../sources/dennett-2005.md)). She learns everything about the colour vision of robots of her model and
comes in two versions. *Unlocked* RoboMary writes software that colours in her camera input, checks
her colour registers against those of normally sighted robots, and so already knows what seeing
colour is like before her colour cameras arrive. *Locked* RoboMary cannot set her colour registers,
since they are filtered to grey. She builds a model of herself, feeds it a red tomato, compares the
state the model enters (Dennett's state B) with her own grey-tomato state, and then "makes all the
necessary adjustment and puts herself into state B". Either way, she meets her first red object
with no surprise. Dennett concludes that a Mary who really knew all the physical facts would learn
nothing on release ([mary-learns-nothing-new](../claims/mary-learns-nothing-new.md), [mary-misimagined](mary-misimagined.md)).

### Beaton's reply

Beaton accepts that both robots can do what Dennett says. His objection is that each does something
the knowledge argument does not give Mary. To refute the knowledge argument, Dennett needs physicalism
to imply that *every* agent who knows all the physical facts can work out what seeing red is like. It
is not enough that *some* physically possible agents can (pp. 14–16). Beaton therefore proposes a
criterion of cheating: an agent cheats if it uses abilities other than those entailed by the
hypotheses of the knowledge argument, namely knowing and reasoning about the physical facts, and
being able to come to know what colours look like in the way we do (p. 16). RoboDennett is the
robot with exactly those abilities.

The argument then runs as follows.

1. To know what it is like to see red is to have one's abstract reasoning about red supported by
   low-level colour processing that discriminates and responds to red
   ([knowing-what-its-like-is-grounded-concept](../claims/knowing-what-its-like-is-grounded-concept.md)).
2. Unlocked RoboMary gets that support by writing directly to her own low-level registers. Nothing in
   knowing the physical facts gives a human, or RoboDennett, that power, so she is cheating.
3. RoboDennett can do everything Locked RoboMary does short of her last step. He can simulate
   himself seeing red, and the simulation then knows what red is like. But that state belongs to the
   simulation, not to him ([self-simulation-knows-not-simulator](../claims/self-simulation-knows-not-simulator.md)).
4. To take the last step he would have to put his own colour processing, or the simulated one, into
   the right causal relation with his reasoning. Knowing all the facts about his own processing does
   not entail the ability to do that ([physical-knowledge-not-self-reconfiguration](../claims/physical-knowledge-not-self-reconfiguration.md)).
5. So RoboDennett does not know what seeing red is like, despite knowing all the physical facts
   ([restricted-knower-lacks-what-its-like](../claims/restricted-knower-lacks-what-its-like.md)).

Beaton also meets Dennett on his own ground, *heterophenomenology*, the method of treating a subject's
behaviour and reports as the evidence about their experience. Since RoboDennett knows exactly what he
would say and do after seeing red, why can he not simply say and do it? Beaton's answer is that much
of what seeing colour changes is not under voluntary control: fast, non-conscious responses, and
low-level reactions such as the changes in alertness that red light produces in monkeys. RoboDennett
can know all about these but cannot produce them by thinking, and when he tries, his consciously
computed responses come too late (pp. 21–22). The difference is therefore one that behaviour could
reveal.

### Significance

Beaton's conclusion is that RoboDennett, not RoboMary, is the right surrogate for Mary, and that
the Mary intuition is the correct intuition about an agent who starts off like us. His wider aim is
a "physicalist, functionalist, heterophenomenological, but essentially qualia-realist" account of
experience (p. 23). In this he sides with the ability replies of Lewis, Nemirow and the later
Jackson ([ability-hypothesis](ability-hypothesis.md)), against Dennett's lone view that Mary learns nothing.

The reply anticipates a distinction later drawn for learning systems, between simulating oneself
and writing to oneself ([self-access-kinds](../concepts/self-access-kinds.md)). A language model that can write its own
weights can install in advance what a first encounter would provide
([write-access-installs-binding](../claims/write-access-installs-binding.md)), and the same point appears in the claim that Mary's gain
depends on how much of her colour pathway can be set without colour experience
([mary-gain-depends-on-prewiring](../claims/mary-gain-depends-on-prewiring.md)). Write access in both cases is an additional ability,
which is Beaton's point.

### The weakest premise

Premise 1 is the most exposed. Beaton states his conclusions in terms of his own analysis of knowing
what it is like, and concedes that he has no argument that the heterophenomenological conclusion
holds if that analysis is rejected (p. 17, n. 11). Premise 4 is also open to Dennett, who suggests
that Locked RoboMary, once her self-model is complete, "can arrange for it to take over for her
locked onboard color system" ([dennett-2005](../sources/dennett-2005.md)). Whether that arrangement uses an ability the
knowledge argument grants or one that it does not is exactly where the two accounts part company.

### In plain terms

Mary is the famous scientist who knows everything physical about colour vision but has never seen
colour. The philosopher Daniel Dennett imagined a robot version, RoboMary, who uses her complete
self-knowledge to set her own circuits as seeing red would, so she is not surprised when she finally
sees it ([mary-misimagined](mary-misimagined.md)). Michael Beaton replied that this is cheating. Knowing all
about your own wiring does not give you the power to rewire yourself, and humans plainly lack that
power. A robot without it, which Beaton calls RoboDennett, could predict perfectly how it would react
to red and still not know what red looks like. Beaton thinks this is entirely consistent with
everything being physical.
