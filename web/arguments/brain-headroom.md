The human brain already shows that ordinary matter, suitably organized, can think — and it does so
under biological handicaps an engineer need not accept. From those two observations the
brain-headroom argument concludes that a system reaching and then exceeding human cognitive
performance across the board is compatible with physics: the [physical possibility of superintelligence](../claims/superintelligence-physically-possible.md).
The brain is the existence proof; the gap between how the brain is built and how a machine could be
built is the headroom.

The inference is abductive and extrapolative rather than deductive. It does not prove that a
superintelligence will be built, or even that one can be built in practice; it argues that the one
working example of general intelligence we possess gives us no reason to posit a ceiling at the human
level, and several reasons to expect room above it.

1. The human brain is a physical mechanism whose cognitive performance arises from its physical
   organization — the arrangement and activity of its neurons — and from nothing outside the physical
   order. It is, in this sense, a machine that thinks.
2. An engineered system could in principle realize the same organization — by whole-brain emulation,
   or by re-engineering the same functional structure in another medium — and would thereby realize
   the same cognitive performance ([chalmers-2010](../sources/chalmers-2010.md)).
3. The brain's design is constrained by its biology in ways an engineered system is not: neural
   signalling runs at speeds on the order of a millionth of those in electronics; brain size is capped
   by the skull and the birth canal; neurons cannot be losslessly copied, backed up, or precisely
   edited; and the whole organ is the product of blind evolutionary search rather than deliberate
   design, optimized for ancestral survival rather than for thinking well ([bostrom-2014](../sources/bostrom-2014.md)).
   These are contingent limits on the one existing example, not limits on what physics permits.
4. Therefore a system that matches human cognitive performance and then exceeds it across virtually
   all domains — by relaxing the very constraints biology imposed — is compatible with physics. That
   is the conclusion: [superintelligence-physically-possible](../claims/superintelligence-physically-possible.md).

The move from premises 1–3 to 4 is the abductive step. Given a physical mechanism that thinks (1–2)
and manifestly poor engineering in that mechanism (3), the best explanation of the observed
capability is that thinking is a physical achievement with headroom above the human case, not that we
have hit a principled wall exactly at the level evolution happened to reach.

### Weakest premise

The point of attack is premise 2, the claim that an engineered system could realize the brain's
organization and thereby its performance. It presupposes that cognitive function is
*substrate-independent* — that what fixes whether a system thinks is the functional or causal
organization it realizes, with the physical material (carbon, silicon) dropping out. That
presupposition is exactly the live dispute at [substrate-independence](../questions/substrate-independence.md). A biological
naturalist who holds that cognition depends essentially on the causal powers of neural biology can
grant premises 1 and 3 and still deny 2: emulating the organization in another medium would then
reproduce the input-output profile without reproducing the thinking. So the argument establishes the
physical possibility of superintelligence only on the (contested) assumption that mind travels with
organization rather than with substrate; it does not settle that assumption by itself.

### In plain terms

Your brain is a physical object, and it thinks. That already proves matter can be arranged so that it
thinks — so there is no magic ingredient a machine is missing in principle. And your brain is, frankly,
badly built for the job: its signals crawl compared to electronics, it can't be made bigger than a
skull allows, it can't be copied or edited, and it was thrown together by evolution for surviving on
the savannah, not for doing physics. A designed system need not carry those handicaps. Put those
together and there is no reason to think human-level thinking is the highest that physics allows —
which is all this argument claims (not that anyone will actually build a superintelligence). The
soft spot: it assumes that copying the brain's *wiring pattern* in some other material would copy the
thinking too. Whether mind rides on the pattern or on the specific biological stuff is itself an open
fight ([substrate-independence](../questions/substrate-independence.md)).
