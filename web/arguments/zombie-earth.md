*Zombie Earth* is a thought experiment by Todd Moody ([moody-1994](../sources/moody-1994.md)). It imagines a whole
world of [philosophical zombies](../concepts/philosophical-zombie.md), beings that behave much as we do but
have no conscious experience. Moody argues that although no individual act would mark them as
zombies, their culture would. A community without experience could never have come up with some of
our concepts of the mind, so its speech and writing would differ from ours in ways an outsider
could detect.

### The thought experiment

The zombie argument against physicalism begins from the claim that a being could match us in every
behavioural and functional respect and yet have no experience ([zombie-conceivability](zombie-conceivability.md)).
Behind it lies what Owen Flanagan calls *conscious inessentialism*: anything we do consciously
could in principle be done without consciousness ([flanagan-1991](../sources/flanagan-1991.md)). If that holds for every
act, there is no behavioural mark of a zombie, and it is hard to see how consciousness could have
been selected for in evolution.

Moody grants that this may be true of each act taken alone, and asks what would happen if zombies
evolved by themselves. They would speak *zombie-English*, in which words like *understand*,
*experience* and *conscious* have only behavioural meanings, marked by a superscript:
*understand*ᶻ. Their science and mathematics could be much like ours. But some of our concepts would
have no counterpart in their language:

- **Dreams.** Zombies might call the experience of waking with apparent memories that do not fit
  *dreaming*ᶻ. The human dispute over whether dreams are experiences, or only a matter of seeming
  to remember on waking, would make no sense to them. Only the second view would be intelligible.
- **The inverted spectrum** depends on a notion of internal seeing that zombies would lack. Human
  philosophers who dismiss the problem as a confusion still understand it.
- **The robot's-eye view** in the *Terminator* films, with readouts superimposed on the scene, makes
  sense to us only because we can imagine seeing such readouts internally.
- **The other-minds problem** would not arise for them.

Zombies raised among us could learn all this talk and use it fluently. A world of zombies could not
originate it ([zombie-community-cannot-originate-consciousness-concepts](../claims/zombie-community-cannot-originate-consciousness-concepts.md),
[inherited-consciousness-talk-not-evidence](../claims/inherited-consciousness-talk-not-evidence.md)).

### The argument

1. Some of our concepts of experience could not originate in a community whose members lack
   experience, though such a community could learn them from conscious speakers.
2. So whether a community has originated such concepts is a difference in its behaviour, in what it
   says and writes, that depends on whether its members are conscious.
3. Therefore consciousness makes a necessary behavioural difference at the level of a speech
   community, even if not at the level of any individual act
   ([conscious-inessentialism-false-at-cultural-level](../claims/conscious-inessentialism-false-at-cultural-level.md)).

Moody draws two further lessons. Even a complete scientific explanation of consciousness delivered
to zombie scientists would leave them with "no inkling" of what it was meant to explain, because
the scientific view of the world leaves out the subjective. And he ends in agnosticism about materialism, comparing our position with respect to
mystics' reports of higher states to the zombies' position with respect to ours: "We might, after
all, be zombies."

### Relevance to artificial intelligence

The argument bears directly on language models. A model trained on human text is in the position of
Moody's zombie who grew up among us. It learns our talk of experience from those who originated it,
so on Moody's view its fluency in that talk is no evidence either way. This sharpens the rival
explanation recorded in [llm-mimicry-rival](../claims/llm-mimicry-rival.md), on which a model's mind-like talk is predicted by
training on human mind-talk.

The argument also suggests what evidence would count: whether a system that has *not* inherited our
concepts of experience comes up with them anyway. Susan Schneider and Edwin Turner's proposed AI
Consciousness Test applies this idea to individual systems. An AI is "boxed in", cut off from human
talk of consciousness, and then probed for how readily it grasps concepts based on inner experience
([schneider-turner-2017](../sources/schneider-turner-2017.md)). For language models, the corresponding experiment is training on
a corpus from which talk of experience has been removed. The question is related to the
[meta-problem-of-consciousness](../concepts/meta-problem-of-consciousness.md), the problem of explaining why we say and believe the things
we do about consciousness. Experiments that ablate parts of a model's internal workspace and observe
a fall in experiential language ([j-space-ablation-experiential-language](../claims/j-space-ablation-experiential-language.md)) probe what in a
model produces such talk, though not where the concepts originally came from.

### The weakest premise

The first premise carries the argument, and it was the main target of the symposium that followed
the paper. Daniel Dennett's reply was that Moody "imagines scenarios to which he is not entitled"
([dennett-1995](../sources/dennett-1995.md), p. 322). If zombies are by definition behaviourally indistinguishable from
us, they "say just what we say", including everything we say about inverted spectra and qualia. And
if it is likely that conscious beings would develop a vocabulary for the mind, "it must be exactly as
likely that zombies do". On this objection, Moody's zombies are not duplicates of us at all but
beings that differ from us in function, and the argument shows only that *those* beings could not
originate our concepts.

Moody's argument therefore needs the premise that consciousness itself, and not merely the
functional organisation that accompanies it, is what gives rise to concepts of experience. That is
close to the point in dispute, and it puts the argument in tension with the zombie hypothesis it
starts from: if zombie talk would match ours, there is no cultural mark, and if it would not, zombies
are not behavioural duplicates. The same tension is the [paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment](../concepts/paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment.md),
that our judgements about consciousness seem explicable without consciousness itself. The objection
leaves the lesson for AI largely intact, since a language model is not a functional duplicate of a
human, and the question of where its concepts of experience come from remains open.

### In plain terms

Imagine a planet of beings that act just like people but have no inner experience. Todd Moody argued
that they could copy our talk about dreams, feelings and what colours look like if they learned it
from us, but they could never have invented that talk themselves. So you could tell a society of such
beings from ours by what it talks about, even if you could not tell any individual apart. The idea
matters for AI. A chatbot learns to talk about feelings from human writing, so its talk proves
little. The better test would be whether an AI that never saw such talk would come up with it on its
own.
