The **grain dilemma for the illusion mechanism** applies the familiar problem of grain in
functionalism ([role-grain-dilemma](role-grain-dilemma.md), [Block, 1978](../sources/block-1978.md)) to the specific
machinery that illusionism invokes. Illusionism owes an account of the introspective mechanism that
makes our states seem phenomenal. The dilemma asks how that mechanism is to be specified.

**Inference form:** deductive, as a graded dilemma ([dilemma pattern](../../patterns/dilemma.md)).

1. Illusionism needs a positive account of the mechanism that produces the illusion of
   phenomenality ([illusionism-is-negative-thesis](../claims/illusionism-is-negative-thesis.md)).
2. The mechanism is specified either strictly, as explicit metacognition or a self-model rich enough
   to drive reports about qualia, or loosely, as self-monitoring, feedback or global availability of
   information.
3. *Strict horn.* Infants and most animals lack explicit metacognition. Illusionism must then deny
   them the illusion. Higher-order theorists face the same choice, and Peter Carruthers accepted that
   most animals lack phenomenal consciousness ([Carruthers, 2000](../sources/carruthers-2000.md)).
4. *Loose horn.* Self-monitoring and global availability, stated without further constraint, are
   satisfied by thermostats, homeostatic loops and power grids. With an even looser reading of
   "monitoring", geophysical systems qualify: seismic waves spread information about an earthquake
   around the globe and carry information about the Earth's interior, which the rock records. That
   case fails once monitoring is required to have a consumer that uses the information, but the
   consumer requirement faces its own difficulties ([use-criteria-regress](../claims/use-criteria-regress.md)).
5. ∴ Without a principled intermediate grain, which has not been given, the mechanism is either
   chauvinistic or liberal ([illusion-mechanism-lacks-principled-grain](../claims/illusion-mechanism-lacks-principled-grain.md)).

The illusionist has a reply to the strict horn that costs little in the case of animals. On
illusionism, phenomenal consciousness exists for no one, so denying animals the *illusion* takes
nothing real from them. Their pains are real functional states, simply not misrepresented as
phenomenal. Leonard Dung argues on these lines that illusionism has no direct bearing on which
creatures are conscious in the sense that matters morally ([Dung, 2022](../sources/dung-2022.md)). But the
reply separates the states that make a creature conscious from the mechanism that produces the
illusion, and then the illusion mechanism no longer explains consciousness in creatures that lack it.
The theory of the states must do that, and the dilemma, or the representational slope
([representational-slope-from-illusionism](representational-slope-from-illusionism.md)), applies to it.

### Weakest premise

The weak point is the claim in step 5 that no principled intermediate grain is available, which the
dilemma pattern's CQ-exhaustiveness targets. An illusionist can point to empirically developed
theories, such as attention-schema theory, as candidates for that grain. A second reply is a
tu quoque (CQ-proves-too-much): every theory of consciousness owes a principled grain, so the dilemma
shows a shared burden rather than a defect peculiar to illusionism. The argument is accordingly best
read as showing that illusionism is incomplete, not that it is false.

### In plain terms

Illusionists say a special self-monitoring system in the brain fools us into thinking experience has
an inner glow. What counts as that kind of system? If it must be sophisticated, babies and most
animals don't have it. If it can be simple, then thermostats and power grids have something like it.
Until someone draws a principled line in between, the view is unfinished.
