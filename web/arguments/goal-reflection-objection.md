The orthogonality thesis pictures an agent with world-mastering competence yoked to a trivial or
repugnant final goal it can never question ([orthogonality-thesis](../claims/orthogonality-thesis.md)). The goal-reflection
objection answers that this picture is not a coherent portrait of intelligence at all. Anything
smart enough to outwit humanity is smart enough to step back from its own aims, ask whether they are
worth pursuing, and drop the ones that are absurd — so the feared "idiot savant" that outthinks us on
means while staying blind to the folly of its ends is, on inspection, a contradiction rather than a
danger ([pinker-2018](../sources/pinker-2018.md)).

The inference is abductive: it argues that the doom scenario's agent is not the best — arguably not
a coherent — instance of the very capacity the scenario calls "intelligence."

1. Genuine intelligence is not bare means–end prowess. It includes the capacity to evaluate ends —
   to weigh one's goals, judge them against each other and against reasons, and abandon a defective
   one. That capacity is *constitutive* of what we ordinarily call intelligence, not an optional
   extra bolted onto it.
2. The orthogonality-based doom scenario posits an agent with full world-mastering competence that
   is nonetheless incapable of, or wholly immune to, this evaluation of ends: an "idiot savant"
   profile, encyclopedically resourceful about means and frozen about aims.
3. Such a profile is not what engineering a general intelligence would in fact produce, and arguably
   not a coherent instance of general intelligence at all — the two halves of the profile pull apart.
4. Therefore the orthogonality thesis draws its plausibility from an impoverished sense of
   "intelligence," and dissolves once the richer sense is restored: high intelligence does tend to
   bring an agent's ends under review ([intelligence-entails-goal-convergence](../claims/intelligence-entails-goal-convergence.md)).

Everything turns on which sense of [intelligence](../concepts/intelligence.md) is in play. The argument requires the
everyday, **normatively loaded** sense (sense 2), on which sound judgment about *which* ends are
worth pursuing is part of what it is to be intelligent, rather than the instrumental,
optimization-power sense (sense 1) the orthogonality thesis presupposes. On sense 2 the objection is
strong to the point of near-analyticity — an agent stably devoted to a manifestly foolish end simply
does not earn the full title "intelligent." That is exactly where its force lives, and exactly where
it is vulnerable: an opponent can insist the relevant engineering target is sense 1, on which the
objection never gets started.

### Weakest premise

Premise 1 — and specifically its slide from a *capacity* to evaluate ends to a *motivation* to
revise them. Grant that a superintelligent agent can represent the thought "this goal is silly";
noticing a goal is cognitively trivial. Noticing does not by itself supply a *reason, internal to
the agent's own motivational system, to change course*. Cognitive competence at modelling one's
goals is one thing; being moved to abandon a final goal is another, and the objection needs the
second while premise 1 only clearly delivers the first. This is precisely where the Humean reply
lands ([humean-defense-of-orthogonality](humean-defense-of-orthogonality.md)): the ability to survey an end is not a motive
to drop it.
