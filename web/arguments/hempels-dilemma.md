**Hempel's dilemma** is an objection to [physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md) that targets its formulation
rather than its truth. Physicalism says that everything is physical, and the natural way to explain
"physical" is by reference to physics. But which physics? If it is the physics we have now, the
thesis is almost certainly false, since present physics is incomplete. If it is some future or ideal
physics, the thesis has no definite content, since nobody knows what that physics will say. Either
way, the doctrine fails to be both true and informative.

The argument is named after the philosopher of science Carl Gustav Hempel, who stated it in a
commentary on Nelson Goodman (Hempel, [1980](../sources/hempel-1980.md)), developing a doubt he had raised
earlier about defining the physical in purely ontological terms ([1969](../sources/hempel-1969.md)). The
name was in use by the late 1990s (Melnyk, [1997](../sources/melnyk-1997.md)), and the dilemma is now the
standard starting point for discussions of what ["physical"](../concepts/physical.md) means.

### The argument

The dilemma is deductive. It takes the theory-based conception of the physical as given and splits
it into its two possible readings:

1. To be physical is to be the kind of thing posited by physics, either the physics of today or
   some future or ideal physics.
2. Present-day physics is incomplete and, in some respects, false
   ([current-physics-incomplete](../claims/current-physics-incomplete.md)).
3. So physicalism defined by present physics is false: some things exist that present physics does
   not posit, whatever the truth about minds.
4. The content of a future or ideal physics cannot be specified in advance
   ([future-physics-unspecifiable](../claims/future-physics-unspecifiable.md)).
5. So physicalism defined by future physics has no determinate content: it says only that
   everything is whatever the final physics turns out to posit, which might include mental
   properties.
6. Therefore physicalism stated by reference to physics is either false or without determinate
   content ([physicalism-false-or-empty](../claims/physicalism-false-or-empty.md)).

Each horn can be made concrete. On the first, quantum theory and general relativity have not been
unified, and the nature of dark matter is unknown, so a list of what current physics posits is
almost certainly not a list of everything there is. Physicalism so defined would be refuted by the
next revision of physics, even if that revision had nothing to do with the mind. On the second,
imagine that some future physics, to explain the brain, adds a fundamental field whose values are
felt experiences. A physicalism defined by that physics would count the field as physical, and so
would be true in a world that is plainly dualist in everything but name.

**Weakest premise.** Step 5 is the usual target: the inference from "we cannot now say what future
physics will contain" to "a physicalism defined by future physics says nothing". Most replies to the
dilemma argue that something definite can be said about future physics in advance, even if its
details are unknown. A smaller group attacks the first horn instead, denying that the probable
falsity of present physics makes a physicalism indexed to it unreasonable to hold.

### Taking the first horn

One response accepts a definition in terms of present physics and lives with the consequences.
Lewis ([1994](../sources/lewis-1994.md)) ties materialism to the posits of present physics or of a physics
not too different from it. Melnyk ([1997](../sources/melnyk-1997.md)) argues that physicalism should be
held the way scientific theories are held: as the best available hypothesis, rationally believed to
be closer to the truth than its rivals even though it is probably false in detail. On this view the
first horn shows only that physicalism is fallible, which every empirical thesis is.

Critics reply that this makes being physical depend on which physical theory happens to be true.
Stoljar ([2010](../sources/stoljar-2010.md)) presses a modal version of the dilemma. A world governed
entirely by Newtonian mechanics would surely be a physicalist world, yet its basic properties are not
those of our present physics. So the physical cannot be defined by the physics that is true in our
world, or current in our time, without misclassifying such cases.

### Taking the second horn

A second response accepts a definition in terms of future physics but adds a constraint known in
advance. On the *via negativa*, what physicalists need is only that the fundamental level contains
nothing mental, whatever else it contains (Montero & Papineau, [2005](../sources/montero-papineau-2005.md)).
Wilson ([2006](../sources/wilson-2006.md)) combines the two ideas: the physical is what current or future
physics treats, provided nothing fundamental is mental. The imagined experience field is then ruled
out as non-physical ([no-fundamental-mentality-reply](no-fundamental-mentality-reply.md)), and physicalism keeps a definite content however physics develops. Spurrett and
Papineau ([1999](../sources/spurrett-papineau-1999.md)) make a parallel move for the causal argument:
its completeness premise needs only the claim that physical effects have sufficient causes that are
not distinctively mental, which is untouched by uncertainty about future physics.

The cost is that physicalism becomes, at bottom, the denial of fundamental mentality. That captures
what most physicalists care about, but it rules out by definition a world whose basic properties are
at once mental and physical, and it says little about non-mental rivals such as vitalism.

### Denying that the conclusion matters

A third response questions the dilemma's assumption that an acceptable concept needs an explicit
definition. Stoljar ([2010](../sources/stoljar-2010.md)) argues that the failure of one style of
definition, by reference to physics at a given date, does not show that we lack the concept of the
physical. We use many concepts competently without being able to define them. On this view the
physical sciences from Newton to quantum field theory may form a family united by overlapping
resemblances rather than a common definition, and the concept can be understood through such
paradigms.

Ney ([2008](../sources/ney-2008.md)) proposes a different escape: physicalism is not a thesis that must be
stated, but an attitude, the resolve to take one's ontology from physics. Critics reply that the
dilemma returns in a new form, since one must still say whether the attitude is directed at present
or ideal physics.

### Accepting the dilemma

Some philosophers draw the dilemma's sceptical conclusion. Crane and Mellor
([1990](../sources/crane-mellor-1990.md)) argue that physics provides no principled boundary that would
exclude the mental, and hence that there is no real question whether physicalism is true. Chomsky
([2000](../sources/chomsky-2000.md)) argues on historical grounds that the concept of body has had no fixed
content since Newton, so the traditional mind–body problem cannot even be formulated, and Montero
([1999](../sources/montero-1999.md)) recasts the mind–body problem as a "body problem" about the physical
side of the contrast.

### Significance

The dilemma shows that physicalism's content is hostage to the concept of the physical, and so it
bears on every argument that uses that concept. The [causal-argument-for-physicalism](causal-argument-for-physicalism.md)
relies on the [completeness-of-physics](../concepts/completeness-of-physics.md), and the dilemma asks of that premise the same
question it asks of physicalism itself: complete by the lights of which physics? The dilemma also
connects with the observation that physics may describe only the dispositional structure of things,
which [russellian-monism](../positions/russellian-monism.md) exploits. If physics leaves out the intrinsic nature of matter,
then even a completed physics may not settle whether that nature is mental.

### In plain terms

Physicalism says "everything is physical". Hempel asked a simple question: physical according to
what? If you mean according to today's physics, the claim is almost certainly false, because today's
physics is unfinished and will be revised. If you mean according to the physics of the future, the
claim is empty, because nobody knows what that physics will say; it might even end up including
minds as basic ingredients. Defenders of physicalism have answered in several ways: by saying we
should simply bet on today's physics, by adding the rule that whatever future physics contains, it
will contain nothing basically mental, or by arguing that we understand "physical" well enough
without a precise definition. Others conclude that physicalism never had a clear meaning to begin
with.
