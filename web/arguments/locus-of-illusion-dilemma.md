The **locus-of-illusion dilemma** asks where the illusion that illusionism posits is located. If
phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, someone must be under it. A dog in pain, an infant, or an
adult enjoying a sunset without ever wondering about the nature of consciousness, makes no judgment
that redness is intrinsic or non-physical. So what, for them, is the illusion an illusion *of*?

**Inference form:** deductive, as a dilemma ([dilemma pattern](../../patterns/dilemma.md)).

1. Either the illusion is *pre-theoretical*, present in ordinary experience before any reflection,
   or it is *theoretical*, present only in those who theorize about consciousness.
2. *Pre-theoretical horn.* Then the illusionist must say what false content ordinary experience
   carries. Keith Frankish's answer is that introspection represents perceptual states as having
   simple, intrinsic qualities ([Frankish, 2016](../sources/frankish-2016.md)). Whether that
   representation is false, rather than an accurate presentation of how things seem, is exactly what
   the realist denies ([no-appearance-reality-gap](no-appearance-reality-gap.md)).
3. *Theoretical horn.* Then the illusion is a philosophers' error about consciousness, not a feature
   of consciousness. There is evidence that ordinary people do not share the philosophers' concept of
   phenomenal properties ([folk-lack-phenomenal-concept](../claims/folk-lack-phenomenal-concept.md)). Illusionism then shrinks to the
   claim that one philosophical theory of experience is false, which many physicalists accept without
   calling themselves illusionists.
4. ∴ Illusionism is either committed to a contested falsity claim about ordinary experience, or
   reduced to a thesis about philosophical theories.

A pointed form of the second horn turns illusionism's method against it. Illusionists study the
illusion through the judgments of people debating consciousness, who are introspecting as they do
so. That the illusion seems to involve an inner observer may reflect the activity of the people
studying it rather than ordinary experience.

### Weakest premise

The weak point is the second horn's assumption that a false representation must amount to a false
*judgment*. Frankish can hold that the misrepresentation is built into introspective processing and
needs no theoretical concept, so that the folk have the illusion without the philosophers' vocabulary
for it. That reply escapes the theoretical horn but must then answer the pre-theoretical one. The
empirical premise is also contested, since the folk-concept results depend on how the questions were
put.

### In plain terms

Illusionists say we are all fooled into thinking experience has a special inner glow. But a dog, a
baby, or someone simply enjoying a sunset isn't thinking anything about inner glows. So who exactly is
fooled? If everyone is, the illusionist owes an account of what false message ordinary experience
carries. If only philosophers are, then illusionism is just the claim that some philosophers have a
mistaken theory, which is far less dramatic than "consciousness is an illusion".
