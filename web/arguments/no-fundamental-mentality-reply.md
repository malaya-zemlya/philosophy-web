The **no-fundamental-mentality reply** answers the second horn of [hempels-dilemma](hempels-dilemma.md). The
dilemma says that a physicalism defined by future physics is empty, because no one knows what future
physics will contain. The reply grants the ignorance but denies the emptiness: whatever future
physics contains, physicalism can require that none of its fundamental entities or properties be
mental. That single constraint, stated now, gives the thesis a definite content that any later
physics can confirm or refute.

The idea has two main versions. On the *via negativa* of Montero and Papineau
([2005](../sources/montero-papineau-2005.md)), the constraint does all the work: what physicalists care
about is that mentality is not part of the ground floor of reality, and nothing more positive is
needed. Wilson ([2006](../sources/wilson-2006.md)) combines the constraint with a positive account: an
entity is physical if it is treated by current or future physics *and* is not fundamentally mental.
Spurrett and Papineau ([1999](../sources/spurrett-papineau-1999.md)) apply the same strategy to the
completeness premise of the [causal-argument-for-physicalism](causal-argument-for-physicalism.md), which then says only that
physical effects have sufficient causes that are not distinctively mental.

### The argument

The reply is deductive:

1. Physicalism can be defined as the thesis that everything is fixed by the entities and properties
   of current or future physics, none of which is fundamentally mental.
2. The constraint "none of which is fundamentally mental" is understood now, whatever future physics
   contains.
3. A thesis with a constraint that is understood now, and that some possible future physics would
   violate, has determinate content.
4. So physicalism defined in this way is not empty, and the second horn of the dilemma fails.

Take the dilemma's own test case, a future physics that adds a fundamental field whose values are
felt experiences. On the unconstrained definition that field counts as physical and physicalism
survives by fiat. On the constrained definition the field is fundamentally mental, so physicalism is
refuted, which is the verdict most physicalists and dualists would give.

**Weakest premise.** Premise 1, that excluding fundamental mentality is the right way to define the
physical. Critics argue that it settles by definition questions that should be left open. It rules
out a world whose basic properties are at once mental and physical, and it says nothing about
non-mental rivals to physicalism such as vitalism, whose vital force is neither mental nor physical.
Stoljar ([SEP](../sources/sep-physicalism.md)) suggests that the constraint is better treated as a
condition any account of the physical must respect, namely that some uninstantiated mental
properties are non-physical, than as a definition of the physical. The reply also leans on the notion
of what is *fundamentally* mental, which needs its own account.

### In plain terms

Hempel's dilemma says that "everything is physical" means nothing if "physical" refers to a future
physics nobody has seen yet ([hempels-dilemma](hempels-dilemma.md)). This reply says we can still give the
claim teeth by adding one rule now: whatever the physics of the future turns out to say, the basic
building blocks it describes must not be minds or experiences. If future scientists ever had to add
a basic "experience field" to explain the brain, physicalism would have been proved wrong. Critics
answer that the rule is too blunt, because it decides some hard questions by definition rather than
by argument.
