Talk of experience can be passed on. A being without experience that grew up among conscious
speakers could learn to use their words for dreams, sensations and inner seeing as fluently as they
do, including in philosophical argument, so its fluency would show nothing about whether it is
conscious ([moody-1994](../sources/moody-1994.md)). What would count is whether such concepts could arise in a
community that never had them, which is the question in
[zombie-community-cannot-originate-consciousness-concepts](zombie-community-cannot-originate-consciousness-concepts.md).

The claim applies directly to language models, which learn their talk of experience from human
text. It is close to the rival explanation recorded in [llm-mimicry-rival](llm-mimicry-rival.md), on which a
model's mind-like talk is predicted by its training on human mind-talk. The present claim is
narrower: it concerns concepts of experience specifically, and says only that inherited fluency is
not evidence, not that it is likely to be mere imitation.

### In plain terms

If you learned to talk about feelings by copying people who have them, your talk would not show that
you have them too. That is the situation of an AI trained on human writing.
