When "physical" is defined by reference to physics, the thesis that everything is physical comes out
either false or empty. If the reference is to current physics it is false, because current physics is
incomplete. If the reference is to some future or ideal physics it lacks determinate content, because
no one can say what that physics will contain. This is the conclusion of [hempels-dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md)
(Hempel, [1980](../sources/hempel-1980.md)).

**Scope.** The claim targets the *formulation* of [physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md), not its truth. It says
nothing about whether minds are brains; it says that a certain way of stating the view leaves it
either refuted or contentless. A physicalist can accept the claim and look for another formulation,
for instance one on which the physical is whatever is not fundamentally mental.

**Who denies it.** Defenders of a current-physics formulation (Lewis, [1994](../sources/lewis-1994.md);
Melnyk, [1997](../sources/melnyk-1997.md)) deny that falsity is the right verdict on the first horn, and
defenders of a constrained future-physics formulation (Wilson, [2006](../sources/wilson-2006.md)) deny that
emptiness follows on the second. Crane and Mellor ([1990](../sources/crane-mellor-1990.md)) accept it and
conclude that there is no real question of physicalism.

Distinct from [mental-is-physical](mental-is-physical.md), which states physicalism about the mind; this claim
concerns whether any such statement has a definite and possibly true content.
