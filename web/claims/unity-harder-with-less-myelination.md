*Myelin* is the fatty sheath around nerve fibres that makes signals travel much faster
([waxman-1980](../sources/waxman-1980.md)). If unity of experience is an engineered synchronisation across a slow
network, then a brain with less myelin, and so slower long-range signals, should find unity harder to
achieve and keep. This is a prediction, not an established result.

### Where it applies

The obvious cases are three. Children and adolescents have incompletely myelinated brains, since white
matter goes on maturing into early adulthood, with frontal and temporal connections maturing last
([lebel-2008](../sources/lebel-2008.md)). Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis slow conduction. White
matter also declines in old age.

### Evidence and its ambiguity

There is suggestive evidence. Children of 10 and 11 bind sights and sounds across wider gaps in time
than adults do, and this *temporal binding window* goes on narrowing beyond the first decade of life
([hillock-2011](../sources/hillock-2011.md)). That fits a system that cannot resolve timing finely. It cuts both ways,
however: a wider window makes binding coarser and more permissive, which is not obviously less unity.
The prediction is cleanest stated as: a less myelinated brain must either integrate more coarsely or
integrate less, and which it does is an empirical question.

### In plain terms

Nerve signals travel faster along fibres wrapped in myelin. Young brains are still adding myelin until
the early twenties. If keeping experience unified means keeping slow signals in sync, a young or
demyelinated brain should find that harder, and there are hints that children's brains do sync sights
and sounds more loosely than adults'.
