The unity thesis holds that a conscious subject's simultaneous experiences can never fall apart. At
any moment, whatever a subject experiences is experienced *together*, as parts of a single total
experience that subsumes them all. The formulation is due to Tim Bayne and David Chalmers
([bayne-chalmers-2003](../sources/bayne-chalmers-2003.md)), and Bayne defends it at book length ([bayne-2010](../sources/bayne-2010.md)).

### Scope

The thesis concerns *phenomenal* unity, the relation of being experienced together, and only
*synchronic* unity, the unity of experiences at one time. It does not claim that a subject's
contents are always integrated for reasoning and report (access unity), that perceived features are
always bound into objects, or that the stream of experience is always continuous over time. It is a
necessity claim: not merely that ordinary experience is unified, but that it could not fail to be.

### Who would deny it

Those who read the split-brain cases as two streams of consciousness in one person deny it, as do
defenders of *partial unity*, on which some experiences are co-conscious with others that are not
co-conscious with each other. Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model denies that there is a fact of
the matter of the kind the thesis asserts ([dennett-kinsbourne-1992](../sources/dennett-kinsbourne-1992.md)). The thesis is also
challenged by [distributed-brain-unity](../arguments/distributed-brain-unity.md), on which unity is a costly achievement of a
well-functioning brain and so cannot hold of necessity.

### In plain terms

The unity thesis says that everything you experience at one moment is always experienced as one
whole, never as two or more separate experiences side by side in the same head. It claims this is
not just usually true but has to be true.
