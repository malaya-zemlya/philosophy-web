When a learner that already knows everything sayable about an item is nonetheless surprised on first
meeting it, the surprise is neither an error nor a sign that its knowledge was incomplete. It is an
accurate registration of something real: the learner's own representation of the item did not yet
carry the item's role, and the encounter is what makes it do so ([first-encounter-adds-binding](first-encounter-adds-binding.md)).
Surprise measures the distance between the learner's representations and its input, not the distance
between its knowledge and the facts.

The claim answers the challenge George Graham and Terence Horgan set for materialists, "to give a
satisfying, phenomenology-respecting, account of why it is rationally appropriate" for a fully
informed Mary to find her first colour experiences surprising, without treating her surprise as an
illusion ([graham-horgan-2000](../sources/graham-horgan-2000.md), pp. 76–77). It denies the inference Frank Jackson draws from
Mary's not saying "ho, hum" to her having lacked knowledge ([jackson-1986](../sources/jackson-1986.md), p. 291), and also
Daniel Dennett's converse inference from her complete knowledge to her not being surprised
([dennett-2005](../sources/dennett-2005.md)). It is reached in [llmary-predicted-surprise](../arguments/llmary-predicted-surprise.md).

### In plain terms

Someone who knows everything about a thing can still be surprised the first time they meet it, and
that surprise is not a mistake. It correctly signals that their mind had not yet been set up to deal
with the thing itself, only to know about it. Being surprised shows that the mind needed adjusting,
not that some fact was missing.
