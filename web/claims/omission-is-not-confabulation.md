A report of how one reached a judgment can leave out processing that contributed to it without being
false. It becomes a [confabulation](../concepts/confabulation.md) only when it positively misdescribes that processing,
for instance by citing a cause that did not operate or by presenting a derivation that did not in fact
produce the result. The mathematician who says that a solution "came to him" while boarding a bus,
after the problem had gone unattended for days, omits the unconscious work that produced it but says
nothing false about it ([poincare-1908](../sources/poincare-1908.md); see [incubation](../concepts/incubation.md)). The shopper who credits a choice of stockings to their
quality, when position was what swayed the choice, confabulates ([nisbett-wilson-1977](../sources/nisbett-wilson-1977.md)).

The claim is a point about how faithfulness should be measured, not a defence of any particular
report. It follows that completeness is the wrong test of a self-report. Any system in which most
processing is unavailable for report, as global workspace theory holds of human minds
([global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md)), will produce incomplete reports as a matter of course. The right
question is whether the report misrepresents the processing it does describe. Applied to the
reasoning traces of language models, the distinction separates a trace that is silent about a factor
that influenced the answer from one that supplies a substitute rationale. Experiments in which models
use a planted hint without acknowledging it ([chen-2025-cot](../sources/chen-2025-cot.md)) can involve either, and the two
call for different verdicts about the system's self-knowledge.

The claim would be resisted by those who hold that any report presented as a full account of one's
reasoning implicitly asserts its own completeness, so that significant omission is itself a kind of
misrepresentation. On that view the distinction drawn here matters only for reports that do not
purport to be complete.
