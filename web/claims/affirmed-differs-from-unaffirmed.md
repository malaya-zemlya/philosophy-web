If one can be certain of the existence of one thing while being entirely unaware of, or unable to
affirm, another, then the first is not the same thing as the second. Avicenna states it in the course of
the floating man passage: what the man affirms (his self) is other than what he does not affirm (his
body and its parts) [marmura-1986](../sources/marmura-1986.md). The principle is what turns the
floating man's self-awareness into a conclusion about the nature of the self.

The principle is widely regarded as the weak point of the argument. Taken generally it is invalid, a
version of what later logicians call the *masked man fallacy*: one may know that one's father is in the
room while not knowing that the masked man is, though the masked man is one's father. Knowledge and
affirmation are sensitive to how a thing is presented, so failing to affirm something under one
description does not show that it is a different thing. Defenders reply that Avicenna's principle
concerns what enters into the very concept of the self, not merely what one happens to know.
