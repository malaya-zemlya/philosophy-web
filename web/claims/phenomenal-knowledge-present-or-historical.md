Anyone who holds that Mary the colour scientist gains phenomenal knowledge on first seeing red faces a
choice. Either that knowledge is carried by the lasting change in her brain, in which case anyone with
the same brain has it, whether or not they ever saw red; or it is not fixed by her present state at
all, but by the fact that a certain experience once occurred, so that two people alike in every lasting
respect can differ in what they know.

The first option gives up the knowledge argument's premise that the knowledge can be had only through
the experience. The second makes knowing what red is like resemble the genuineness of a banknote, which
depends on where the note came from and cannot be detected in the note itself. Neither knower could
then tell from the inside whether they had the knowledge, and it would make no difference to anything
either of them later thought, said or did. The claim is reached in [retention-dilemma](../arguments/retention-dilemma.md).

### In plain terms

If Mary learns something special when she first sees red, then either that learning is stored in her
brain, so that anyone with an identical brain knows it too, or it is not stored in her at all and
depends only on the fact that she once had the experience. The first undercuts the famous argument;
the second makes the knowledge something she could never tell she had.
