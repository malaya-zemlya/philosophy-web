In ordinary but degraded states, such as severe fatigue, anxiety or attention-deficit/hyperactivity
disorder (ADHD), experience can present not as a single field but as several sub-processes, like
somewhat interacting subagents, with no orchestrator pulling them together. On this report, the
contents are not all present at once as parts of one scattered whole. They seem to form a whole
only in retrospect, and bringing them into a unified state takes effort.

### Scope

The claim is a phenomenological report about how such states present to the subject. It does not
assert, by itself, that two experiences occur at the same moment without being experienced together.
The report fits two structures: rapid switching between sub-processes, each of which has the floor in
turn (synchronic unity preserved, diachronic unity lost), or genuine co-occurrence without
togetherness (a breach of the [unity-thesis](unity-thesis.md)).

The phrase "only in retrospect" carries a methodological point. The unity theorist's main evidence is
introspection: attend to your experience, and everything is co-present. But attending is itself an
act of integration, so unity found on inspection may be produced by the inspection. The same applies
to this report, which is also made later, from a more orchestrated state. Neither side's
introspection is neutral, which may leave the question undecidable from the first person in exactly
the states where it matters most.

It is distinct from the clinical cases usually tested against the unity thesis, such as the split
brain or dissociative identity disorder, because it concerns common states of ordinary brains with no
lesion or surgery. It is distinct from [unity-dualizes-to-unity-of-agency](unity-dualizes-to-unity-of-agency.md), which concerns
the structure of the problem of unity rather than a report of its failure.

### Who would deny it

Defenders of the unity thesis, notably Tim Bayne ([bayne-2010](../sources/bayne-2010.md)), would accept the report
but redescribe it as a loss of access unity, attentional control or continuity over time, with each
moment's experience still one, however disordered.

### In plain terms

When you are very tired, anxious, or have severe ADHD, your mind may not feel like one unified scene.
It can feel more like several separate processes running and bumping into each other, with no one in
charge. It may only *seem* unified when you look back, and pulling it together takes effort.
