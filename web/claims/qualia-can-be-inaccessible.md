Having an experience and being able to know about it are different things, and the first can occur
without the second. A subject may undergo a state that genuinely feels some way while that feel is not
available to introspection, memory or report. The subject has the quale but cannot get at it.

### Background

The idea follows from Ned Block's distinction between *phenomenal* consciousness, the felt character of
a state, and *access* consciousness, the availability of its content for reasoning, report and the
control of action ([block-1995](../sources/block-1995.md)). If the two are distinct, they can in principle come apart.
Block later argued that they actually do: phenomenal consciousness *overflows* access, so that we
experience more than we can report at any moment ([block-2007](../sources/block-2007.md)). His central evidence is George
Sperling's partial-report experiments, in which subjects briefly shown a grid of letters say they saw
all of them, yet can report only a few. When cued after the display, they can report any row asked for
([sperling-1960](../sources/sperling-1960.md)). On Block's reading, all the letters were experienced, but only some could be
accessed.

### Scope

The claim says only that some qualia *can* be inaccessible to their owner. It does not say that this
is common, that all qualia are inaccessible, or that the inaccessible ones can never be detected by
indirect means. It is distinct from [subject-can-be-wrong-about-own-qualia](subject-can-be-wrong-about-own-qualia.md), which concerns
access that delivers a *false* verdict. Here there is no verdict at all.
It is also distinct from [inside-vantage-access-limited](inside-vantage-access-limited.md), which says a system's internal vantage on
its own *processing* is poorer than an external one. That claim concerns facts about the mechanism; this
one concerns the qualitative character of experience itself. And it is distinct from
[qualities-exist-unexperienced](qualities-exist-unexperienced.md), Sam Coleman's thesis that qualities of the experiential kind can
exist without being experienced by any subject. An inaccessible quale *is* experienced; it is only not
accessed.

### What it opposes

The claim is odd from the standpoint of a long tradition, running back to Descartes, on which one's own
experience is the thing one knows best. Many arguments in the philosophy of mind take that privileged
access as given. Those arguments are collected, and the bearing of this claim on them set out, in
[fallible-access-undercuts-qualia-arguments](../arguments/fallible-access-undercuts-qualia-arguments.md).

The claim is also double-edged for the defender of qualia. It is the natural reply to
[qualia-relational-from-cases](../arguments/qualia-relational-from-cases.md): the intrinsic quality may be real but unavailable. Yet a quale
that made *no* difference to anything its owner or anyone else could ever observe would be an idle
residue, explaining nothing ([real-feel-residue-is-idle](../arguments/real-feel-residue-is-idle.md)). The usual defence against that charge, that the
feel is observed from the first person ([feel-is-the-watching-not-an-unwatched-difference](../arguments/feel-is-the-watching-not-an-unwatched-difference.md)), does
not help here, since an inaccessible quale is by hypothesis not observed even by its owner. Block's version
escapes the charge only insofar as the inaccessible experience still leaves indirect traces, such as the
cued reports in Sperling's experiments. Global workspace theorists ([global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md)) reject
the claim, reading those results as rich access to fragmentary contents.

### In plain terms

We usually assume that if you are experiencing something, you can tell what it is like. This claim says
that is not guaranteed. You might experience something without being able to notice, remember or
describe it. A classic example: shown a grid of letters for a split second, people feel they saw them
all but can name only a few.
