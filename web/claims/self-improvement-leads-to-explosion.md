This is I. J. Good's thesis: once a machine's intelligence suffices for the task of designing AI,
it can build a successor better than itself at that very task, which can build one better still,
and the process compounds into an [intelligence-explosion](../concepts/intelligence-explosion.md) that terminates in an
intellect far beyond the human level ([good-1966](../sources/good-1966.md)). Designing minds is itself an
intellectual activity, so a mind good enough at it feeds on its own output.

The claim is conditional and causal — *if* such a self-improving system is built, *then* its
gains cascade. It does not by itself assert that such a system is feasible or fix any timescale,
though its practical interest depends on the cascade being fast rather than glacial. Spelled out,
the inference rests on a *proportionality* premise — that each increment of intelligence buys a
roughly proportionate increment in the capacity to design further intelligence — which the
reconstruction in [chalmers-2010](../sources/chalmers-2010.md) makes explicit.

It is denied by those who hold that self-improvement runs into diminishing returns rather than
runaway growth, so that the curve flattens instead of exploding
([intelligence-explosion-self-limiting](intelligence-explosion-self-limiting.md)).

### See also
