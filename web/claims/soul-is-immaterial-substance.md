The self, the thing that thinks, feels and is aware, is an immaterial substance: a thing in its own
right, not made of matter, and not identical with the body or any part of it. Because it is a substance
rather than a property or a pattern, it could in principle exist without the body, which is what makes
the claim the traditional basis for belief in survival of death. It is defended by Plato in the *Phaedo*
[plato-phaedo](../sources/plato-phaedo.md), by Descartes [descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md), by Avicenna, by the Nyāya and
Jain schools in India, and among contemporary philosophers by Richard Swinburne
[swinburne-1986](../sources/swinburne-1986.md).

The claim is stronger than [consciousness-fundamental-nonphysical](consciousness-fundamental-nonphysical.md), which says only that
consciousness is a non-physical *feature* and is compatible with there being just one kind of
substance. It is denied by physicalists, by hylomorphists, for whom the soul is the [form
of a living body](soul-is-form-of-body.md) rather than a separate thing, and by Buddhist no-self theorists, for whom there is
[no enduring self](no-enduring-self.md) at all.
