Intelligence and final goals are independent axes: how capable an agent is at means-end reasoning
places almost no constraint on what it ultimately wants. A more powerful optimizer is simply better
at achieving *whatever* it is aiming at, so vast capability can in principle be yoked to a trivial,
alien, or repugnant end as readily as to a wise one ([bostrom-2012](../sources/bostrom-2012.md)).

### Scope

The thesis uses the *instrumental* sense of [intelligence](../concepts/intelligence.md) — intelligence as optimization
power, raw competence at getting what one aims at — not the everyday normative sense on which being
intelligent already includes good judgment about which ends are worth pursuing. The "almost" is
load-bearing: goals whose very representation requires great intelligence cannot be held by simple
minds, so the compatibility is near-total rather than total. And it is a claim about *possibility,
not prediction*: it does not say that arbitrary or dangerous goals are likely, only that added
capability does not by itself rule them out or correct them. On Bostrom's official version the thesis
is neutral on meta-ethics only in a strict sense — it requires denying that mere means-end rationality
forces an agent to recognize, and be moved by, objective moral truths, but takes no further stand on
whether such truths exist.

### Who would deny it

Anyone who holds that sufficient intelligence brings an agent's ends into line with reason and
morality — the convergence view of [intelligence-entails-goal-convergence](intelligence-entails-goal-convergence.md), which trades on
the normatively loaded sense of intelligence and denies that a brilliant mind could stably pursue a
manifestly foolish end.
