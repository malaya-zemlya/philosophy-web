No law of physics and no principled barrier prevents an engineered system from greatly exceeding
the best human cognitive performance across virtually all domains of interest — the level of
capability [superintelligence](../concepts/superintelligence.md) names. The usual ground is that the brain is itself a
physical machine, so nothing in principle forbids building, simulating, or re-engineering a
system that does what it does and more ([chalmers-2010](../sources/chalmers-2010.md)); the notion is developed at
book length by [bostrom-2014](../sources/bostrom-2014.md).

The claim is modal and modest. It asserts *possibility*, not imminence: it says nothing about
when, how, by whom, or even whether such a system will actually be built, and nothing about
takeoff speed. Nor does it presuppose that intelligence is a single scalar — it needs only that
each of the relevant performances can be separately exceeded, not that they collapse onto one
axis. It is therefore the narrowest of the three sub-issues bundled into
[superintelligence-feasibility](../questions/superintelligence-feasibility.md), the bare physical-possibility question.

It is denied from two directions. Some hold that cognition is essentially biological, so that no
silicon system could match the full range of human thought. Others deny the claim is even
well-posed: on the multidimensional view of intelligence ([intelligence-not-one-dimensional](intelligence-not-one-dimensional.md)),
"exceeding humans across the board" has no determinate referent, so there is nothing here to be
possible or impossible.
