Unlike a passing experience, knowing what an experience is like outlasts the experience. Someone who
has learned what red looks like can afterwards recognise red, imagine it, compare it with other
colours and say what it is like, long after the first sight of red is over. Whatever that knowledge
consists in, it must therefore be carried by something that persists in the knower between the
experience and its later use.

That persisting thing is a competence, not a memory of the episode. David Lewis notes that "even if you
eventually forget the occasion itself, you will very likely retain your ability to imagine such an
experience" ([lewis-1988](../sources/lewis-1988.md)), and Daniel Dennett asks, of the same point, "you could forget your
first color experiences and still have phenomenal concepts, couldn't you?" ([dennett-2005](../sources/dennett-2005.md)).
An episodic memory of first seeing red is a further item a knower may have or lack, and the knowledge
of what red is like does not depend on it.

The claim is neutral on what the knowledge is about. A phenomenal realist can hold that the competence
is knowledge *of* a non-physical quality; the claim says only that the knowing itself must be retained,
and so needs a lasting vehicle.

### In plain terms

Once you know what red looks like, you keep knowing it. You can recognise red later, picture it and
describe it, even if you have forgotten the day you first saw it. So that knowledge has to be stored
somewhere in you, as a lasting skill rather than as a memory of the first time.
