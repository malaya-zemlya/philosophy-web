On this answer to the [knowledge-argument](../concepts/knowledge-argument.md), what Mary gains when she first sees red is the
placement of a hitherto unencountered state within her own web of expectations. She learns in what
context the experience of red occurs: which states of her visual system, and which situations in the
world, bring it on. She also learns what follows once the experience is in play: what it disposes her
to expect, recall, imagine, say and do next. The gain is wholly relational, and nothing about red
taken by itself is added.

### Scope

The claim is about the *structure* of Mary's gain. It explains why no textbook could supply it: the
placement consists in the state actually occurring and her own system adjusting around it, which no
description of the state can bring about. It stays neutral on whether this relational gain is best
called knowledge-that or know-how.

It is distinct from [mary-gains-abilities-not-facts](mary-gains-abilities-not-facts.md), although close to it. Lewis's abilities
to recognise, imagine and remember are dispositions of just this context-and-consequence kind
([lewis-1988](../sources/lewis-1988.md)), but the ability hypothesis commits to calling the gain non-factual know-how,
which this claim does not. It is also distinct from [mary-gains-old-fact-new-guise](mary-gains-old-fact-new-guise.md), which
locates the novelty in a new way of grasping an old fact. This claim locates it in the new relations
the state enters into. Finally, it is distinct from [mary-gains-self-locating-knowledge](mary-gains-self-locating-knowledge.md),
Perry's view that Mary learns indexical facts of the form "*this* is what seeing red does to *me*"
([perry-2001](../sources/perry-2001.md)). The two overlap, since a placement in one's own expectations is about
oneself, but the self-locating reply turns on the first-person *form* of the new knowledge. This claim
turns on its *content*, which is the experience's typical antecedents and consequents.

### Who would deny it

The phenomenal realist denies it: relational placement leaves out the intrinsic character of red, what
the experience is like in itself, and that is precisely what Mary was supposed to lack
([mary-gains-acquaintance](mary-gains-acquaintance.md)). Defenders of the original knowledge argument also deny it,
holding that she gains a new fact ([some-truths-about-experience-not-physically-deducible](some-truths-about-experience-not-physically-deducible.md)).

### In plain terms

When Mary finally sees red, what does she get? On this view, two things: she finds out what situations
bring on the experience of red, and what that experience leads her to expect, remember or do next.
That is the whole of it. Her "knowing red" is entirely a matter of how the experience fits in with
everything else going on in her mind. Critics say this misses the most important part, which is simply
*what red looks like*.
