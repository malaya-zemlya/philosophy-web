Qualities of the kind we meet in experience, such as the redness in a visual experience of red or the
sharpness in a sharp pain, can exist without anyone being aware of them. Experience, on this view, is not
what makes a quality the quality it is; it is a relation a subject can stand in to a quality that would
have existed anyway, so that a quality is only *contingently* experienced. The claim is the core of
panqualityism, whose name Herbert Feigl introduced [feigl-1960](../sources/feigl-1960.md), and is defended today chiefly by
Sam Coleman [coleman-2015](../sources/coleman-2015.md), [coleman-2016](../sources/coleman-2016.md).

The claim is distinct from [consciousness-fundamental-ubiquitous](consciousness-fundamental-ubiquitous.md), which places experience
itself, including a subject's point of view, at the fundamental level; this claim places there only the
qualities, without subjects. It is denied by those who hold that a quality of experience is essentially
a way of being experienced, so that an unexperienced pain or redness is a contradiction, and by David
Chalmers's objection that no arrangement of qualities could by itself necessitate awareness of them
([qualities-do-not-necessitate-awareness](qualities-do-not-necessitate-awareness.md)).
