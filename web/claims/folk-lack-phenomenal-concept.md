Justin Sytsma and Edouard Machery report experiments in which non-philosophers, unlike philosophers,
did not group mental states as subjective experiences by phenomenal quality, but by valence: they
were willing to say that a simple robot sees red, yet not that it feels pain
([Sytsma & Machery, 2010](../sources/sytsma-machery-2010.md)). They conclude that the philosophical concept
of phenomenal consciousness is not the folk one.

The interpretation is disputed. David Chalmers replies that the result was predicted by the observation
that ordinary mental terms have both a functional and a phenomenal reading, with sensation terms such as
"pain" more apt to suggest the phenomenal one than perception terms such as "see". On his view most
people have concepts of specific phenomenal states, such as feeling pain or experiencing colour, even if
it is open whether they have a unifying concept of phenomenal consciousness
([Chalmers, 2018](../sources/chalmers-2018.md), p. 13, n. 7). The claim should therefore be read narrowly: the
evidence bears on a *unified* folk concept, not on whether ordinary people have any phenomenal concepts at
all. How widely the intuitions behind the hard problem are shared is the empirical side of the
[meta-problem-of-consciousness](../concepts/meta-problem-of-consciousness.md).

The claim bears on illusionism because, if it is true, the illusion that phenomenal properties exist
may be present mainly in those who theorize about consciousness
([locus-of-illusion-dilemma](../arguments/locus-of-illusion-dilemma.md)).
