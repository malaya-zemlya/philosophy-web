The contents of conscious experience come only from intermediate levels of the processing hierarchy in
each modality. They do not come from the most peripheral, sensory stage, which registers the raw signal,
and they do not come from the most central, conceptual stage, which represents meaning and category.
In hearing speech, we are aware of phonological structure (the sounds of words, segmented and
stressed) but neither of the acoustic waveform nor of syntax and meaning as such. In vision, we are aware
of surfaces as laid out from our point of view, roughly Marr's 2½-D sketch, but neither of the
retinal image's edges and blobs nor of the viewpoint-free object models used for recognition. The claim
is Ray Jackendoff's [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md) and is the core commitment of
[intermediate-level-theory](../positions/intermediate-level-theory.md).

The claim is denied by defenders of *cognitive phenomenology*, who hold that understanding and thinking
have experiential character of their own, and by defenders of rich perceptual content, who hold that we
can visually experience high-level properties such as being a pine tree. It is distinct from
[consciousness-is-global-broadcast](consciousness-is-global-broadcast.md), which says what makes a content conscious (its being made
widely available), whereas this claim says which level of representation conscious contents are drawn
from.
