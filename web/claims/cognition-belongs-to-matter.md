The processes we ordinarily call mental, such as judging, thinking, sensing and the sense of being an
"I," are transformations of matter (*prakṛti*), not of consciousness. Consciousness itself (*puruṣa*) is
a pure, inactive witness that does nothing and has no contents of its own; it merely illuminates the
activity of a material intellect (*buddhi*), ego-sense (*ahaṃkāra*) and mind (*manas*). The claim belongs
to the Sāṃkhya school, whose classical statement is Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhyakārikā*
[isvarakrsna-samkhyakarika](../sources/isvarakrsna-samkhyakarika.md).

The claim draws the line between mind and matter in an unusual place. Cartesian dualism puts thought on
the side of the immaterial mind, so that the [immaterial self](soul-is-immaterial-substance.md) is
above all a thinking thing. Sāṃkhya puts thought on the side of matter and leaves only bare awareness
outside it, so that a functional or computational account of cognition could be complete without
touching consciousness. It is denied by Cartesians and by most physicalists, and is close in spirit to
[Jackendoff's view](consciousness-is-intermediate-level.md) that thought itself is never conscious.
