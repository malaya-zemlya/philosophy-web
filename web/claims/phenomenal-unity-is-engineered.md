The unity of a subject's experience at a moment is not something a brain has automatically. It is
the output of integrating work that has to be done, costs resources and time, and can be done well or
badly. It therefore holds only while the brain is functioning well. Under fatigue, stress, disorder
or damage, unity should degrade, and it should be expected to come in degrees.

### Scope

The claim concerns *phenomenal* unity, the relation of being experienced together, not merely the
integration of information for report and action. Its most direct consequence is that the
[unity-thesis](unity-thesis.md) is false: a unity that depends on good functioning cannot hold of necessity.
It is compatible with unity being the normal state of a healthy waking brain.

It is distinct from [subject-unity-graded-by-integration](subject-unity-graded-by-integration.md), which concerns how *many subjects*
a system contains and treats integration bandwidth as fixing the count. This claim concerns the unity
of a *single* subject's experience and adds that the integration is an effortful, failure-prone
process rather than a standing structural fact.

### Who would deny it

Defenders of the unity thesis deny it. So would a global workspace theorist who holds that a content is
conscious only once it has been integrated, since on that view whatever is conscious is unified by
definition, even if the integrating is costly. Kant would accept the part about work: for him the
unity of consciousness is the product of an act of synthesis, though he took that act to be a
condition of any experience at all rather than an achievement that can partly fail.

### In plain terms

Your experience feeling like one whole is not free. The brain has to work to pull everything together,
so it only manages this fully when it is working well. When you are exhausted, stressed or unwell, the
unity can fray.
