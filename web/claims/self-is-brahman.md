The true self (*ātman*) of each person is not an individual thing at all but is identical with
*Brahman*, the single, partless, universal consciousness that is the ground of all reality. The sense of
being one bounded individual among many, with this body and these thoughts, comes from ignorance
(*avidyā*), which superimposes the features of body and mind onto pure consciousness. This is the central
thesis of Śaṅkara's Advaita ("non-dual") Vedānta, expressed in the Upaniṣadic sayings "that thou art"
(*tat tvam asi*) and "I am Brahman" (*ahaṃ brahmāsmi*) [shankara-brahmasutra](../sources/shankara-brahmasutra.md),
[sep-shankara](../sources/sep-shankara.md).

The claim is distinct from [cosmos-is-fundamental-subject](cosmos-is-fundamental-subject.md): the cosmopsychist's cosmos is a
subject with its own point of view, whereas Advaita's Brahman is pure, witnessing consciousness without
a perspective or contents of its own. It is denied by the dualist schools of Vedānta, which hold that
individual souls are eternally distinct from God, by Sāṃkhya, which holds that there are many distinct
centres of consciousness, and by Buddhist no-self theorists, who deny any enduring self, individual or
universal.
