The brain processes information in many places at once, across billions of neurons and specialised
regions, with no central clock. Its signals travel along axons at speeds ranging from under 1 m/s in
thin unmyelinated fibres to tens of metres per second in thick myelinated ones. Conduction velocity
rises with fibre diameter and myelin thickness ([waxman-1980](../sources/waxman-1980.md)). Crossing the cortex,
including between the hemispheres, can therefore take tens of milliseconds, and the senses are
processed at different speeds. Those delays are the same order of magnitude as the intervals within
which events are experienced as simultaneous.

### Scope

This is an empirical claim about neural architecture. It says nothing by itself about consciousness;
its philosophical use is as a premise in [distributed-brain-unity](../arguments/distributed-brain-unity.md).

### In plain terms

The brain is not like a computer chip with one clock ticking for everything. It is millions of
processes running at once, passing messages that take a noticeable fraction of a second to get across
the brain, about as long as the moments we experience as "now".
