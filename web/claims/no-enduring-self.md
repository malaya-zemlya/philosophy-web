There is no enduring self, no permanent owner or controller, over and above the changing stream of
bodily and mental events that make up a life. What we call a person is a causally connected series of
physical forms, feelings, perceptions, volitional formations and moments of consciousness, the five
*aggregates* (*skandhas*) of Buddhist analysis, and "I" is a convenient designation for that series
rather than the name of a further thing [sep-mind-indian-buddhism](../sources/sep-mind-indian-buddhism.md). This is the Buddhist
doctrine of *anattā* (Pali; Sanskrit *anātman*), "not-self," and has close Western parallels in Hume's
bundle theory [hume-treatise](../sources/hume-treatise.md) and Parfit's reductionism [parfit-1984](../sources/parfit-1984.md).

The claim is denied by anyone who holds that the self is a substance, whether immaterial
([soul-is-immaterial-substance](soul-is-immaterial-substance.md)) or, as in Advaita Vedānta, identical with universal
consciousness ([self-is-brahman](self-is-brahman.md)), and by those who hold that personal identity is a
[further fact](identity-is-further-fact.md) not reducible to the continuity of a series. It is
distinct from [identity-is-psychological-continuity](identity-is-psychological-continuity.md), which says what personal identity
*consists in*; the no-self claim says that there is no self for identity to be the identity *of*,
beyond the series itself.
