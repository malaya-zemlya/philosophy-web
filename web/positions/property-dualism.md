*Property dualism* is the view that there is only one kind of substance, the physical stuff of
bodies and brains, but two fundamentally different kinds of property. Besides the physical
properties that physics describes, some physical things, such as working brains, also have
non-physical mental properties ([consciousness-fundamental-nonphysical](../claims/consciousness-fundamental-nonphysical.md)). Usually the
non-physical properties are taken to be the phenomenal ones, the felt qualities of experience: the
way red looks or pain hurts. A brain in pain is a single physical thing, but its painfulness is a
property over and above its physical properties.

### How it differs from neighbouring views

Unlike [substance-dualism](substance-dualism.md), property dualism posits no immaterial soul
([soul-is-immaterial-substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md) is rejected). The subject of experience is the brain or the
person, and the dualism lies only in its properties. Unlike [physicalism](physicalism.md), it denies that
the phenomenal properties are fixed by the physical ones with metaphysical necessity. In the
vocabulary of [supervenience](../concepts/supervenience.md), experience supervenes on the physical only by the laws of
nature, not in every possible world. David Chalmers calls his version *naturalistic dualism*
([chalmers-1996](../sources/chalmers-1996.md)). Phenomenal properties are fundamental, like mass and charge, and are tied
to physical properties by contingent *psychophysical laws*, which a complete science would state
alongside the laws of physics.

### Arguments for it

The main arguments for property dualism are the anti-physicalist thought experiments. If a
[philosophical-zombie](../concepts/philosophical-zombie.md), a physical duplicate of a person with no experience, is possible,
then the physical facts do not necessitate the phenomenal ones ([zombie-conceivability](../arguments/zombie-conceivability.md)).
Frank Jackson's [knowledge-argument](../arguments/knowledge-argument.md) asks us to imagine Mary, a scientist who knows every
physical fact about colour vision but has never seen colour, and concludes that she learns something
new on first seeing red, so that some truths about experience cannot be deduced from the physical
truths ([some-truths-about-experience-not-physically-deducible](../claims/some-truths-about-experience-not-physically-deducible.md), [jackson-1982](../sources/jackson-1982.md)).
Both arguments aim at the [metaphysical-gap](../concepts/metaphysical-gap.md), the thesis that the phenomenal facts are
something over and above the physical facts.

### The problem of mental causation

Property dualism avoids the interaction problem of an immaterial soul, but faces a version of it for
properties. If the physical world is causally closed, every bodily movement has a sufficient physical
cause, and a non-physical phenomenal property seems to have nothing left to cause. Kim's
[causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md) presses this point ([irreducible-mental-causally-excluded](../claims/irreducible-mental-causally-excluded.md)).
Property dualists have three main responses. Some, like Jackson in 1982, accept
[epiphenomenalism](epiphenomenalism.md), on which phenomenal properties are real but cause nothing. Some accept
that mental and physical causes systematically overdetermine their effects. Others, including
Chalmers in later work, move towards [russellian-monism](russellian-monism.md), on which phenomenal or
proto-phenomenal properties are the intrinsic nature of physical properties and so share in their
causal work.

Property dualism is sometimes stated as a form of [emergence](../concepts/emergence.md): experience is a strongly
emergent property of complex physical systems, produced by laws that could not be derived from
physics.

### In plain terms

Property dualism says there is only one kind of stuff in the world, physical stuff, but that some of
it has two kinds of features. A brain has physical features, such as its chemistry and electrical
activity, and also features that are not physical at all, such as how its experiences feel. On this
view there is no soul. The felt side of experience is an extra ingredient of nature attached to
certain brains by laws of its own. Its main difficulty is explaining how these non-physical features
could make any difference to what the body does.
