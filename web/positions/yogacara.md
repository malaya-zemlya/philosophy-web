*Yogācāra* ("practice of yoga") is a school of Mahāyāna Buddhist philosophy, founded in India in the
fourth century CE by the brothers Asaṅga and Vasubandhu, which holds that what we take to be an external
world of objects is nothing but the content of consciousness. Its slogan is *vijñaptimātra*,
"representation-only" or "consciousness-only": experience presents objects as if they existed outside
and independently of the mind, but there is nothing out there beyond the appearances themselves
[sep-yogacara](../sources/sep-yogacara.md). The school keeps the Buddhist denial of an enduring self, so its idealism is
unusual. Consciousness is real, but it is a stream of momentary events belonging to no one, not the mind
of a self or of a God.

The position commits to the claims that [reality is fundamentally
mental](../claims/reality-is-fundamentally-mental.md) and that [there is no enduring self](../claims/no-enduring-self.md). Scholars disagree about how
strong its idealism is. On the traditional reading it denies outright that material objects exist; on
some modern readings it is chiefly an epistemological or phenomenological thesis, that we have access only
to experience and should not speculate about what lies beyond it.

### The Twenty Verses

Vasubandhu's *Twenty Verses* (*Viṃśatikā*) is the school's classic argument [vasubandhu-vimsatika](../sources/vasubandhu-vimsatika.md).
Its strategy is to show that everything that seems to require external objects can be explained without
them. An opponent objects that if experience were not caused by objects, it would not be fixed to
particular places and times, would not be shared between people, and would not have real effects.
Vasubandhu answers each point with an example. In a *dream*, things appear at definite places and times,
and a dream can have real bodily effects, yet there are no objects. Hungry ghosts (*pretas*), beings of the
same kind with the same karma, all see a river as flowing with pus, which shows that a shared world can be
produced by a shared mental condition. The appearance of a common world is explained by similar karmic
dispositions in many streams of consciousness, not by a common external cause.

Vasubandhu adds an argument against material atoms. If an atom has parts facing in different directions,
it is divisible and not an atom; if it has none, atoms cannot combine to make extended objects, since
partless things cannot touch side by side. So extended material objects cannot be built from atoms, and
there is no other candidate for what external objects might be made of.

### Store-consciousness and the eight consciousnesses

To explain how experience can be orderly without an external world, Yogācāra posits a deeper level of
mind. Besides the six ordinary consciousnesses (one for each of the five senses, plus mental
consciousness), it recognizes a seventh, the afflicted mind (*manas*), which mistakes the stream for a
self, and an eighth, the *store-consciousness* (*ālaya-vijñāna*). The store-consciousness is a continuous,
subliminal stream that holds the "seeds" (*bīja*) laid down by past actions. When conditions are right,
the seeds ripen into the experiences of the other consciousnesses, including the appearance of a world.
This takes over the role that the external world and the self play in other philosophies: it accounts for
continuity, memory and the regularity of experience. Critics, both Buddhist and Hindu, objected that it
looks suspiciously like a self under another name.

### Relation to other views

Yogācāra is often compared with Berkeley's idealism, but the differences are deep. Berkeley's world is
the content of minds that are souls and of the mind of God; Yogācāra has neither souls nor a creator, only
impersonal streams of consciousness. It is closer, in its treatment of a shared world as the product of
parallel streams, to Leibniz's monadology. It differs from Advaita Vedānta, which also treats the world of
plurality as appearance, in denying that there is any one changeless consciousness behind it. Within
Buddhism, the Madhyamaka school of Nāgārjuna criticized Yogācāra for treating consciousness as more real
than its objects, when on the Madhyamaka view both are equally "empty" of intrinsic nature.

### In plain terms

Yogācāra is a Buddhist form of the view that the world is "all in the mind." What seem to be solid
objects outside us are really just the contents of experience. Its main argument is the dream: in a dream,
things seem real, located and shared, and can even affect your body, yet nothing is out there. Why think
waking life is different? What makes the world orderly and shared, on this view, are deep mental habits
laid down by past actions, stored in a hidden layer of mind. Unusually for an idealism, it denies there is
any self whose mind this is: there are only streams of experience, belonging to no one.
