*Christian physicalism* (also *Christian materialism*) is the view, held by a number of contemporary
Christian philosophers and theologians, that human beings have no immaterial soul. A person is a
physical organism, or is constituted by one, and the Christian hope of life after death rests entirely
on the resurrection of the body rather than on the survival of a soul. Its leading defenders include the
philosophers Peter van Inwagen [van-inwagen-1978](../sources/van-inwagen-1978.md), Lynne Rudder Baker [baker-2000](../sources/baker-2000.md) and
Kevin Corcoran [corcoran-2006](../sources/corcoran-2006.md), and the theologian Nancey Murphy, who calls her version
*nonreductive physicalism* [murphy-2006](../sources/murphy-2006.md).

The position commits to the claims that [the mental is physical](../claims/mental-is-physical.md) and that
[survival of death requires bodily resurrection](../claims/survival-requires-resurrection.md). It rejects
the traditional view that [the self is an immaterial substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md).

### Motivations

Christian physicalists give three kinds of reason. The first is scientific. Neuroscience has found that
every mental capacity once attributed to the soul, from memory and emotion to moral judgment and religious
experience, depends on specific brain processes and can be damaged by damaging them. Murphy argues that
the soul has been left with no work to do. The second is philosophical: the interaction problem for
substance dualism, the difficulty of saying how an immaterial soul could move a body, is avoided
altogether. The third is biblical. Many scholars argue that the Hebrew Bible conceives of a human being
as a unified living creature rather than a soul housed in a body, and that the New Testament hope is the
resurrection of the dead rather than the immortality of the soul, an idea they regard as a later import
from Greek philosophy [cullmann-1958](../sources/cullmann-1958.md).

### Forms of the view

Van Inwagen holds that human persons are living animals, identical with their organisms. Baker defends a
*constitution view*: a person is not identical with her body but is *constituted* by it, as a statue is
constituted by a lump of clay without being identical with it. What makes the constituted thing a person
is a capacity for a first-person perspective, the ability to think of oneself as oneself. Murphy's
nonreductive physicalism holds that persons are wholly physical, but that their higher capacities, such as
rationality and moral and religious awareness, are emergent features of complex neural and social systems
that cannot be reduced to lower-level physics. All three versions deny any immaterial part, and all of
them face the same problem about life after death.

### The resurrection problem

If a person is a physical thing and that thing is destroyed at death, decays and is scattered, what could
make a body raised at the resurrection the *same* person rather than a new person exactly like the old
one? God could reassemble the original atoms, but the same atoms are shared over time by many bodies, and
reassembling a scattered thing seems to produce a replica rather than the original. This is the
*duplication* or *gap* problem.

Christian physicalists have proposed several answers. Van Inwagen suggested, as a mere possibility, that
God might preserve each body at death, removing it and leaving a simulacrum in its place, so that the
very same organism could later be revived [van-inwagen-1978](../sources/van-inwagen-1978.md). Dean Zimmerman's "falling
elevator" model supposes that at the moment of death each particle of the body divides, one set of
offspring continuing as the corpse and the other immediately carrying the life of the person forward
elsewhere, so that there is no gap in the causal chain [zimmerman-1999](../sources/zimmerman-1999.md). Baker holds that the
same person exists wherever the same first-person perspective is constituted, so that a resurrection body
need not be made of the same matter at all [baker-2000](../sources/baker-2000.md). Some, reviving an old view called
*mortalism*, accept that the person simply does not exist between death and resurrection and hold that
God's re-creation is enough for identity. Critics reply that every answer either makes identity depend on
a miracle tailored to the purpose or turns into a view on which a perfect copy would count as survival,
which most hold it does not.

### Relation to other views

Christian physicalism differs from secular physicalism less in its metaphysics of mind than in combining
it with a theistic account of survival. It differs from Thomistic [hylomorphism](hylomorphism.md), which also
emphasizes the unity of soul and body but holds that the soul subsists between death and resurrection. It
also differs from William Hasker's *emergent dualism*, which agrees that the mind arises from the brain but
holds that what arises is a new, non-physical substance that could survive the brain's destruction
[hasker-1999](../sources/hasker-1999.md).

### In plain terms

Most Christians have traditionally believed that people have souls that live on after death. Christian
physicalists disagree. They think a human being is a physical creature and nothing more, but they still
believe in life after death, through the resurrection of the body that the Bible describes. They point to
neuroscience, which shows that everything the soul was supposed to do depends on the brain, and to
biblical scholars who argue that the early Christian hope was resurrection, not an immortal soul. The hard
question for them is identity: if your body rots away and God later makes a new one, why is that you and
not a perfect copy of you?
