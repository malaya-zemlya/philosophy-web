The *intermediate-level theory* holds that we are conscious only of a middle stage of mental
processing. Perception and language are processed in a hierarchy of levels, from the raw sensory signal
at the bottom to abstract meaning and category at the top. The theory says that what appears in
awareness is drawn from neither end of this hierarchy but from the middle. When someone speaks to you, you
are not aware of the sound wave as such, nor of the grammatical structure or meaning of the sentence,
but of something in between: a sequence of words heard as sounds of your language, with their rhythm,
stress and intonation. The theory was proposed by the linguist and cognitive scientist Ray Jackendoff
in *Consciousness and the Computational Mind* [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md) and later developed by the
philosopher Jesse Prinz [prinz-2000](../sources/prinz-2000.md), [prinz-2012](../sources/prinz-2012.md).

The position commits to the claim that [the contents of
experience are drawn only from intermediate levels of processing](../claims/consciousness-is-intermediate-level.md). It is a theory of *which*
representations give [phenomenal-consciousness](../concepts/phenomenal-consciousness.md) its form, not of why any representation is
experienced at all. Jackendoff was explicit about the limit. He distinguished the *computational mind*,
the system of representations and processes that cognitive science describes, from the
*phenomenological mind*, the domain of experience, and called the relation between them the
*mind-mind problem*. He found it "completely unclear" how computations, however complex, "can add up to
an experience" (p. 18), and so framed his hypotheses as claims about which computational distinctions
are "caused by/supported by/projected from" into awareness, leaving the nature of that relation open
[jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md). Two ground rules govern the whole inquiry. The *Hypothesis of Computational
Sufficiency* says that "every phenomenological distinction is caused by/supported by/projected from a
corresponding computational distinction," though not every computational distinction shows up in
awareness, which is what leaves room for unconscious processing. The *Hypothesis of the Nonefficacy of
Consciousness* says that awareness of something "cannot in itself have any effect on the computational
mind"; only the computational states that support it can (pp. 24–25). The framework is therefore
explicitly a form of [epiphenomenalism](epiphenomenalism.md) about experience, and the intermediate-level theory
is its answer to the question of exactly which computational distinctions emerge into awareness.

### Language: sounds, but not raw sounds

Jackendoff's starting point was the architecture of language as linguists describe it. Speech is
processed through a *phonological* structure (segments, syllables, words as sound-forms, stress and
intonation), a *syntactic* structure (the grammatical organization of the sentence) and a *conceptual*
structure (its meaning). He observed that in speaking or listening we are not aware of three coexisting
organizations, one for each level. The form of linguistic awareness comes from phonology alone: "we
experience the inner speech stream as segmented into words and possibly further into syllables or
individual segments," with its rhythm and intonation (p. 288). Even within phonology, not everything is
available. "We certainly do not experience phonological segments as composites of distinctive
features," the articulatory and acoustic properties that phonologists use to analyse speech sounds. By
contrast, "the units of syntax—nouns, verbs, prepositional phrases, and so on—do not present themselves
to awareness at all," and the units of conceptual structure are hidden still more completely
(p. 288) [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md).

He gave several arguments that phonological structure is both necessary and sufficient for the form of
linguistic awareness:

1. *Sufficiency: nonsense and foreign speech.* Nonsense syllables such as *zup* and *riz*, and speech in a
   language one does not understand, are experienced as linguistic form even though no meaning is
   attached.
2. *Necessity: the tip of the tongue.* When a word is on the tip of one's tongue, the meaning one intends
   is present and the word's place in the sentence is fixed, but its sound is missing. What one
   experiences is a void "all affect and no form," just as the theory predicts if awareness is projected
   from a phonological representation that is empty (pp. 290–291).
3. *Modality.* Conceptual structure is shared between language and vision. If awareness came from it, "we
   would not be able to tell whether we were seeing something or being told it" (p. 290). Memory, by contrast,
   often does lose track of whether one saw something or was told it, as one would expect if long-term
   memory is stored largely as conceptual structure.
4. *Thinking in a language.* Bilinguals can say which language they think or dream in. That would make no
   sense if verbal imagery were projected from language-neutral conceptual structure.
5. *Sign language.* In American Sign Language, which has no phonology in the spoken sense, verbal imagery
   takes the form of the structures that replace phonology. Anecdotally, deaf native signers experience
   it mainly as bodily sensations of signing, hearing signers mainly as seen gestures.

The same holds for inner speech. Thought is often experienced as a sequence of verbal images, yet these
images are "our experiential evidence that thought is taking place," not thought itself. Jackendoff
later put this by saying that the "talking voice in the head" is phonological structure linked to a
thought, while the thought itself is "completely unconscious" [jackendoff-1996](../sources/jackendoff-1996.md). He
acknowledged that the conclusion is counterintuitive: "How can the contents of consciousness consist of
just a string of sounds?" [jackendoff-2007](../sources/jackendoff-2007.md)

### Music

Musical awareness follows the same pattern. Drawing on his work with Fred Lerdahl [lerdahl-jackendoff-1983](../sources/lerdahl-jackendoff-1983.md), Jackendoff argued
that what we hear is the *musical surface*, the ordered sequence of notes and chords. This is not the
lowest level, since a good deal of auditory processing is needed to discriminate pitches and attack
points, nor the highest, since four further levels of hierarchical structure (grouping, metre and two
kinds of reduction) underlie our understanding of a piece. One does not hear "the musical surface plus
four contrapuntal hierarchies." The analogue of nonsense syllables is contemporary music written to
frustrate the rules of musical grammar: listeners find it incomprehensible, but they certainly hear it
(pp. 292–293) [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md).

### Vision

For vision Jackendoff drew on David Marr's computational theory [marr-1982](../sources/marr-1982.md). Marr divided
visual processing into a *primal sketch*, which registers local edges, blobs and changes of intensity; a
*2½-D sketch*, which represents the orientation and distance of visible surfaces relative to the
viewer; and a *3-D model*, a viewpoint-independent, hierarchical representation of an object's whole
shape, used for recognition. Jackendoff argued that "the level of visual structure most directly
responsible for the form of visual awareness is the 2½D sketch" (p. 293) [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md).

The primal sketch is too poor: it does not even divide the visual field into surfaces, and it comes
before the images from the two eyes are fused, whereas we never experience two discrepant views. The
3-D model is in one way too rich and in another too poor. It is too rich because it includes the hidden
back and interior of objects and is organized around axes and coordinate systems, whereas "one sees the
surfaces of objects, not the axes down their middles." It is too poor because it is shared between sight
and touch, so that if it were the basis of visual awareness "I could not tell whether I was seeing or
feeling an object."

The result is a distinction between the *form* of visual awareness, how things look, which comes from
the 2½-D sketch, and visual *understanding*, what one takes oneself to be seeing, which comes from the 3-D
model and conceptual structure held in register with it. Jackendoff illustrated the gap with several
cases. A bookcase looks exactly the same whether or not a cat is hidden behind it, though one
understands the two scenes differently. A stick half in water looks bent even when one knows it is
straight. A blurred scene in fog is seen without being understood, until a moment of recognition
supplies a 3-D model and new contours suddenly appear in it. The same analysis extends to visual
imagery, which is likewise viewer-centred, and to Berkeley's old puzzle about images of triangles: an
image must depict some particular triangle, but it can stand for triangles in general because the
abstract structures in register with it do the generalizing.

### Form, content and feel

Jackendoff's final formulation, *Theory VIII*, distinguishes three contributions to experience: "the
intermediate levels provide its form, the central levels provide its content, and the affects provide
its 'feel'" (p. 309) [jackendoff-1987](../sources/jackendoff-1987.md). *Affects* are features attached to phenomenal
entities rather than to their form: outer versus inner (percept versus image), ego-initiated versus not
(the sense of willing something), meaningful versus meaningless, and familiar versus novel. On this
account a hallucination is "an error of affect," an image experienced with the affect proper to a
percept, and *déjà vu* is the affect *familiar* attached to something new. They answer a problem the earlier formulations could not: seeing and visual imagining
both draw on the 2½-D sketch, so something other than form must tell them apart. The theory also
explains, Jackendoff argued, why "pure sensation" cannot be introspected, why one feels only one's own
pain, and why, as William James complained, the self seems to show up in awareness only as "peculiar
motions in the head or between the head and throat."

The theory already included a role for attention. Jackendoff required that a conscious representation
be among those selected as most salient in short-term memory and "enriched by attentional processing,"
and he explained the greater vividness of attended parts of experience in these terms. Experience of a
single object through several senses is unified because the separate intermediate-level
representations are each in register with the same central representation of the object.

### Prinz's AIR theory

Prinz turned Jackendoff's hypothesis into a neuroscientifically grounded theory, the *attended
intermediate-level representation* (AIR) theory [prinz-2012](../sources/prinz-2012.md). He made attention the deciding
condition: intermediate-level representations become conscious when, and only when, attention makes
them available to working memory. This accounts for cases such as inattentional blindness, in which an
unattended object in plain view goes unseen although it is processed. Prinz proposes neural realizers for
both conditions and draws a strong corollary: high-level properties such as *being a pine tree* or
*being my car keys* are judged rather than experienced, and thought has no experiential character
beyond the sensory imagery, inner speech and feeling that accompany it. Neuroscientific work fits the
general picture. Francis Crick and Christof Koch argued that we are not directly aware of activity in
primary visual cortex, the first cortical stage of vision [crick-koch-1995](../sources/crick-koch-1995.md), a hypothesis that
has shaped the search for the [neural-correlates-of-consciousness](../concepts/neural-correlates-of-consciousness.md).

### Objections

The theory's main opponents are defenders of *cognitive phenomenology*, who hold that understanding has
an experiential character of its own [bayne-montague-2011](../sources/bayne-montague-2011.md). Galen Strawson's example contrasts
a French speaker with a monolingual English speaker listening to the same French sentence
[strawson-1994b](../sources/strawson-1994b.md). The sounds are the same for both, yet there seems to be something it is like
for the first to *understand* them. Jackendoff anticipated the objection. Asking what is meant by
saying that English "sounds meaningful" and Armenian does not, he answered that the difference lies in
conceptual structure "hidden behind the scenes," together with a different affect attached to the
utterance. The critic replies that this concedes a difference in experience and merely relabels it.

A parallel objection concerns perception. Susanna Siegel argues that once you have learned to recognize
pine trees, pine trees *look* different to you, so that a high-level property enters visual experience
[siegel-2010](../sources/siegel-2010.md). The intermediate-level theorist can reply, as Jackendoff did for recognition in
fog, that understanding changes the intermediate-level representation itself by top-down feedback,
rather than entering experience directly. A further worry is that the notion of a "level" is borrowed
from particular models of language and vision, so that the theory is only as secure as those models.
Marr's three-stage scheme, in particular, is no longer accepted in its original form. Jackendoff
himself noted that the theory could be checked only for language, vision and music, the faculties for
which a theory of levels existed.

### Relation to other theories

The intermediate-level theory answers a different question from most theories of consciousness. It is
distinct from [representationalism](representationalism.md), which identifies phenomenal character with
representational content in general; the intermediate-level theory restricts which level of content
can be phenomenal. [global-workspace](global-workspace.md) and [higher-order-theories](higher-order-theories.md) say what
*makes* a representation conscious (its global broadcast, or its being the object of a higher-order
state), and could in principle be combined with the claim that only intermediate-level representations
qualify. Jackendoff rejected the main alternatives of his day: "central-level" theories, on which
conceptual structure is the locus of awareness (he counted Dennett's early buffer model among them), and
"all-level" theories, on which every level in short-term memory is experienced. Prinz argues further that
if his version of the theory is correct, higher-order thought accounts of consciousness fail
[prinz-2000](../sources/prinz-2000.md). The theory also bears on the [grain-problem](../concepts/grain-problem.md): if experience reflects a
level of processing well above the firing of individual neurons, its smoothness relative to the brain's
fine grain is less surprising.

### In plain terms

Your brain processes what you hear and see in stages, from raw signal up to full understanding. This
theory says you are only ever aware of the middle stages. When someone talks to you, you hear neither
the raw sound wave nor the grammar and meaning: you hear words, as sounds. That is why nonsense words
like "zup" still sound like language, and why, when a word is on the tip of your tongue, you know exactly
what you mean but experience only a frustrating gap where the sound should be. Vision works the same way.
You see surfaces from where you stand, not the raw pattern of light and not the whole object with its
back side included; a bookcase looks the same whether or not a cat is hiding behind it, even though you
know the difference. Meaning, on this view, is understood behind the scenes rather than experienced.
Critics reply that understanding a sentence surely feels like something over and above hearing its
sounds.
