*Advaita Vedānta* ("non-dual Vedānta") is the school of Hindu philosophy, systematized by Śaṅkara in the
eighth century CE, which holds that the true self of every person is identical with *Brahman*, the one
partless, changeless consciousness that is the only ultimate reality. The many individual selves, and the
world of distinct things they perceive, are appearances produced by ignorance (*avidyā*). When ignorance is
removed, nothing remains to be distinguished: there is just one consciousness, which each of us always was
[sep-shankara](../sources/sep-shankara.md), [shankara-brahmasutra](../sources/shankara-brahmasutra.md).

The position commits to the claim that [the individual self is identical with
the one universal consciousness](../claims/self-is-brahman.md), and so to a form of the view that [reality is
fundamentally mental](../claims/reality-is-fundamentally-mental.md). It rejects the Buddhist [denial of an enduring self](../claims/no-enduring-self.md):
Advaita's complaint against the Buddhists is not that they doubt the ordinary self but that they fail to
find the real one.

### The self as witness

Advaita's self is not the self of everyday life. It is not the body, the senses, the mind or the ego, all
of which are objects that can be observed and that change. It is the *witness* (*sākṣin*), the pure
awareness to which all of these appear, and which cannot itself become an object, just as an eye cannot
see itself. Advaita finds evidence for such a witness in the three states of waking, dreaming and
dreamless sleep. In waking and dreaming there are objects; in deep sleep there are none, yet on waking
one can say "I slept well and knew nothing," which Advaitins read as testimony that awareness persisted
without any content. The witness is the constant across the three states, and is sometimes called the
"fourth" (*turīya*).

### Superimposition and the problem of the many

The central concept of Advaita is *superimposition* (*adhyāsa*): the mistaken attribution of the features
of one thing to another. The standard example is a rope seen in dim light as a snake. The snake is not
nothing, since it is really experienced, but it is not real either, since it vanishes when the rope is
seen correctly. In the same way, the features of body and mind (being born, being in pain, being this
particular person) are superimposed on pure consciousness, and the features of consciousness are
superimposed on body and mind, so that the body seems to be aware.

This gives Advaita its answer to the question of how one consciousness can appear as many, the
question that modern philosophers call the [decombination-problem](../concepts/decombination-problem.md). The usual image is space
in jars. Space is one and undivided, but the space inside each jar seems separate from the space in the
others, and seems to come and go when a jar is made or broken. Consciousness is likewise one; the
appearance of many separate subjects comes from the limiting adjuncts (*upādhis*) of individual bodies and
minds, not from any real division. The cost of this answer is that it must treat the plurality of selves,
and the world itself, as ultimately unreal, an appearance with no independent being.

### Rivals within Vedānta

Advaita is one of several schools that interpret the same scriptures, the Upaniṣads, the
*Bhagavad Gītā* and the *Brahma Sūtras*, and its rivals reject its central thesis. Rāmānuja's
*Viśiṣṭādvaita* ("qualified non-dualism") holds that individual souls and the world are real and
distinct, though they depend on Brahman as a body depends on its soul. Madhva's *Dvaita* ("dualism")
holds that souls are eternally and irreducibly distinct from God and from one another. Both object that
Advaita cannot say *whose* ignorance produces the illusion of plurality, since before the illusion there
is no one but Brahman to be ignorant, and Brahman is pure knowledge.

### Modern connections

Advaita has drawn renewed attention in contemporary philosophy of mind. Miri Albahari argues that a
universal consciousness without a point of view of its own, of the Advaitic kind, avoids difficulties that
face [cosmopsychism](cosmopsychism.md), on which the cosmos is a subject with its own perspective
[albahari-2020](../sources/albahari-2020.md). Bernardo Kastrup's analytic idealism, on which individual minds are
dissociated parts of one universal mind, offers a comparable picture in the language of psychiatry
[kastrup-2019](../sources/kastrup-2019.md).

### In plain terms

Advaita Vedānta, the most influential school of Hindu philosophy, teaches that deep down there is only one
consciousness, and that you are it. The feeling of being a separate person with your own body and
thoughts is a kind of illusion, like seeing a coiled rope in the dark and taking it for a snake. Your real
self is not your body, your thoughts or your personality, but the plain awareness in which all of those
appear. Many people seem to exist the way space seems divided into the insides of many jars: break the jars
and there was only ever one space. Critics, including other Hindu schools, ask who exactly is under the
illusion, if there is only one consciousness and it is perfectly aware.
