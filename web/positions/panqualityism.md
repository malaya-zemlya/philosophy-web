*Panqualityism* is the view that the basic constituents of the world have intrinsic *qualities* of the
same kind as those we find in experience, such as redness, sweetness or the felt sharpness of pain, but
that these qualities exist *unexperienced*. Consciousness, on this view, comes later. A quality becomes
part of someone's experience only when a subject is aware of it, and being aware is something that
arises in suitably organized systems such as brains. Qualities therefore come first, both in the order
of nature and in the order of explanation, and experience is a relation that some systems come to bear
to qualities that were there all along. The name was introduced by Herbert Feigl, who credited it to a
conversation with Stephen C. Pepper [feigl-1960](../sources/feigl-1960.md), and the view is defended today chiefly by Sam
Coleman [coleman-2015](../sources/coleman-2015.md), [coleman-2016](../sources/coleman-2016.md).

The position commits to the claim that [qualities of the kind found
in experience can exist unexperienced](../claims/qualities-exist-unexperienced.md), and accepts the objection to panpsychism that
[micro-subjects cannot sum into a macro-subject](../claims/micro-subjects-do-not-sum.md). It rejects the
panpsychist claim that [consciousness itself is present in
the simplest physical entities](../claims/consciousness-fundamental-ubiquitous.md).

### Qualities before consciousness

The view is sometimes summarized as the claim that *qualia precede consciousness*, and the summary needs
one refinement. In most usage, *qualia* are by definition the qualities of experiences, so an
unexperienced quale would be a contradiction. Panqualityists deny that this is part of what the
qualities are. The redness in your experience of a ripe tomato is, on their view, a quality that is
*contingently* experienced by you: the very same kind of quality could exist with no one aware of it.
Coleman sometimes speaks of "unexperienced qualia" to make the point vivid. What precedes consciousness,
then, is not experience but its qualitative material.

Consider an analogy with music. A melody played in an empty hall still has its pitch, rhythm and
timbre; what it lacks is a listener. Panqualityism holds that the world's basic qualities are like the
melody: fully there, with determinate character, but unheard until a subject is organized to attend to
them. The analogy is imperfect, since sound waves are not themselves qualities in the relevant sense,
but it captures the separation of quality from awareness that the view requires.

### Lineage

Versions of the view were popular among the *neutral monists* of the late nineteenth and early twentieth
centuries, who held that the world is built from a stuff that is neither mental nor physical in itself
(see [neutral-monism](neutral-monism.md)). Ernst Mach treated colours, sounds and pressures as the elements of
which both bodies and minds are composed [mach-1914](../sources/mach-1914.md). William James argued that "pure experience"
is neutral material that counts as mental in one context and physical in another
[james-1904](../sources/james-1904.md), and Bertrand Russell developed a similar picture in *The Analysis of Mind*
[russell-1921](../sources/russell-1921.md). Among later philosophers, Peter Unger argued that physical things must have
intrinsic qualities and that the qualities we know from experience are the only ones we can conceive
[unger-1999](../sources/unger-1999.md). Panqualityism is now usually classed as a form of *panprotopsychism*, the view
that the fundamental properties are not themselves experiential but are precursors from which experience
can be constituted ([protophenomenal-properties-fundamental](../claims/protophenomenal-properties-fundamental.md)), and as a form of [russellian-monism](russellian-monism.md), since the qualities play the role
of the intrinsic natures that physics leaves unspecified ([intrinsic-structural-distinction](../concepts/intrinsic-structural-distinction.md))
[chalmers-2017](../sources/chalmers-2017.md), [sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md).

### Why hold it

Panqualityism is a response to the [combination-problem-panpsychism](../concepts/combination-problem-panpsychism.md). Ordinary panpsychism
puts tiny *subjects* of experience at the base of reality and then faces the problem that subjects seem
unable to combine into further subjects, each having a private point of view that excludes the others
[coleman-2014](../sources/coleman-2014.md). Panqualityism removes the micro-subjects. Qualities, unlike subjects, have no
point of view to protect, and there is no evident obstacle to many qualities combining into a richer
quality or a structured field of them, much as colours mix [coleman-2012](../sources/coleman-2012.md). The subject is then
explained separately, as something that arises at the level of brains.

The view also keeps what attracts people to panpsychism. It gives physics an intrinsic nature that is
not a mystery, since we are acquainted with qualities in our own experience, and it avoids the brute
emergence of qualitative character from the wholly non-qualitative, since the qualities were there from
the start. In the [hard problem](../questions/hard-problem.md), it divides the explanatory burden in two: the
*qualitative* side of consciousness is fundamental, while the *subjective* side, the fact that the
qualities are experienced, is given a reductive explanation. The Stanford survey describes it accordingly
as a middle way between panpsychism and physicalism [sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md).

### Explaining awareness

Panqualityism owes an account of how qualities come to be experienced. The usual strategy is
reductive. Coleman combines panqualityism with a *higher-order* theory of awareness: a quality becomes
conscious when it figures in a higher-order mental state, and in particular when that state does not
merely represent the quality by a symbol but *embeds* it, as a quotation embeds the words it quotes
[coleman-2015](../sources/coleman-2015.md). On this *quotational* view, the very quality, and not a description of it, is
taken up into the subject's awareness, which is meant to explain why experienced qualities seem
immediately present to us (compare [higher-order-theories](higher-order-theories.md)). Other panqualityists prefer a
functionalist account, on which a quality is experienced when it plays the right role in an organism's
cognitive economy.

### Objections

The main objection, due to David Chalmers, is that the move from unexperienced to experienced qualities
leaves the same kind of gap that panpsychism was meant to close
[no arrangement of qualities necessitates awareness of
them](../claims/qualities-do-not-necessitate-awareness.md) [chalmers-2017](../sources/chalmers-2017.md). It seems conceivable that a world contains all the same qualities,
organized in the same way, and that nothing in it is aware of any of them. If that is possible, the
facts about qualities do not ground the facts about experience, and the subjective side of consciousness
is as unexplained as before. Panqualityism, Chalmers concludes, avoids the subject-combination problem
only by facing an *awareness problem* that looks equally hard. Coleman replies that a properly
developed account of awareness, such as the quotational theory, shows that awareness involves nothing
over and above qualities appropriately embedded, so that the scenario only seems conceivable.

A second objection is that the notion of an unexperienced quality is incoherent. Many philosophers hold
that a pain just is a way of feeling, and redness, as a quality we know, just is a way of appearing; strip
away the experiencing and nothing qualitative remains. Panqualityists reply that this confuses the
quality with our acquaintance with it, but the dispute turns on intuitions that are hard to adjudicate. A
third objection, pressed against neutral monism generally, asks how an electron's having a quality like
redness could ground its charge or mass, and doubts that sensory qualities are the right kind of thing to
be the intrinsic nature of fundamental physics.

### Relation to neighbouring views

Panqualityism shares with [panpsychism](panpsychism.md) the conviction that something qualitative goes all
the way down, but denies that this something is experience or involves subjects. It shares with
[neutral-monism](neutral-monism.md) the idea that the basic stuff is neither mental nor physical in itself, but
gives that stuff a positive, qualitative character rather than leaving it unknown. It differs from
[physicalism](physicalism.md) in holding that qualitative character is fundamental and irreducible, though it
agrees with the physicalist that subjectivity can be explained.

### In plain terms

Panqualityism says that the basic ingredients of the world have qualities like the ones we experience,
such as colours, sounds and feels, but that nobody is experiencing them. Think of a song playing in an
empty room. Experience comes later, when a brain is organized in the right way to "take in" those
qualities. The view was designed to fix a problem with panpsychism, which says tiny bits of matter have
tiny experiences of their own: it is hard to see how millions of tiny experiencers could merge into one
mind. Qualities, unlike experiencers, can blend easily, like paints. Critics reply that this just moves
the mystery: it is still unclear how unfelt qualities ever become felt. Some also doubt that a quality like
pain can exist without anyone feeling it.
