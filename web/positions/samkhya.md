*Sāṃkhya* is one of the oldest schools of Indian philosophy, and its classical form is a dualism of two
utterly different principles: *puruṣa*, pure consciousness, and *prakṛti*, matter or nature. Its
distinctive feature is where it draws the line between them. Everything we would ordinarily call mental,
including the intellect, the sense of "I," the mind and the senses, belongs on the side of matter. Only
bare, contentless, inactive awareness belongs to *puruṣa*. The school's classical text is the
*Sāṃkhyakārikā* of Īśvarakṛṣṇa (c. 4th–5th century CE) [isvarakrsna-samkhyakarika](../sources/isvarakrsna-samkhyakarika.md). Its
metaphysics was adopted, with the addition of a supreme Lord, by the Yoga school of Patañjali, so the two
are often treated as one system.

The position commits to the claims that consciousness is [fundamental
and non-physical](../claims/consciousness-fundamental-nonphysical.md) and that [thought and cognition are processes of
matter](../claims/cognition-belongs-to-matter.md). It rejects the Buddhist [denial of a self](../claims/no-enduring-self.md) and, unlike Advaita,
holds that there are many selves, not one.

### Matter and its evolutes

*Prakṛti* is a single, uncreated, ever-changing material principle made up of three strands or
qualities (*guṇas*): *sattva* (lightness, clarity), *rajas* (activity, passion) and *tamas* (heaviness,
inertia). When their balance is disturbed, matter unfolds into a series of products. First comes the
intellect (*buddhi*), the faculty of judgment and decision; from it, the ego-sense (*ahaṃkāra*), the sense
that experiences and actions are "mine"; from that, the mind (*manas*), the senses, the organs of action,
the subtle elements and finally the gross elements of the physical world. Together with *puruṣa*, these
make up the school's twenty-five principles (*tattvas*).

The striking consequence is that the intellect, the ego and the mind are material. They are made of the
same stuff as earth and water, only subtler. Thinking, deciding and the sense of being a self are things
matter does. In modern terms, Sāṃkhya places the whole of cognition, everything a functional or
computational theory of mind would describe, on the physical side, and leaves outside it only the bare
fact of awareness, roughly what contemporary philosophers call *phenomenal consciousness* as opposed to
*access* (see [access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness](../concepts/access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness.md)).

### Consciousness as witness

*Puruṣa* is pure consciousness. It does nothing, changes in no way and has no contents. It is a witness
in whose light the transformations of matter become experienced. The *Kārikā* explains their relation
with the image of a lame man and a blind man: matter can act but cannot see, consciousness can see but
cannot act, and together, the lame man riding on the blind man's shoulders, they make experience
possible. Because the intellect, which really does the judging, is lit up by consciousness, and
consciousness seems to take on the intellect's activity, each is mistaken for the other: we think that
consciousness acts and that the intellect is aware. Bondage is this confusion.

Sāṃkhya holds that there are many *puruṣas*, one for each living being. Its argument is that if there
were only one, all beings would be born, die and be liberated together, and they plainly are not.

### Liberation

The goal of the system is *kaivalya*, "isolation": the discriminating knowledge that consciousness is not
matter and has never really been involved in it. The *Kārikā* likens matter to a dancer who withdraws
once she has been seen. When the intellect has displayed itself fully and the distinction is grasped,
matter ceases to perform for that *puruṣa*, which remains in its own nature, alone. Yoga supplies the
practical discipline of meditation by which the mind's activity is stilled and this discrimination
achieved.

### Objections

The classic objection, pressed by Vedāntins and Buddhists alike, is the interaction problem in an Indian
form. If consciousness is utterly inactive and unchanging, and matter is utterly unconscious, how do they
come together at all, and how can the "confusion" between them occur, if neither is the kind of thing that
can be confused? Advaita kept Sāṃkhya's picture of consciousness as a pure witness but resolved the
difficulty by denying the plurality of selves and the full reality of matter. A modern objection is that
the view makes consciousness causally idle, a form of [epiphenomenalism](epiphenomenalism.md), since everything
that happens, including our talk about consciousness, is done by matter.

### In plain terms

Sāṃkhya, an ancient Indian philosophy, splits reality into two: pure awareness and everything else. The
surprise is where it puts the mind. Thinking, deciding, remembering and even the sense of "I" are all, on
this view, things that matter does. Only the bare fact of being aware is not material. Awareness itself
does nothing; it just witnesses, like a lame man who can see riding on the shoulders of a blind man who
can walk. Our trouble, according to Sāṃkhya, is that we confuse the two, imagining that awareness is busy
thinking. Freedom comes from seeing the difference clearly. Critics ask how two such completely different
things could ever be connected.
