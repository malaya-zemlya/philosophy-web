*Substance dualism* is the view that a person consists of two distinct things, a material body and an
immaterial mind or soul, and that the mind is the thing that thinks, feels and wills
([soul-is-immaterial-substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md)). The mind is not a property or activity of the brain but a
separate *substance*, a bearer of properties in its own right, which could in principle exist without
any body at all. In its usual form, *interactionist* dualism, mind and body act on each other: a pin
prick causes pain, and a decision to raise one's arm causes the arm to rise
([mental-events-have-physical-effects](../claims/mental-events-have-physical-effects.md)).

### Descartes and the classic arguments

The view is most closely associated with René Descartes ([descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md)). Descartes
argued that he could clearly conceive of himself existing as a thinking thing without a body, while
he could not conceive of himself without thought, and concluded that mind and body are really
distinct. He also argued from their different natures. Body is extended in space and divisible into
parts, while the mind has no extension and no parts, so the two cannot be the same thing. Avicenna's
earlier [floating-man](../arguments/floating-man.md) anticipates the first of these arguments. In recent philosophy the
view has been defended by Richard Swinburne, who argues that facts about personal identity are not
fixed by any bodily or psychological facts and that a person therefore has an immaterial soul as an
essential part ([swinburne-1986](../sources/swinburne-1986.md)).

Substance dualism differs from [property-dualism](property-dualism.md), which accepts only one kind of
substance but holds that some of its properties, the phenomenal ones, are non-physical. The
substance dualist's further claim is that the subject of experience is itself non-physical. That
makes the view attractive to those who hope for survival after bodily death, and it is the dualist
answer recorded in [soul](../questions/soul.md) and surveyed in [soul](../concepts/soul.md).

### The interaction problem

The oldest and most pressing objection was put to Descartes in 1643 by Princess Elisabeth of
Bohemia ([elisabeth-1643](../sources/elisabeth-1643.md)). If the mind has no extension and no location, how can it move a
body? Causation between bodies seemed to require contact, and an unextended mind can touch nothing.
Descartes's replies, including his suggestion that the mind acts on the body through the pineal
gland, were widely thought to move the problem rather than solve it.

The modern form of the objection appeals to the [causal-closure](../concepts/causal-closure.md) of the physical. If every
physical event has a sufficient physical cause, there is no room for a non-physical mind to make the
brain do anything, and an interacting soul would have to violate the laws of physics. Jaegwon Kim adds
a *pairing problem*: when two souls act at the same moment, nothing spatial links each soul to its own
body rather than another, so it is unclear what makes a given soul the cause of changes in one body
in particular ([kim-2005](../sources/kim-2005.md)). Substance dualists have answered that the closure of physics is
an assumption rather than a finding, or that a soul might act in ways that respect conservation
laws.

Not every substance dualist is an interactionist. *Occasionalism* makes God the
only true cause of both mental and bodily events, and Leibniz held that mind and body run in a
harmony pre-established by God without acting on each other ([leibniz-monadology](../sources/leibniz-monadology.md)). A
substance dualist who gives up interaction altogether arrives at a form of
[epiphenomenalism](epiphenomenalism.md).

### In plain terms

Substance dualism says you are made of two different things: a physical body and a non-physical mind
or soul. Your thoughts and feelings belong to the soul, not to your brain, and the soul could in
principle exist without the body. This is the picture defended by the seventeenth-century philosopher
René Descartes. Its famous difficulty, first raised by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, is explaining
how something with no physical existence can push a physical body around.
