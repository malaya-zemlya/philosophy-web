*No-self* (Pali *anattā*, Sanskrit *anātman*) is the Buddhist teaching that there is no enduring self,
no permanent soul or inner owner of experience, behind the changing flow of bodily and mental events that
make up a life. It is one of the "three marks" that Buddhism attributes to all conditioned things, along
with impermanence and unsatisfactoriness, and it sets Buddhism apart from almost every other classical
Indian school, nearly all of which affirmed a self (*ātman*). The position commits to the claim that
[there is no enduring self over and above the stream of events](../claims/no-enduring-self.md). What we call a
person is a causally connected series, and "I" is a useful label for it rather than the name of a further
thing [sep-mind-indian-buddhism](../sources/sep-mind-indian-buddhism.md), [collins-1982](../sources/collins-1982.md).

### The five aggregates

Buddhist analysis divides a person into five *aggregates* (*skandhas*): physical form (*rūpa*), feeling
(*vedanā*, the pleasant, painful or neutral tone of experience), perception or recognition (*saṃjñā*),
volitional formations (*saṃskāra*, including intentions and habits), and consciousness (*vijñāna*, the
bare awareness of an object through one of the senses or the mind). In the early *Discourse on the
Characteristic of Not-Self*, the Buddha takes each aggregate in turn and argues that it cannot be the
self, because it is impermanent, because it brings suffering, and because we cannot control it, as we
should be able to control a self that was truly our own. Since there is nothing in a person besides the
aggregates, there is nothing that is the self.

The most famous illustration is the *chariot* in the *Questions of King Milinda*
[milindapanha](../sources/milindapanha.md). The monk Nāgasena asks the king whether his chariot is the pole, the axle, the
wheels or the frame, and the king denies each in turn. Nor is it something apart from these parts. "The
chariot" is a name used for the parts assembled in a certain way, and the same holds for the name
"Nāgasena": it designates the aggregates, not a further entity they belong to.

### Conventional and ultimate truth

The developed form of the view, in the Abhidharma schools, distinguishes two levels of truth
[sep-abhidharma](../sources/sep-abhidharma.md). At the *conventional* level, persons, chariots and forests exist and can be
spoken of truly. At the *ultimate* level, only the momentary, impersonal events (*dharmas*) into which
they can be analysed exist. Mark Siderits calls this *Buddhist reductionism* and compares it with Derek
Parfit's view that a person's existence consists in nothing more than the existence of a brain and body
and the occurrence of a series of connected physical and mental events [siderits-2003](../sources/siderits-2003.md),
[parfit-1984](../sources/parfit-1984.md). The comparison runs both ways: Parfit himself noted that the Buddha would have
agreed with him.

The doctrine is also a practical one. Belief in a self is held to be the root of craving and attachment,
and so of suffering. Seeing that there is no self to protect or gratify is part of the path to
liberation (*nirvāṇa*).

### Rebirth without a transmigrating self

Buddhism also teaches rebirth, and critics, both Indian and modern, asked how rebirth is possible if
there is no self to be reborn. The Buddhist answer is that rebirth is continuity of a causal series, not
the transfer of an entity. In the *Questions of King Milinda*, Nāgasena compares it to lighting one lamp
from another: the second flame is neither the same as the first nor wholly different, since it arises in
dependence on it. Karma carries over as the causal influence of earlier events on later ones in the same
stream.

### Mind without matter

Denying a self did not make Buddhism materialist. The Buddhist logician Dharmakīrti (7th century)
argued against the Cārvāka view that consciousness arises from the body. Each moment of consciousness,
he held, must have as its main cause a prior moment of consciousness, since matter and mind are too
unlike for one to be the substantial cause of the other; so the mental stream cannot begin with the
body, and does not end with it [taber-2003](../sources/taber-2003.md). The argument, which supports rebirth, combines a
denial of the soul with a denial of physicalism, and resembles the modern case for panpsychism that
experience cannot emerge from the wholly non-experiential (see [genetic-panpsychism](../arguments/genetic-panpsychism.md)).

### The personalist dissent

Not all Buddhists accepted the full reduction. The *Pudgalavāda* or "personalist" schools held that the
person (*pudgala*) is real, neither identical with the aggregates nor separate from them, and that only
such a person could make sense of memory, responsibility and rebirth. Vasubandhu's refutation of the
person, the ninth chapter of his *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*, is the classic reply
[vasubandhu-abhidharmakosa](../sources/vasubandhu-abhidharmakosa.md): whatever is neither identical with the aggregates nor distinct from
them is, he argues, merely a conceptual construction.

### Objections

The standard objection, pressed by the Hindu schools of Nyāya and Vedānta, is that experience requires a
subject. Memory, recognition and the unity of experience at a moment seem to need a single thing that
remembers, recognizes and experiences; a mere series of events cannot remember its own earlier members.
Buddhists reply that the series contains the right causal connections, so that a later moment can carry
the memory of an earlier one, and that the felt unity of experience is itself one of the events in the
stream rather than evidence of a further owner. A second objection is moral: if there is no enduring
self, it is unclear why anyone should be responsible for past deeds or concerned about future suffering.
Buddhists answer that responsibility and concern follow the causal series, and Parfit drew a similar
conclusion, that what matters in survival is psychological continuity rather than identity
([identity-not-what-matters](../claims/identity-not-what-matters.md)).

### In plain terms

Buddhism teaches that there is no permanent "you" behind your experiences. Look for the self and you find
only a body, feelings, perceptions, impulses and moments of awareness, all constantly changing. "You" is a
name for that changing bundle, the way "chariot" is a name for wheels, axle and frame put together, not a
further thing hiding inside. This was meant as a practical insight, since believing in a fixed self is
thought to be the root of craving and suffering. Buddhism also teaches rebirth, and explains that what
continues is not a soul but a chain of causes, like a flame passed from one candle to the next. Critics
ask how a mere bundle could remember things or be responsible for what it did.
