*Emergence* is the appearance, in a complex whole, of properties that none of its parts has and that
seem to be more than the sum of the parts. A traffic jam can travel backwards along a motorway while
every car in it moves forwards. Wetness belongs to water, but not to any single H₂O molecule. In the
philosophy of mind the question is whether consciousness is emergent in the same way, arising from
neurons none of which is conscious, and if so in which sense of "emergent".

### Weak and strong emergence

The word covers two very different ideas, which David Chalmers separates as *weak* and *strong*
emergence ([chalmers-2006](../sources/chalmers-2006.md)).

A phenomenon is *weakly emergent* when it arises from the low-level facts in a way that is
surprising or hard to predict, but can still be derived from those facts in principle. The traffic
jam is an example, and so are the gliders and other moving patterns in the Game of Life, a simple grid-based simulation,
which follow from its few simple rules even though no one would guess them from the rules alone.
Weak emergence fits easily with physicalism and with reduction. It is a claim about how hard
something is to predict or explain, not about anything extra in the world.

A phenomenon is *strongly emergent* when its truths cannot be derived even in principle from the
truths of the low-level domain, so that complete knowledge of the parts and their laws would still
leave it out. C. D. Broad's emergentism took this form ([broad-1925](../sources/broad-1925.md)). On his view, the
behaviour of certain complexes could not be deduced even from complete knowledge of their parts and
of how those parts behave in other combinations, so special *trans-ordinal* laws were needed to link
the levels. Strong emergence is incompatible with reduction, and in its usual form with physicalism.
Chalmers argues that consciousness is the one clear candidate for strong emergence in nature.

### Emergence and consciousness

Strong emergence offers one way to take consciousness to be both real and irreducible without making
it a separate substance. Experience would arise from suitably organised matter by laws of its own.
That is close to [property-dualism](../positions/property-dualism.md) and to the reply recorded in
[strong-emergence-acceptable](../arguments/strong-emergence-acceptable.md), which defends the emergence of experience against the need
for fundamental mentality ([strong-emergence-is-acceptable](../claims/strong-emergence-is-acceptable.md)).

Two objections dominate. Galen Strawson argues that experience could not emerge from wholly
non-experiential ingredients, because genuine emergence must be intelligible in terms of what it
emerges from, and nothing about non-experiential physics makes experience intelligible
([no-radical-emergence](../claims/no-radical-emergence.md), [strawson-2006](../sources/strawson-2006.md)). He takes this as a reason for
[panpsychism](../positions/panpsychism.md). The second objection concerns causation. If an emergent property has new
powers of its own, it must sometimes push matter around in ways physics alone does not predict, which
conflicts with the [causal-closure](causal-closure.md) of the physical. If it has no new powers, it seems to
be idle, the problem raised by [causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md).

### In plain terms

Something is "emergent" when a whole has features none of its parts have, as a crowd can panic
although no single person is a panic. Sometimes this is just a matter of complexity: given enough
computing power you could predict the crowd from the people. That is weak emergence, and nobody finds
it mysterious. Strong emergence is the bolder idea that some features of a whole could not be worked
out even in principle from its parts. Some philosophers think consciousness is like this, arising from
brain cells by laws of its own. Critics doubt that anything really works that way.
