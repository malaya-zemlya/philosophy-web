*Extended consciousness* is the thesis that the physical processes on which conscious experience
depends, and which carry its content, are not always confined to the brain but can include the body
and parts of the surrounding world. It extends the [extended-mind](extended-mind.md) thesis from cognition,
such as belief, memory and calculation, to experience itself.

### Sources of the idea

The thesis has two main roots. Susan Hurley argued against the picture of the mind as a "sandwich" in
which perception is input, action is output and consciousness is the filling, and defended *vehicle
externalism*: the processes that carry conscious content need not stop at the skull
([hurley-1998](../sources/hurley-1998.md)). Alva Noë, developing a sensorimotor account of perception, argues that
experience is something an animal does in skilful engagement with its environment, as dancing is
something a body does in a room, and not an event inside the head ([noe-2009](../sources/noe-2009.md)). Mark Rowlands
defends an extended account of experience within a broader theory of embodied and extended mind
([rowlands-2010](../sources/rowlands-2010.md)). On these views the richness of visual experience, for instance, is supplied
by the world one can explore with the eyes, not by a detailed inner picture.

### The case of the notebook

The extended mind thesis is usually illustrated with a notebook, and the notebook shows clearly why
the further step to consciousness is contested. Consider a mathematician such as Gauss together with
the diary in which he recorded his discoveries ([gray-1984](../sources/gray-1984.md)). The diary may well be part of
his mathematical memory. But an entry on a page he is not reading is not at that moment part of his
experience; it becomes experienced only when he reads it, and then by way of perception, as any page
in front of him would. The diary works like long-term memory, a store of standing states, while
consciousness concerns what is occurrently available. Defenders of extended consciousness accordingly
rest their case less on notebooks than on tightly coupled perception and action, where the loop
between organism and world runs fast enough to look like part of the experience itself.

### The live dispute

Several leading defenders of the extended mind deny that consciousness extends. Andy Clark argues that
conscious experience requires fast, high-bandwidth integration that external loops cannot supply
([clark-2009](../sources/clark-2009.md)), and David Chalmers that conscious states require a direct access to
information that external resources reached through perception lack ([chalmers-2019](../sources/chalmers-2019.md)).
Karina Vold replies that these are contingent differences of speed and bandwidth, not a principled
barrier, and that the parity reasoning behind the extended mind carries over to consciousness
([vold-2015](../sources/vold-2015.md)). The dispute is set out in [extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness](../claims/extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness.md).

The question has a counterpart in artificial systems. A language model attends to its whole context
at every step, so the reasoning it has written down is not consulted like a notebook page but re-read
in full each time, which makes its context window more like working memory than like Otto's notebook.
Yet what stays in that context is decided by the software that runs the model, so the boundary between
the model's active workspace and an external store is set from outside it
([cot-bears-access-signature](../claims/cot-bears-access-signature.md), [cot-serial-verbal-workspace](../claims/cot-serial-verbal-workspace.md)). Whether a workspace of
that kind could be a vehicle of consciousness is one form of the general question of where a conscious
subject's boundary lies ([boundary-problem](boundary-problem.md)).

### In plain terms

Many philosophers accept that a notebook can be part of your *memory*. Extended consciousness is the
bolder idea that your *experience* itself, what it is like for you right now, can be partly made of
things outside your head. Its defenders point to how seeing works: you take in a detailed scene by
looking around it, not by storing a detailed picture inside. Its critics say that whatever is
outside reaches you too slowly and indirectly to be part of experience: a page in your notebook isn't
part of what you are experiencing until you read it.
