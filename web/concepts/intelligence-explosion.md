An *intelligence explosion* is the hypothesized runaway process in which a machine clever enough
to improve its own design produces a better designer, which in turn produces one better still,
and so on — each generation more capable at the very task of building minds — until the process
cascades far past human ability into [superintelligence](superintelligence.md). The idea is not merely that
machines might one day be smart, but that the making of intelligence could feed on itself, so that
crossing a certain threshold sets off a self-accelerating climb rather than a steady one.

### Good's original statement

The notion was first set out by the statistician I. J. Good in 1966 ([good-1966](../sources/good-1966.md)). Good
defined an *ultraintelligent machine* as one that "can far surpass all the intellectual activities
of any man however clever." Because designing machines is itself one of those intellectual
activities, he reasoned, such a machine "could design even better machines; there would then
unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far
behind." His famous conclusion draws out the stakes: "the first ultraintelligent machine is the
last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how
to keep it under control." The closing proviso — docility, controllability — is where the safety
concern is already latent in the founding text.

### The structure of the inference

Later philosophical work reconstructs Good's leap as a chain of conditionals rather than a single
intuition. In David Chalmers's canonical analysis ([chalmers-2010](../sources/chalmers-2010.md)), the load-bearing
premise is a *proportionality* idea: an increase in a system's intelligence tends to yield a
roughly proportionate increase in its capacity to design still greater intelligence. Grant that,
add that there is no near ceiling on how far the climb can go, and the explosion follows. Stated
this plainly, the inference also shows where it can be resisted — whether gains really compound,
or whether each step is harder than the last. Those are live disputes, argued out in
[proportionality-thesis](../arguments/proportionality-thesis.md) and [diminishing-returns-objection](../arguments/diminishing-returns-objection.md); the concept
itself only names the shape of the reasoning, not its verdict.

### Takeoff speed

A crucial parameter of the scenario is its *takeoff speed*: how long the climb from roughly
human-level to radically superhuman ability would take. A **fast** (or "hard") takeoff unfolds
over hours to days; a **slow** (or "soft") takeoff over years to decades ([bostrom-2014](../sources/bostrom-2014.md)).
The distinction is not academic bookkeeping. Takeoff speed largely decides whether a
misspecified goal could be noticed and corrected while the system is still within human reach: a
slow takeoff leaves room to observe, test, and intervene, whereas a fast takeoff could carry a
flawed objective past the point of correction before anyone acts. This is the concept's live
connection to [misaligned-superintelligence](../questions/misaligned-superintelligence.md) — the danger there turns on how much
warning the explosion would give.

### The skeptical view

Not everyone grants that intelligence is the sort of thing that can explode. One influential
objection holds that intelligence is bottlenecked by an agent's environment, the data available
to it, and the sheer time real-world experiments take, rather than by the speed of the thinker —
so recursive self-improvement would meet diminishing returns instead of runaway growth
([chollet-2017](../sources/chollet-2017.md)). The full case is developed in
[intelligence-explosion-self-limiting](../claims/intelligence-explosion-self-limiting.md); here it marks only that the concept names a
hypothesis, not an established fact.

### A concrete analogy

The characteristic signature of the idea is *compounding feedback*, and the cleanest illustration
is a nuclear chain reaction. One fission releases neutrons that trigger further fissions, each of
which releases more neutrons still; below a critical mass the reaction fizzles, but above it the
rate of reactions climbs on itself and the release becomes explosive. An intelligence explosion
posits the same structure in the domain of design: each improvement in a mind's ability to build
minds raises the rate at which further improvements arrive, so that past a critical threshold the
capability grows on its own output rather than by outside effort. Whether minds actually have a
"critical mass" of this kind is exactly what the surrounding debate contests.
