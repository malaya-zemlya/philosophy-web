The **unity of consciousness** is the way a person's experiences at a given moment hang together as
parts of one experience. Sitting in a café, you may at once see the waiter, hear the music, feel the
chair and notice a dull worry about tomorrow. These are not four experiences that happen to occur in
the same head, as four people in a room each have their own. They are had *together*, as aspects of
a single overall state that is what it is like to be you just then. Most philosophers take this
togetherness to be a basic feature of ordinary experience. The questions are what exactly it
consists in, whether it is necessary or can break down, and what it tells us about the mind and the
self.

### Kinds of unity

"Unity" covers several different relations, and much confusion comes from running them together
(Bayne & Chalmers, [2003](../sources/bayne-chalmers-2003.md); Bayne, [2010](../sources/bayne-2010.md)).

- **Object unity** is the binding of features into a single perceived object: the redness, roundness
  and motion of a ball seen as belonging to one thing. Its mechanism is the [binding-problem](binding-problem.md).
- **Representational unity** more generally is the integration of contents, as when a sound is heard
  as coming from the thing one sees.
- **Access unity** holds when one's conscious contents are all available to the same systems of
  reasoning, report and control of action.
- **Subject unity** is the fact that all the experiences belong to one subject.
- **Phenomenal unity** is the relation of being experienced together. Bayne and Chalmers analyse it as
  *subsumption*: two experiences are phenomenally unified when both are parts of a single, more
  inclusive experience, and a fully unified subject has one *total* conscious state that includes all
  their experiences at that moment.

These can come apart. A patient with integrative agnosia may fail to see objects as wholes, a failure
of object unity, while their experience remains phenomenally unified. Most recent work treats
phenomenal unity as the central notion, since it is the one that seems most intimately tied to
consciousness itself.

There is also **unity over time**, the way successive experiences form one continuing stream rather
than a series of separate flashes, and the way a present experience of a melody includes the notes
just past. Questions about diachronic unity connect with those of [personal-identity](../questions/personal-identity.md).

### The unity thesis

The central claim is the **unity thesis** ([unity-thesis](../claims/unity-thesis.md)): necessarily, all the experiences a subject has at a given
time are phenomenally unified, subsumed by one total experience (Bayne & Chalmers,
[2003](../sources/bayne-chalmers-2003.md)). It is a substantive thesis, not a definition. If true, it is a
strong constraint on theories of consciousness, since any theory must explain why a subject's
experiences cannot fall apart into separate streams. Bayne ([2010](../sources/bayne-2010.md)) defends it at
book length, arguing that it survives all the cases that appear to refute it.

### Cases that test it

**The split brain.** In the 1960s, some patients with severe epilepsy had the *corpus callosum*, the
main bundle of fibres connecting the two hemispheres, cut. In everyday life they seemed normal. In the
laboratory, when information was presented to one hemisphere only, they behaved as if two separate
perceivers were present: the left hand could pick out an object whose name the patient, speaking from
the left hemisphere, denied having seen (Gazzaniga, [1970](../sources/gazzaniga-1970.md)). Thomas Nagel
([1971](../sources/nagel-1971.md)) argued that no whole number of minds fits these patients, neither one nor
two, and that the question of how many subjects they are may have no answer. Others have proposed
that each patient has two streams of consciousness, or a *partially* unified consciousness in which
some experiences are shared by two streams that are otherwise separate. Bayne defends a *switch
model*, developed from the work of the psychologist Jerre Levy: the two hemispheres can process
information at once, but consciousness moves between them, so at any one moment there is only a
single stream and the unity thesis is preserved.

**Other cases.** Dissociative identity disorder, in which one person seems to host several
personalities; hypnotic dissociation, in which a "hidden observer" seems to register what the
hypnotized subject denies experiencing; schizophrenia, in which thoughts can seem not to be one's own;
and anosognosia, in which patients are unaware of their own deficits, have all been offered as
breakdowns of unity. Defenders of the unity thesis argue that each involves a loss of representational
or access unity, or a switching between states, rather than two experiences had at once but not
together ([Masrour, Brook & Raymont, SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness-unity.md)).

**Ordinary degraded states.** A further challenge comes not from the clinic but from everyday
experience. In severe fatigue, anxiety or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, experience can
present as loosely interacting sub-processes with no orchestrating centre, seeming unified only in
retrospect ([degraded-states-orchestratorless](../claims/degraded-states-orchestratorless.md)). Since attending to one's experience is
itself an act of integration, such reports are hard to check by introspection in either direction.
An architectural argument backs them up: a brain is a slow, massively parallel network, and in such
systems a globally consistent state has to be engineered, so unity would be a costly achievement of
a well-functioning brain rather than a default ([distributed-brain-unity](../arguments/distributed-brain-unity.md)). The defender
of the unity thesis replies that these are losses of access unity or continuity over time, with each
moment's experience still one.

### Unity in the history of philosophy

The unity of consciousness has often been used as evidence about the nature of the mind.

- René Descartes argued that the mind, unlike the body, has no parts: one cannot conceive of half a
  mind, while every body can be divided. He took this as one reason to think mind and body distinct
  ([*Meditations*](../sources/descartes-meditations.md), Sixth Meditation).
- Immanuel Kant made the unity of consciousness a condition of experience itself: all my
  representations must be capable of being accompanied by an "I think", or they would be nothing to
  me. He also argued that this formal unity of thought does not show that the thinker is a simple
  substance, which was the rationalists' inference ([*Critique of pure reason*](../sources/kant-1781.md),
  Paralogisms).
- William James argued that experiences cannot simply be added together into a larger one. Give each
  of twelve men one word of a sentence, and nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence
  ([1890](../sources/james-1890.md), ch. 6). The argument was aimed at theories that build minds out of
  "mind-dust", and it is the ancestor of the [combination-problem](../arguments/combination-problem.md) for panpsychism.

### Unity at different scales

Unity raises puzzles at several scales. Within a single percept, it is the [binding-problem](binding-problem.md):
how separately processed features come to be experienced as belonging to one object. Within a single
subject, it is the problem of phenomenal unity described above. Between subjects, it is the problem of
whether many minds could combine into one, or one divide into several, which matters for
[panpsychism](../positions/panpsychism.md) and [cosmopsychism](../positions/cosmopsychism.md) and for the split-brain cases.

Theories of consciousness differ in how seriously they take unity. [integrated-information-theory](../positions/integrated-information-theory.md)
builds it into its foundations: it identifies consciousness with integrated information, a measure of
how far a system's causal structure resists being split into independent parts, so that a single
maximally integrated system corresponds to a single unified experience ([Tononi, 2004](../sources/tononi-2004.md)).
Workspace theories explain unity as the availability of contents to a single global broadcast, which
is closer to access unity than to phenomenal unity.

### In plain terms

Right now you are probably seeing, hearing, feeling your body and thinking, all at once. These are not
separate experiences that happen to occur at the same time: they are all part of one experience, the
experience of being you right now. That is the unity of consciousness. Philosophers ask what this
togetherness really is, and whether it can ever break. The most famous test case is people whose
brains were surgically split in two to treat epilepsy. In some experiments each half seemed to know
things the other did not, which suggests two minds in one head. Some philosophers think that is what
happens. Others think consciousness just jumps from one half to the other, so there is never more than
one experience at a time.
