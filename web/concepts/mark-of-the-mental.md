A *mark of the mental* is a feature that all mental phenomena have and nothing else has. It would be
a single test that sorts beliefs, desires, pains, perceptions and moods onto one side of a line and
stones, storms and chemical reactions onto the other. The phrase comes from Franz Brentano, who
proposed that the mark is *intentionality*, the way a mental state is directed at or about something
([brentano-1874](../sources/brentano-1874.md)). Whether any such mark exists is still disputed. The question matters
because the [mind–body problem](mind-body-problem.md) assumes we know which things count as
mental, and every proposed criterion either leaves out something we call mental or lets in something
we do not.

The search has two sides. A criterion for the mental is only half of the distinction, and saying what
makes something *physical* turns out to be just as hard ([physical](physical.md)). This entry covers the
mental side. It is distinct from [intentionality](intentionality.md), which defines one candidate criterion,
and from [mind-body-problem](mind-body-problem.md), which asks how the mental relates to the physical once the two
are told apart. Background on the intentionality literature is in [sep-intentionality](../sources/sep-intentionality.md).

### Non-extension

The oldest modern answer is Descartes's. In the *Meditations* the mind is a *thinking thing* and the
body an *extended* thing, defined by size, shape and motion in space ([descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md)).
On this view the mark of the mental is that it lacks spatial extent. A neuron has a location and a
diameter, but it seems senseless to ask how many millimetres wide a thought about Paris is, or which
way it faces. Descartes also held that whatever is in the mind is transparent to it, so his criterion
combined non-extension with immediate awareness.

Non-extension fits [substance-dualism](../positions/substance-dualism.md) naturally, but it begs the question against the
physicalist. If thoughts are brain processes they do have locations, and the oddity of asking for a
thought's width may reflect only how we talk about thoughts. Some experiences also seem to be located
somewhere: a pain is felt in the foot, and visual experience is laid out in a spatial field. Brentano
considered non-extension and judged it too disputed to serve as the defining mark.

### Intentionality

Brentano's own proposal was that every mental phenomenon "includes something as object within itself". To
believe is to believe something, to fear is to fear something, and to hear is to hear a sound
([brentano-1874](../sources/brentano-1874.md)). He called this the *intentional inexistence* of an object, meaning its
existence "in" the act of mind. Physical phenomena, he held, have nothing of the kind: a stone is not
about anything ([intentionality](intentionality.md)). *Brentano's thesis* is usually split in two. The first
part says that all mental phenomena are intentional, and the second that no physical phenomenon is.

Intentionality has a feature that makes it look unlike any physical relation. One can think about
things that do not exist, such as Pegasus or a promotion that never comes. Physical relations need
both relata: nobody can kick a horse that does not exist, though anyone can imagine one. Roderick
Chisholm restated the thesis in terms of language ([chisholm-1957](../sources/chisholm-1957.md)). Sentences reporting
mental states behave oddly in logic. From "Ann is looking for the fountain of youth" it does not
follow that there is a fountain of youth. From "Ann believes the morning star is bright" it does not
follow that she believes the evening star is bright, though they are the same planet. Sentences
reporting physical facts lack these peculiarities. This logical property is called *intensionality*
(with an *s*), and Chisholm offered it as a test for the mental that does not rely on introspection.

Both halves of the thesis face objections. Against the first half, pains, itches and moods such as
undirected anxiety or elation seem to be about nothing. A headache is not a headache *of* anything.
Defenders reply that sensations do have objects. A pain represents a disturbance in a part of the body,
and a mood represents the world as a whole in a certain light. Tim Crane defends Brentano's thesis on
these lines ([crane-1998](../sources/crane-1998.md)), and [representationalism](../positions/representationalism.md) makes a stronger claim of the
same kind, that the felt quality of an experience just is what the experience represents
([dretske-1995](../sources/dretske-1995.md), [tye-2000](../sources/tye-2000.md)).

A further objection concerns thought itself, the case Brentano's thesis seems to fit best. Not every
episode of thinking is formed and focused enough to be about anything definite. When someone drifts
toward sleep, is very tired or drunk, or lets the mind go slack, what passes through it can be
fragmentary: half-images, the rhythm of a sentence with no proposition attached, an urge that never
settles on an object. Contemplative traditions go further and report states of wakeful awareness with
no object at all. Robert Forman collected such reports under the name *pure consciousness event*
([forman-1990](../sources/forman-1990.md)), and Thomas Metzinger has analysed meditative "pure awareness" as a
candidate for *minimal phenomenal experience*, consciousness stripped of ordinary object-directed
content ([metzinger-2020](../sources/metzinger-2020.md)). The intentionalist can reply in two ways. One reply holds that a
blurred thought still has content, only vague or indeterminate content, much as a blurred photograph
is still a photograph of something. The other grants that the states are objectless but denies that
they are thoughts, classing them as bare awareness. Each reply is costly. The first must explain what
a content is when no one, including the thinker, could say what it is. The second concedes that some
mental states lack aboutness, which is to give up the first half of the thesis. Since these states are
plainly conscious, they also count in favour of the consciousness criterion discussed below. They suggest
a different picture, on which aboutness is something thought achieves to a greater or lesser degree
rather than a property every mental state has.

Against the second half, many physical things seem to be about something. A map is about a city, a
sentence about its subject, and the rings of a tree carry information about its age. The usual reply
distinguishes *intrinsic* from *derived* intentionality ([intrinsic-derived-intentionality](intrinsic-derived-intentionality.md)).
A map means something only because people use it to mean something, so its aboutness is borrowed from
minds ([searle-1992](../sources/searle-1992.md)). A naturalist who explains content in terms of information or
biological function denies the second half outright and treats intentionality as a physical relation
of a special kind ([dretske-1988](../sources/dretske-1988.md)). For such a naturalist, intentionality can still mark out
the mental, but no longer as something non-physical. How a physical system could be about anything is
the [problem of intentionality](../questions/intentionality.md).

### Consciousness

A second family of answers locates the mark in *consciousness*, the fact that there is something it is
like to be in the state ([phenomenal-consciousness](phenomenal-consciousness.md), [qualia](qualia.md)). This criterion
captures the pains and moods that intentionality seemed to miss, but it loses unconscious beliefs and
desires. You believed that the Atlantic is wet before you read this sentence, yet the belief was not
present to consciousness. Cognitive science also attributes unconscious mental processing to perception
and language.

Two responses keep consciousness central. John Searle's *connection principle* holds that an
unconscious state is mental only if it is the kind of state that could in principle become conscious
([searle-1992](../sources/searle-1992.md)). The *phenomenal intentionality* programme argues that genuine
intentionality itself derives from conscious experience ([horgan-tienson-2002](../sources/horgan-tienson-2002.md),
[intentionality-grounded-in-phenomenality](../claims/intentionality-grounded-in-phenomenality.md)). If that is right, the two criteria merge, and
consciousness is the deeper mark.

### Privileged access

A third answer is epistemic. Each of us knows our own mental states in a special way: directly,
without inference or observation, and with an authority others lack ([first-person-access](first-person-access.md),
[privileged-first-person-access](../claims/privileged-first-person-access.md)). Physical facts are public and anyone can check them in the
same way. Richard Rorty argued that what makes a state paradigmatically mental is that first-person
reports of it are treated as *incorrigible*, so that no one else is in a position to overrule them. He
added that this status is a social practice that a mature neuroscience might one day change
([rorty-1970](../sources/rorty-1970.md)).

The epistemic criterion is vulnerable because self-knowledge turns out to be fallible. People
confidently report reasons for their choices that experiments show played no role
([confabulation](confabulation.md)), and introspection of one's own experience can be unreliable
([sep-introspection](../sources/sep-introspection.md)). The criterion also says nothing about unconscious states, which by
definition are not known in this way.

### Location and the extended mind

A naive criterion puts the mental inside the head and the physical everywhere. The *extended mind*
thesis challenges this. Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue that an external resource, such as a
notebook that a man with memory loss consults as others consult memory, can be part of his cognitive
system and can store his beliefs ([extended-mind](extended-mind.md), [clark-chalmers-1998](../sources/clark-chalmers-1998.md)). Whatever
one thinks of the thesis, it shows that location inside the skull cannot be what makes a state mental.

### Is there a single mark?

Many philosophers now doubt that one feature unifies the mental. David Chalmers distinguishes a
*psychological* concept of mind, defined by what a state does in the cognitive economy, from a
*phenomenal* concept, defined by how it feels ([chalmers-1996](../sources/chalmers-1996.md)). The two concepts sort cases
differently and raise different problems ([access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness](access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness.md)). Rorty went
further and argued that intentional states and felt sensations have nothing deep in common. On his
view the idea of the mind as one realm is a historical construction, largely owed to Descartes, and
not a natural kind awaiting a definition ([rorty-1979](../sources/rorty-1979.md)). Gilbert Ryle's earlier attack on the
"ghost in the machine" made a related point: the picture of mental and physical as two parallel worlds
rests on a confusion about how mental concepts work ([ryle-1949](../sources/ryle-1949.md)). A middle position treats
"mental" as a *cluster concept*. Intentionality, consciousness and privileged access usually go
together in the clearest cases, no one of them is necessary, and the boundary cases are left to theory.

The answer shapes the mind–body problem itself. If intentionality is the mark, the central puzzle is
how meaning fits into nature. If consciousness is the mark, the central puzzle is the
[hard-problem](hard-problem.md). If there is no single mark, there may be several mind–body problems rather
than one.

### In plain terms

We sort things into "mental" (thoughts, feelings, wishes, pains) and "physical" (rocks, cells,
electricity) without much hesitation. Philosophers have tried to say exactly what the mental things
have in common, and each answer runs into trouble. "Mental things take up no space" seems to fit
thoughts, but a pain is felt in a particular spot. "Mental things are always *about* something" fits
beliefs and fears, but a plain headache is not about anything. A drowsy or drunken thought may be too
unformed to be about anything definite, and meditators report awareness with no object at all. In the
other direction, a map is about a city without having a mind. "Mental things are felt" fits pains, but you hold countless beliefs you aren't thinking about
right now. "You know your own mind better than anyone else" is challenged by experiments showing that
people often misjudge why they did what they did. Some philosophers conclude that "mental" isn't a
single kind of thing at all, just a bundle of related features.
