Every representation has two sides. Its *content* is what it represents: the state of affairs a
sentence describes, the scene a picture depicts, the object a perception is of. Its *vehicle* is the
physical thing that does the representing: the ink marks, the paint on the canvas, the pattern of
neural activity. The *content/vehicle distinction* is the observation that the two have different
properties and that the properties of one need not be shared by the other. The word "long" is short.
A huge painting can depict a microscopic object. Someone can shout "softly, on tiptoe" at the top of
their voice ([Dennett & Kinsbourne, 1992](../sources/dennett-kinsbourne-1992.md)).

### The two can conflict

The two sides of a sign are processed separately, and they can pull against each other. In the
*Stroop effect*, people are asked to name the colour of the ink in which a colour word is printed.
They are noticeably slower when the word names a different colour, as when "red" is printed in green,
than when it does not ([Stroop, 1935](../sources/stroop-1935.md)). The colour of the ink belongs to the
vehicle and the colour named belongs to the content. The task asks about one, and the other gets in
the way.

### The content/vehicle confusion

The distinction is easy to state and easy to forget. Ruth Millikan ([1993](../sources/millikan-1993.md))
opens her study of the *content/vehicle confusion* with her small daughter, who woke in her mother's
bed and announced, "Mommy, I saw your dream!" The dream was taken to be the mother's because it
happened in the mother's bed, so a property of where the dream occurred was mixed up with a property
of what it was about. Millikan distinguishes two directions of the mistake. *Content internalizing*
projects properties that a perception attributes to the world onto the inner vehicle that carries
it. *Content externalizing* runs the other way, reading properties of the vehicle into what is
perceived, as if one could argue from the nature of the representation to what it must represent.
She argues that both moves survive in sophisticated forms among philosophers who explicitly reject
inner pictures, and that they apply equally to sense data, neural states and perceptual capacities.
Daniel Dennett ([1991](../sources/dennett-1991.md)) is the other standard source for the warning, and
Susan Hurley ([1998](../sources/hurley-1998.md)) built on the distinction to argue that the vehicles of
conscious content need not be confined to the head, a view known as *vehicle externalism*.

The confusion has a natural home in theories of perception. It is tempting to think that seeing a
smooth red surface requires a smooth red patch somewhere in the brain, or that the blind spot must be
"filled in" by neural activity standing in for the missing part of the scene. Dennett argued that this
does not follow. A representation of a uniform surface needs only to carry the content that the
surface is uniform, and the absence of any representation of a gap is not the same as a
representation that there is no gap. The same reasoning applies to time: a representation of A before
B need not consist of a representation of A followed by a representation of B. Millikan gives an
example from Gareth Evans, who assumed that a representation of simultaneous things must itself have
simultaneous parts, and that pulsing and continuous sensations must be represented by something
pulsing and continuous. Yet one can represent pulsing and continuousness in words, as this sentence
does, without anything pulsing at all ([Millikan, 1993](../sources/millikan-1993.md), p. 259;
[time-represented-vs-time-of-representing](time-represented-vs-time-of-representing.md)).

### A live dispute about experience

The distinction is at the centre of a dispute about the nature of conscious experience.
*Representationalists* hold that what an experience is like is fixed entirely by its content, by how
it represents the world to be ([phenomenal-character-is-representational](../claims/phenomenal-character-is-representational.md)). Gilbert Harman
([1990](../sources/harman-1990.md)) argued that when we introspect an experience we find only what it
represents, never any *mental paint*, his term for intrinsic properties of the experience itself.
He held that the contrary view confuses properties of what is represented with properties of the
vehicle that represents it, so representationalism presents itself as respecting the content/vehicle
distinction. Ned Block ([2003](../sources/block-2003b.md), pp. 172–173) rejects the charge of confusion and
defends the existence of mental paint. As the name suggests, a painting has properties of its paint
as well as of the scene it depicts, and Block argues that experience likewise has phenomenal
properties that its representational content does not exhaust, whether or not introspection gives
direct access to them. The dispute is therefore partly over whether the felt character of experience
belongs to content or to vehicle.

### Related distinctions

**Use and mention.** In language the distinction takes a special form when the vehicle itself becomes
the topic. One can *use* a word to talk about a thing ("Boston is a city") or *mention* it to talk
about the word ("'Boston' has six letters"). W. V. O. Quine ([1940](../sources/quine-1940.md)) gave the
classic statement of the distinction and of the convention of marking mention with quotation marks.
Confusing the two produces errors such as inferring that Boston has six letters.

**Self-reference and paradox.** Comparing a sign's vehicle with its content can lead to paradox.
In the [grelling-nelson-paradox](grelling-nelson-paradox.md), Kurt Grelling and Leonard Nelson ([1908](../sources/grelling-nelson-1908.md)) called an adjective
*autological* if it has the property it expresses ("short" is short, "English" is English) and
*heterological* if it does not ("long" is not long). Then they asked whether "heterological" is
heterological. If it is, it has the property it expresses, so it is autological. If it is not, it
lacks the property it expresses, so it is heterological. They presented this as a relative of the
paradox Bertrand Russell had found in set theory, which concerns the set of all sets that are not
members of themselves. Russell's own remedy, the theory of types ([1908](../sources/russell-1908.md)),
separates entities into levels so that nothing can be said of a totality to which it belongs.

These kinships should not be overstated. Russell's paradox arises from self-membership and does not
involve signs at all, and most cases of content/vehicle confusion involve no self-reference. What the
three ideas share is a structural lesson: a representation and what it represents occupy different
levels, and reasoning that moves between the levels without noticing produces error or, at the
limit, contradiction.

### In plain terms

A thing that stands for something else, like a word, a picture or a brain signal, has its own features
that are separate from the features of what it stands for. The word "long" is short. A billboard of an
ant is enormous. Mixing up the two is a common mistake, as when people assume that seeing red must
involve something red inside the head. Philosophers also argue about whether the way an experience
feels is just a matter of what it shows you, or partly a feature of the experience itself, like the
paint on a canvas as opposed to the scene it depicts.
