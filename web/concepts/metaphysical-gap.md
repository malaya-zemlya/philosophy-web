A **metaphysical gap** (or *ontological gap*) between the phenomenal and the physical would exist if
the facts about conscious experience were something over and above the physical facts: if a world
could be exactly like ours in every physical respect and yet differ in what, if anything, it is like
to be its inhabitants. The claim is about how the world is, not about what we know or can explain. If
there is such a gap, experience is not wholly fixed by the physical, and some form of
[dualism](../positions/dualism.md) (or a close relative) is true. If there is not, then however mysterious
experience looks from where we stand, the physical facts settle it completely, as
[physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md) holds.

The notion is narrower than the [mind-body-problem](mind-body-problem.md). That problem asks about the
whole relation between mind and body, including thought, intentionality and mental causation. The
metaphysical gap concerns one thesis about one part of the mind: whether phenomenal truths follow from
physical truths with metaphysical necessity.

### Explanatory, epistemic and metaphysical gaps

Three gaps are commonly distinguished, and much of the debate turns on how they are related.

The [explanatory-gap](explanatory-gap.md) is Joseph Levine's term ([1983](../sources/levine-1983.md)). Levine contrasted the
identity of pain with the firing of certain nerve fibres with scientific identities such as that of
heat with the mean kinetic energy of molecules. We pick out heat by what it does: it makes things
expand, melts ice, and causes sensations of warmth. Once we know that molecular motion does exactly
these things, the identity leaves nothing about heat unexplained, and there is no further question
why that motion should be heat. In the case of pain, by contrast, the role can be fully explained and a question still
remains: why should nerve firing of that kind *feel* like this, or like anything at all? Levine
presented the gap as an alternative to Saul Kripke's modal argument for dualism
([Kripke, 1980](../sources/kripke-1980.md)). Kripke had argued that the apparent contingency of the
pain–nerve identity shows the identity to be false. Levine held that the argument does not establish
a metaphysical conclusion, but that what remains of it is a real epistemological point, a failure of
explanation. He has continued to treat the gap as real while declining to infer that physicalism is
false ([Levine, 2001](../sources/levine-2001.md)).

The **epistemic gap**, in David Chalmers's terminology ([2003](../sources/chalmers-2003.md)), is the
claim that the phenomenal truths cannot be deduced a priori from the complete physical truth: no
amount of physical information would let one work out, by reasoning alone, what an experience is
like, or whether there is any. The thought experiments of the
[philosophical-zombie](philosophical-zombie.md) and of Mary in the [knowledge-argument](knowledge-argument.md) are
designed to show such a gap.

The **metaphysical gap** is the further claim that the phenomenal truths are not necessitated by the
physical truths at all, even in the a posteriori way that "water is H₂O" is necessary. An
epistemic gap concerns our concepts and what can be derived from them; a metaphysical gap concerns
what could have been otherwise. The two can come apart: Levine accepts the first and suspends judgment
on the second, and many physicalists accept the first and deny the second.

### From the epistemic gap to the metaphysical gap

The main argument for a metaphysical gap tries to bridge the two. In outline:

1. There is an epistemic gap: the phenomenal truths are not a priori deducible from the physical
   truths. (Equivalently, it is conceivable that the physical truths hold and the phenomenal truths
   do not, as in a zombie world.)
2. If there is an epistemic gap of this kind, there is a metaphysical gap: what is ideally
   conceivable is metaphysically possible ([modal-rationalism](../arguments/modal-rationalism.md)).
3. If there is a metaphysical gap, physicalism is false.

Chalmers ([1996](../sources/chalmers-1996.md), [2003](../sources/chalmers-2003.md)) develops this as the
[zombie-conceivability](../arguments/zombie-conceivability.md) argument, using a two-dimensional semantics
([Chalmers, 2002](../sources/chalmers-2002.md)) to explain why the ordinary cases of a posteriori necessity,
such as water and H₂O, do not provide counterexamples to step 2. In the water case, he argues, what is
conceivable is only that something *watery* other than H₂O fills the lakes, not that water itself is
not H₂O. In the case of consciousness, there is no corresponding distinction between how pain seems
and what it is, since the seeming is the experience itself, so the conceivability of zombies cannot be
explained away in the same manner. This last point descends from Kripke.

### Physicalist responses

Chalmers's taxonomy of [materialism-types](materialism-types.md) sorts physicalists by where they block
the argument.

- **Type-A** materialists ([type-a-materialism](../positions/type-a-materialism.md)) deny step 1. On
  reflection, once all the functions of consciousness are explained, nothing is left over, and zombies
  are not really conceivable. There is no epistemic gap and so no metaphysical gap to infer.
- **Type-B** materialists ([type-b-materialism](../positions/type-b-materialism.md)) grant step 1 and deny
  step 2. Phenomenal and physical properties are identical, but the identity is necessary a posteriori,
  and the gap in our knowledge has an explanation that reveals nothing about the world. The most common
  explanation is the *phenomenal concept strategy*: we think about experiences through special
  first-person concepts that are conceptually isolated from physical concepts, so the identity cannot
  be seen a priori even though it holds (Loar, [1990](../sources/loar-1990.md); Papineau,
  [2002](../sources/papineau-2002.md)). Block and Stalnaker ([1999](../sources/block-stalnaker-1999.md)) argue
  more generally that ordinary macro-truths are not a priori derivable from microphysics either, so the
  absence of an a priori bridge does not make consciousness special. These replies are collected as the
  [conceivability-not-guide-to-possibility](../arguments/conceivability-not-guide-to-possibility.md) objection.
- **Type-C** materialists ([type-c-materialism](../positions/type-c-materialism.md)) hold that the epistemic gap
  is real now but will close as science and concepts develop, so that step 1 will turn out false.
  A related view, associated with [mysterianism](../positions/mysterianism.md), holds that the gap will never
  close for us, because of the limits of human concepts, without being a gap in nature
  (McGinn, [1989](../sources/mcginn-1989.md)).

Some physicalists go further and argue that an explanatory gap is just what physicalism predicts,
since physically realized minds with limited, evolved ways of representing themselves should be
expected to find their own experience opaque ([Van Gulick, SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness.md)).

### If the gap is real

Accepting a metaphysical gap leaves several options, which differ on how experience fits the causal
order.

- **Interactionist dualism** treats phenomenal properties as non-physical and causally effective,
  at the cost of denying the [completeness-of-physics](completeness-of-physics.md).
- **[Epiphenomenalism](../positions/epiphenomenalism.md)** keeps physics complete by making phenomenal
  properties effects of the physical that cause nothing in turn.
- **[Russellian-monism](../positions/russellian-monism.md)** locates the gap between experience and the
  *structural* description that physics provides, and holds that experience, or something
  proto-phenomenal, is the intrinsic nature that the structure leaves open
  ([Chalmers, 2003](../sources/chalmers-2003.md)). On this view the gap is real with respect to physics
  but closed with respect to physical things, and [panpsychism](../positions/panpsychism.md) is one version
  of it.

The last option shows that whether there is a metaphysical gap depends on what counts as
[physical](physical.md). On a theory-based conception, Russellian monism affirms a gap; on an
object-based conception, which counts the intrinsic nature of ordinary objects as physical, it denies
one (Stoljar, [2001](../sources/stoljar-2001.md)). The same uncertainty about the physical that drives
[hempels-dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md) therefore affects the formulation of the gap.

### In plain terms

Imagine a perfect copy of you, atom for atom. Would it feel things the way you do, or could it be a
"zombie", acting just like you but with nobody home? Most people find they can at least *imagine*
the zombie, and nobody can explain how brain activity produces the feeling of pain, as opposed to the
behaviour of avoiding damage. That is a gap in our understanding. The *metaphysical* gap is a bolder
claim: that the gap is in reality itself, so that feelings really are something extra on top of all the
physical facts. Dualists say yes. Most physicalists say the gap is only in our understanding, the way
"water" and "H₂O" once seemed like two different things before chemistry showed they were one.
Whether that comparison works is the heart of the dispute.
