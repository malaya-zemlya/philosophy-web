The *meta-problem of consciousness* is the problem of explaining why we think that consciousness
poses a hard problem, or, in David Chalmers's other phrasing, why we think consciousness is hard to
explain ([Chalmers, 2018](../sources/chalmers-2018.md)). The [hard-problem](hard-problem.md) asks why physical
processing is accompanied by experience at all. The meta-problem asks instead about a fact of human
behaviour: people say things like "there is a hard problem of consciousness" and "explaining behaviour
does not explain experience", and these *problem reports*, and the *problem intuitions* behind them,
need explaining.

### A topic-neutral problem

What makes the meta-problem tractable is that it can be posed in *topic-neutral* terms, without
assuming that phenomenal consciousness exists. Reports are behaviour, and the judgments behind them are
cognitive states, so their causes can in principle be studied by the ordinary methods of psychology,
neuroscience and computational modelling. Chalmers sorts the problem intuitions into several kinds.
*Explanatory* intuitions say that consciousness resists physical explanation. *Metaphysical*
intuitions say that it is non-physical, as in the conceivability of
[zombies](philosophical-zombie.md). *Knowledge* intuitions say that we know about it in a special,
first-person way, as in the case of Mary the colour scientist. Chalmers allows that a realist may
eventually want to relax the topic-neutrality requirement, but holds that the meta-problem as such is
best posed without it (p. 19).

The meta-problem is the general form of an older puzzle, the
[paradox of phenomenal judgment](paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment.md) (Chalmers, 1996). If our
judgments about consciousness can be fully explained in physical and functional terms, the explanation
seems to make no essential reference to consciousness itself, and threatens to debunk the very beliefs
it explains.

### Relation to the illusion problem

Keith Frankish calls the problem of explaining why experience seems phenomenal the *illusion problem*
([Frankish, 2016](../sources/frankish-2016.md)). The name was suggested by Chalmers, who describes the
illusion problem as "a close relative" of the meta-problem: it is the version that arises once one adds
the thesis that consciousness is an illusion (pp. 8–9). The two share an explanatory target, the
seeming. They differ on what a solution shows.

- The meta-problem is *neutral*. A solution explains the problem intuitions but leaves open whether
  they are correct. In Chalmers's words, it "subsumes the illusion problem while being more general and
  more neutral" (p. 10).
- The illusion problem *presupposes* that the intuitions are false. Illusionists typically hold that
  solving the meta-problem will solve or dissolve the hard problem, since a physical explanation of why
  we seem to have non-physical properties lets those properties be dismissed as illusory
  ([phenomenality-is-illusion](../claims/phenomenality-is-illusion.md)).

A realist answers differently. Since judgments about consciousness systematically reflect the
character of consciousness, a realist should expect the mechanisms of consciousness to play a central
role in producing those judgments (p. 36). On that view a solution to the meta-problem is not the end
of the inquiry but a guide to the hard problem.

### How widespread the intuitions are

Whether the problem intuitions are shared outside philosophy is an empirical question, and Chalmers
notes that little experimental work has targeted them directly (p. 13). They are clearly not
universal at the level of reflective judgment, since some people reject each of them, and he holds that
a full solution to the meta-problem must explain both why they are widely shared and why they are not
universal (p. 14). The question matters to illusionism, since it bears on whether the supposed illusion
belongs to ordinary experience or to theorizing about it ([locus-of-illusion-dilemma](../arguments/locus-of-illusion-dilemma.md),
[folk-lack-phenomenal-concept](../claims/folk-lack-phenomenal-concept.md)).

### In plain terms

Many people feel that consciousness is deeply mysterious and could never be explained by brain science.
The meta-problem asks a different question: why do we feel that way? That question is about what
people think and say, so scientists can study it without first deciding whether consciousness is
something extra. Illusionists think answering it would settle everything, because the mystery would
turn out to be a trick of the brain. Others think the answer will lead back to consciousness itself.
