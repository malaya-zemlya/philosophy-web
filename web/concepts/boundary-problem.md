The *boundary problem* is the question of why conscious experience comes packaged in separate, sharply
bounded individuals, and what in nature decides where one experiencing subject ends and another begins.
Your experience right now has an edge. It includes the sights and sounds around you and the feeling of
your body, but it does not include the experience of the person sitting next to you. Nor do you and your
chair together form a larger experiencing being, or the cells of your brain each host a separate
subject. Yet the physical world shows no comparably sharp joints. Your brain is continuously coupled to
your body, your body to the air and the floor, and all of it to everything else by gravitational and
electromagnetic interaction. Something has to explain why the experiential world is carved into
discrete subjects at all, and why the cuts fall where they do. The problem was framed in detail by Gregg
Rosenberg [rosenberg-1998](../sources/rosenberg-1998.md), [rosenberg-2004](../sources/rosenberg-2004.md).

### The problem

Rosenberg posed the problem for any theory that connects consciousness to physical or informational
properties found throughout nature. Suppose experience is associated with some kind of information
processing or causal interaction. Such interaction occurs at every scale: within a neuron, between
neurons, between brain regions, between a brain and its environment, and between people in a
conversation. If the property that makes for experience is present at all these scales, there seem to be
two unwelcome options. Either experience is present at every level, so that every neuron, every brain
region and every crowd is a subject in its own right, or there is a single, unbounded experience
spreading across everything that interacts. Neither matches what we find, which is a definite number of
bounded subjects of roughly the size of a brain. The task is to say what principle, grounded in the
physics or the organization of the system, draws the edges in the right places.

David Chalmers lists the boundary problem as one aspect of the [combination-problem-panpsychism](combination-problem-panpsychism.md),
alongside the unity, awareness and grain problems [chalmers-2016](../sources/chalmers-2016.md). It is especially pressing
for panpsychism, which must say which collections of experiencing micro-entities make up a further
subject and which remain mere aggregates: why a brain is a subject while a rock, a hat or a nation
presumably is not. But the problem is not confined to panpsychism. Tomer Fekete, Cees van Leeuwen and
Shimon Edelman show that computational and information-based theories face it too
[fekete-2016](../sources/fekete-2016.md). A measure of consciousness that applies to a whole brain will typically also
apply, to some degree, to its subsystems and to larger systems containing it, and so threatens to
multiply subjects within subjects or to count an arbitrary "hive" of systems as one mind.

Andrés Gómez-Emilsson and Christopher Percy, who call the boundary problem "the overlooked cousin to the
binding problem," divide it into several sub-questions [gomez-emilsson-percy-2023](../sources/gomez-emilsson-percy-2023.md). Why are the
edges of a subject hard rather than graded? Why do subjects not form at lower levels (individual
neurons) or at higher ones (groups of people)? Why is each subject's experience private to it? And what
keeps a subject's boundary stable over time?

### Proposed solutions

*Causal individuation.* Rosenberg's own approach belongs to his "carrier" theory of causation, on which
causal relations require intrinsic carriers that are experiential in nature [rosenberg-2004](../sources/rosenberg-2004.md).
On this view, a natural individual is a unit bound together by a special, unified kind of causal
interaction, and a subject's boundary lies where that interaction stops. The difficulty is to specify the
unifying relation precisely enough that it does not, like ordinary causal connection, link everything to
everything.

*Maximal integration.* [integrated-information-theory](../positions/integrated-information-theory.md) offers the clearest attempt to settle
boundaries by a principle. Its *exclusion postulate* says that, of all the overlapping systems that
integrate information, only the one whose integrated information (Φ) is maximal is conscious, and its
edges are where Φ peaks [tononi-2016](../sources/tononi-2016.md). This rules out subjects within subjects by fiat. Critics
reply that the postulate makes consciousness depend on facts about neighbouring systems in a way that
seems epiphenomenal: a subsystem that loses out keeps all its causal powers but ceases to be conscious.
They also ask why, after brain damage lowers the main system's Φ, consciousness disappears rather than
shifting to the next-highest system [gomez-emilsson-percy-2023](../sources/gomez-emilsson-percy-2023.md).

*Physical fields.* Several recent proposals locate the boundaries in physics rather than in
computation. Gómez-Emilsson and Percy suggest that subjects correspond to topologically closed pockets
in the brain's electromagnetic field, regions sealed off by closed loops of field lines, which would
give boundaries that are sharp and independent of any observer's description
[gomez-emilsson-percy-2023](../sources/gomez-emilsson-percy-2023.md). Other proposals appeal to coherent phase states or to quantum
entanglement. All of them remain speculative and largely untested.

*Graded boundaries.* A deflationary response denies that the boundaries are as sharp as the problem
assumes. Luke Roelofs argues that how many subjects there are depends on how tightly their parts are
integrated, so that boundaries can shift and blur [roelofs-2019](../sources/roelofs-2019.md). Split-brain patients, in whom
cutting the corpus callosum seems to produce something between one subject and two, are the leading case
(see [subjects-actually-merge-and-divide](../claims/subjects-actually-merge-and-divide.md) and [subject-unity-graded-by-integration](../claims/subject-unity-graded-by-integration.md)).
On this view the boundary problem asks for the *conditions* under which subjects are more or less
distinct, not for a single sharp line. Opponents reply that whether there is something it is like to be
a given system seems to admit of no halfway cases (see [phenomenal-determinacy](../claims/phenomenal-determinacy.md)).

### The outer edge

The proposals above mostly concern where boundaries fall within and among brains. A related debate
concerns a subject's outer edge, between it and the world it is coupled to. The [extended-mind](extended-mind.md)
thesis holds that cognition can include external resources, such as a notebook consulted the way other
people consult memory ([clark-chalmers-1998](../sources/clark-chalmers-1998.md)). Most defenders of that thesis deny that
consciousness extends along with it, on the ground that loops through the environment are too slow and
indirect to take part in experience ([extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness](../claims/extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness.md)). Defenders
of [extended-consciousness](extended-consciousness.md) reply that no principled line separates the two cases
([parity-argument-extended-consciousness](../arguments/parity-argument-extended-consciousness.md)). If they are right, a subject's boundary need not
coincide with the skull, and the boundary problem has to be posed for systems made of a brain together
with parts of its world. If the non-entailment view is right instead, the edge of the cognitive system
and the edge of the experiencing subject can come apart, and a theory of boundaries must say which one
it is drawing. Artificial systems give the question a new form. What a language model can draw on as
working context is decided by the software that runs it, so the boundary of its serial reasoning
workspace is set from outside the model ([cot-serial-verbal-workspace](../claims/cot-serial-verbal-workspace.md)).

### Relation to neighbouring problems

The boundary problem is distinct from three problems it is often grouped with. The
[binding-problem](binding-problem.md) asks how separately processed features (the red, the round and the
rolling of a ball) are unified within one subject's experience; the boundary problem asks why that
unified experience has an edge at all. The combination problem asks how many small subjects could compose
one large one; the boundary problem asks which of the many candidate composites actually is a subject.
The [decombination-problem](decombination-problem.md), which faces cosmopsychism, asks how one cosmic subject could give
rise to many bounded ones; the boundary problem asks where the bounds fall. All three belong to the wider
topic of the [unity-of-consciousness](unity-of-consciousness.md).

### In plain terms

Your experience has edges. You feel your own body and see your own room, but you do not feel what the
person next to you feels, and you and your chair do not share one big experience. Yet physically,
everything is connected to everything else: your brain is wired to your body, your body touches the
floor, and so on outward. So why does experience come in separate, sealed-off packages, roughly one per
brain? Why isn't each brain cell a tiny separate mind, or a whole crowd one big mind? That is the
boundary problem. Proposed answers say the edge falls where the brain's activity is most tightly
integrated, or where its electrical field forms a closed pocket. Some philosophers suspect the edges are
blurrier than they seem, pointing to patients whose brain halves have been surgically separated. A
related question runs the other way: whether a mind, or even an experience, can reach outward beyond the
brain into the notebooks and tools a person relies on.
