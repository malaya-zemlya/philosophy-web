The **neural correlates of consciousness** (NCCs) are the brain processes that go with conscious
experience: the neural activity present when a person is conscious, or conscious of something in
particular, and absent when they are not. Searching for them is the main program of the modern
science of consciousness. It was launched by Francis Crick and Christof Koch
([1990](../sources/crick-koch-1990.md)), who argued that consciousness, long thought too elusive for
science, could be studied by asking which neurons change their activity when experience changes.

The program is deliberately neutral about the [mind-body-problem](mind-body-problem.md). A dualist, an identity
theorist and a functionalist can all agree that experience is correlated with certain brain activity,
while disagreeing about whether that activity *is* the experience, *causes* it, or merely accompanies
it. That neutrality is the program's strength, since it lets the science proceed without settling
metaphysics, and also its limit, since finding the correlates does not by itself explain why they are
accompanied by experience.

### Defining an NCC

Almost any brain activity correlates with consciousness in some way: blood oxygen, the brainstem
arousal systems, the motor commands that produce a report. To pick out something more interesting,
David Chalmers ([2000](../sources/chalmers-2000.md)) defined an NCC as a *minimal* neural system whose states
map onto states of consciousness, such that each state of the system is *sufficient*, under normal
conditions, for the corresponding conscious state. Koch's formulation is "the minimal set of neuronal
events and mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept". Minimality excludes the
trivial answer that the whole brain is sufficient. Sufficiency excludes mere prerequisites, such as an
oxygen supply, and mere consequences, such as the act of reporting.

Two kinds of NCC are usually distinguished ([Koch et al., 2016](../sources/koch-massimini-boly-tononi-2016.md)).
- The **full NCC**, or NCC of *state*, is what distinguishes being conscious at all from dreamless
  sleep, anaesthesia or coma.
- **Content-specific NCCs** are what distinguish one experience from another: seeing a face rather
  than a house, hearing a tone rather than silence.

### The contrastive method

The basic method holds the stimulus fixed and lets the experience vary, so that any neural difference
can be attributed to the experience rather than to the input. In *binocular rivalry*, each eye is
shown a different image, and the subject's experience alternates between the two although nothing on
the retina changes. Leopold and Logothetis ([1996](../sources/leopold-logothetis-1996.md)) recorded neurons in
monkeys whose firing tracked which image the animal reported seeing, and found that the proportion of
such neurons rises in higher visual areas. Masking, in which a briefly flashed image is sometimes seen
and sometimes not, and comparisons of sleep, anaesthesia and waking, apply the same logic.

A striking clinical use is the detection of awareness in patients who cannot move. Owen and colleagues
([2006](../sources/owen-2006.md)) asked a patient diagnosed as vegetative to imagine playing tennis or walking
through her house, and found the same distinct patterns of brain activity that healthy volunteers
produce. This was taken as evidence of awareness detected without any behaviour at all.

### Methodological problems

**Report.** In the contrastive method, the experience is identified by the subject's report, so the
neural difference found may belong to reporting, attending or remembering rather than to experiencing.
Aru and colleagues ([2012](../sources/aru-2012.md)) argue that the method confounds the NCC with its
prerequisites and its consequences. Tsuchiya and colleagues ([2015](../sources/tsuchiya-2015.md)) propose
*no-report paradigms*, which infer what the subject experiences from involuntary signs such as eye
movements or pupil size. Studies using such paradigms suggest that some activity in prefrontal cortex once counted as
an NCC is connected with report rather than with experience.

**Access and phenomenal consciousness.** Underneath the report problem lies the distinction between
[access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness](access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness.md). If every experience is available to report and
reasoning, then the correlates of access are the correlates of experience. If, as Block
([2007](../sources/block-2007.md)) argues, experience can *overflow* access, there could be experience
whose correlates no report-based experiment can find. The leading theories divide on this point.
[global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md) identifies consciousness with the broadcast of information to many
brain systems and so with access, while recurrent processing and [integrated-information-theory](../positions/integrated-information-theory.md)
locate it in processing that can occur without access. The dispute is connected to a further
empirical one, whether the NCC lies mainly in the front of the cortex or in a posterior "hot zone"
([Koch et al., 2016](../sources/koch-massimini-boly-tononi-2016.md)).

**Necessity.** Even a well-established correlate need not be necessary. Brains are plastic and
redundant, and creatures with very different nervous systems, such as birds and octopuses, may be
conscious. An NCC found in human cortex is therefore at best sufficient for consciousness in creatures
like us, and need not be the only way experience can be realized ([Wu & Morales, SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness-neuroscience.md)).

### Correlation and explanation

Suppose the content-specific NCCs were all found, so that for every experience we could name the
neural state that goes with it. Three questions would remain.

1. **Constitution or cause?** Is the NCC the experience itself, as the [type-identity](../positions/type-identity.md)
   holds, or something that produces it? The data are the same either way.
2. **Why this experience?** A correlation says *that* a pattern of activity in visual cortex goes with
   seeing red, not *why* it should feel like that, or like anything. This is the
   [explanatory-gap](explanatory-gap.md) in empirical form, and it is why Chalmers treats the NCC program as a
   solution to the "easy problems" that leaves the [hard-problem](hard-problem.md) standing.
3. **Beyond the calibration set?** Correlations are established in subjects who can report. Whether
   they hold in infants, animals, patients who cannot respond, or machines built differently from
   brains is a further question that the correlations cannot answer by themselves
   ([substrate-independence](../questions/substrate-independence.md)).

The idea of reading a person's experience directly from their NCCs, and the problems of telling
whether such a reading succeeds, are treated in [consciousness-reading-machine](../questions/consciousness-reading-machine.md).

### In plain terms

Scientists cannot see experiences, but they can watch brains. The search for the neural correlates of
consciousness asks which brain activity goes along with experience. A typical experiment shows each
eye a different picture; the person sees first one and then the other, though nothing on the screen
changes, and the scientists look for the brain cells whose activity flips when the experience flips.
The hard part is making sure that what they find goes with the *experience*, not with the person
*saying* what they saw. And even a complete map of which brain activity goes with which experience
would tell us *what* goes with feeling, not *why* brain activity should feel like anything at all.
