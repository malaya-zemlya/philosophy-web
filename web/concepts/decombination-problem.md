The *decombination problem* is the question of how a single conscious universe could give rise to the
many separate conscious beings it contains. It is the central difficulty for [cosmopsychism](../positions/cosmopsychism.md),
the view that the cosmos as a whole is the one fundamental conscious subject and that individual minds
like yours and mine are derived from it. Cosmopsychism was proposed partly to escape the
[combination-problem-panpsychism](combination-problem-panpsychism.md), which asks how countless tiny experiences could add up to
one mind. Cosmopsychism starts from the whole instead of the parts, so it has nothing to add up. What it
must explain instead is the reverse: how one all-encompassing experience divides into many bounded,
private ones, each of which seems sealed off from the rest. You do not experience my headache, and
neither of us experiences what is going on in the Andromeda galaxy, even though on this view all of
these experiences are in some sense aspects of the one cosmic consciousness.

David Chalmers gave an early statement of the difficulty, under the name *decomposition problem*: "how does a single
subject give rise to multiple dependent subjects? How do macroqualities yield microqualities, and how
does macroexperiential structure yield microexperiential structure?" He judged these problems "just as
hard as the original combination problem" [chalmers-2016](../sources/chalmers-2016.md). The name *decombination problem*,
now standard, was given currency by Miri Albahari [albahari-2020](../sources/albahari-2020.md) and Gregory Miller
[miller-2018](../sources/miller-2018.md).

### The shape of the problem

The decombination problem has the same three faces as the combination problem, run in reverse. The
*subject* face asks how one subject could ground many subjects, each with its own point of view. The
*quality* face asks how the cosmic experience, whatever its qualities, could yield the specific
qualities each of us experiences. The *structure* face asks how the structure of the whole could yield
the very local and idiosyncratic structure of an individual experience, such as the layout of your
visual field at this moment.

The subject face is again the hardest, and the arguments that sharpen the combination problem transfer
almost unchanged. Sam Coleman's point that a subject's perspective is defined by what it excludes
[coleman-2014](../sources/coleman-2014.md) applies in reverse: if the cosmic subject has *all* experiences, it seems unable
to contain a subject whose essence is to have *only these* experiences and no others. Goff's
conceivability argument also transfers [goff-2009](../sources/goff-2009.md). It seems conceivable that the universe is
conscious while none of its parts is, a kind of *cosmic zombie* in reverse, and if that is possible then
the cosmic experience does not by itself necessitate yours [sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md).

Miller argues that the two problems are at bottom the same question, namely whether one subject can be
a proper part of another [miller-2018](../sources/miller-2018.md). The combination problem asks whether a subject can be
built *from* subjects that are its parts; the decombination problem asks whether a subject can *have*
subjects as its parts. If subjects cannot be proper parts of subjects, both views fail; if they can, both
problems are solved at once. On this reading, cosmopsychism gains no advantage from reversing the
direction of explanation, although it may still be preferable on other grounds.

### Responses

*Grounding by subsumption.* Philip Goff argues that the cosmopsychist does not need to *analyse* a
human subject as a piece cut out of the cosmic subject [goff-2017](../sources/goff-2017.md), [goff-2020](../sources/goff-2020.md). A
fact can instead be grounded *by subsumption*, as an aspect of a wider unity: the way one note in a chord
exists as an aspect of the whole chord without the chord being assembled from separate notes. Goff
concedes that pure consciousness may not suffice for this, and supplements the cosmic grounding base with
a further, partly unknown nature ("consciousness+").

*Dissociation.* Bernardo Kastrup's analytic idealism models individual minds on *dissociative identity
disorder*, in which one mind hosts several alters with mutually inaccessible experiences
[kastrup-2019](../sources/kastrup-2019.md). If such dissociation actually occurs within one human mind, there is a natural
model for how one cosmic mind might give rise to many walled-off subjects. Critics reply that it is
disputed whether alters are genuinely distinct subjects, and that the model names the phenomenon rather
than explaining it.

*Individuation without dissection.* Itay Shani and Joachim Keppler deny that ordinary subjects must be
carved out of cosmic consciousness by a process that simply reverses combination
[shani-keppler-2018](../sources/shani-keppler-2018.md). They identify cosmic consciousness with the vacuum state of quantum field
theory and treat individual subjects as relatively self-contained patterns arising within it. Their
claim is that this offers a model of individuation rather than of dissection, though it depends on
contested physical speculation.

*A subjectless ground.* Albahari argues that the problem is fatal to any view on which the cosmic
ground is itself a *subject*, a "great I am" with its own point of view [albahari-2020](../sources/albahari-2020.md). Her
alternative, inspired by Advaita Vedānta, grounds the world in a universal consciousness that is not a
perspective at all. Individual perspectives then arise as limitations within it rather than as subjects
carved out of a larger subject. The question this raises is how perspectives arise from something that
has none, which some critics regard as a new form of the original problem.

*Borrowing from priority monism.* Another line holds that the problem is no worse than a familiar feature
of priority monism, the metaphysics on which the cosmos is the one fundamental object. The priority
monist already explains how a diverse world of parts depends on a single whole, and the cosmopsychist
can borrow that explanation. Miller has replied that the decombination problem is not just this
"heterogeneity problem" for priority monism, because subjects raise difficulties that ordinary parts
do not [miller-2021](../sources/miller-2021.md).

### Relation to neighbouring problems

The decombination problem is related to, but distinct from, the *boundary problem* for panpsychism, which
asks what fixes the edges of a subject [rosenberg-2004](../sources/rosenberg-2004.md). The boundary problem asks *where*
subjects begin and end; the decombination problem asks *how* a whole can ground any bounded subject at
all. It also connects to the [unity-of-consciousness](unity-of-consciousness.md), since the puzzle is precisely that each
derived subject's experience is unified within itself and walled off from every other.

The entry is distinct from [cosmopsychism](../positions/cosmopsychism.md) and [cosmos-is-fundamental-subject](../claims/cosmos-is-fundamental-subject.md),
which state the view itself and mention the problem only in passing; this entry sets out the problem,
its forms and the responses to it.

### In plain terms

Some philosophers think consciousness is built into the universe. One version says the tiniest particles
have tiny experiences, which then have to be stitched together into a whole mind like yours; that
stitching is the "combination problem." Another version, cosmopsychism, starts at the top: the whole
universe is one big conscious being, and your mind is somehow part of it. That avoids the stitching, but
raises the opposite question. If there is just one universal consciousness, why are you shut inside your
own experience, unable to feel what anyone else feels? How does one mind become many separate,
walled-off minds? That is the decombination problem. Proposed answers compare it to a single person with
several dissociated personalities, or to one note within a chord, but critics argue that splitting one
mind into many is just as puzzling as merging many into one.
