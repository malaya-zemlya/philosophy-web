The *extended mind* thesis holds that the processes and states that make up a mind are not always
confined to the brain, or even to the body, but can include parts of the world outside it, such as a
notebook, a calculator or a phone, when these are coupled to the agent in the right way. On this view
the boundary of a mind is set by what does the cognitive work, not by the skull.

### The parity principle and Otto

The thesis was introduced by Andy Clark and David Chalmers ([clark-chalmers-1998](../sources/clark-chalmers-1998.md)). Its engine
is a *parity principle*: if a part of the world functions as a process that we would unhesitatingly
call cognitive were it going on in the head, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive
process. Their central example compares two people who want to visit a museum. Inga recalls that it is
on 53rd Street and goes there. Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease, carries a notebook in which he writes
down what he needs to know; he looks up the address and goes there. Clark and Chalmers argue that the
notebook entry plays, for Otto, the role that a stored memory plays for Inga, so Otto believed the
museum was on 53rd Street even before he opened the notebook, and the belief is partly located in the
notebook.

Not every external resource qualifies. Clark and Chalmers suggest that it must be reliably available
and typically consulted, that its contents must be more or less automatically endorsed, and that they
must have been consciously endorsed at some point in the past. A library book or the internet as a
whole usually fails these conditions. The working notebook of a scientist plausibly meets them. Carl
Friedrich Gauss's mathematical diary, a sequence of 146 terse entries recording his discoveries from
1796 to 1814, is a natural example ([gray-1984](../sources/gray-1984.md)): an entry that Gauss could consult and act on
was, by the parity principle, part of his mathematical memory.

### Varieties of the thesis

It is common to distinguish a *state-oriented* version, on which particular mental states such as
Otto's belief are partly external, from a *process-oriented* version, on which cognitive processes such
as remembering or calculating include the manipulation of external structures, as when long
multiplication runs through the pen and paper as much as through the head
([sep-content-externalism](../sources/sep-content-externalism.md)). The thesis also differs from the older doctrine of *content externalism*, which
says that what a thought is about depends on the environment. Extended mind is
a claim about the *vehicles* of thought, the physical bearers of content, not about content itself.

### Objections

The best-known objection, due to Fred Adams and Kenneth Aizawa, is that extended-mind arguments commit a
*coupling-constitution fallacy*: from the fact that a notebook is causally coupled to Otto's cognition
it does not follow that it is part of that cognition, since a process can depend causally on many
things that are not among its parts ([adams-aizawa-2001](../sources/adams-aizawa-2001.md)). They also argue that cognition
requires content that is not derived from interpretation, and the marks in a notebook have content
only because someone reads them ([intrinsic-derived-intentionality](intrinsic-derived-intentionality.md)). Gauss's diary gives
the point an edge: some of its entries are so cryptic that their meaning has had to be reconstructed,
so what the marks contain depends on who is reading them. A further question is whether the thesis
stops at belief and memory or carries over to experience itself, which is the issue of
[extended-consciousness](extended-consciousness.md). Clark and Chalmers themselves drew the line at consciousness
([extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness](../claims/extended-cognition-not-extended-consciousness.md)).

### In plain terms

The extended mind idea says that your mind isn't necessarily all inside your head. If you rely on a
notebook the way other people rely on their memory, writing things down, trusting what you wrote and
looking it up whenever you need it, then the notebook is doing the job memory does, and it counts as
part of your mind. Critics reply that being *connected* to your thinking isn't the same as being *part*
of it, and that words on a page mean nothing until someone reads them.
