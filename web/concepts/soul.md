The *soul* is, in its broadest sense, whatever it is in a living being that makes it alive, aware and
the self that it is. Nearly every philosophical and religious tradition has a word for it: Greek *psychē*,
Latin *anima*, Hebrew *nefesh*, Arabic *nafs* and *rūḥ*, Sanskrit *ātman*, *puruṣa* and *jīva*, Chinese
*hun* and *po*. The words do not mean the same thing. For some traditions the soul is a separate,
immortal thing temporarily housed in a body; for others it is the body's life and organization; for
Buddhism, the belief in such a thing is the root error to be overcome. What the traditions share is a
question, recorded in this web as [soul](../questions/soul.md): is there a self that is something more than the
living body and its activity, and if so, what is it?

This entry sorts the main answers by their metaphysical type rather than by tradition. The same type of
answer often appears independently in different cultures, Avicenna's floating man anticipating
Descartes's argument from doubt, for example, and grouping by type brings out these parallels as well as
the deep disagreements within each tradition. The surveys in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy are
the main references [sep-ancient-soul](../sources/sep-ancient-soul.md), [sep-mind-indian-buddhism](../sources/sep-mind-indian-buddhism.md),
[sep-dualism](../sources/sep-dualism.md), [sep-afterlife](../sources/sep-afterlife.md); Jonardon Ganeri's *The Self* reconstructs the classical
Indian debates among Buddhists, Naiyāyikas, Vedāntins and materialists [ganeri-2012](../sources/ganeri-2012.md).

### 1. The soul as an immaterial substance

The most familiar view in the West is that the soul is an immaterial substance, a thing in its own right
that is not made of matter, that thinks and feels, and that can exist without the body
([soul-is-immaterial-substance](../claims/soul-is-immaterial-substance.md)). The person is the soul, and the body is its instrument or its
temporary home. Death is the separation of the two, and the soul's survival is the basis of immortality.

The classic Greek statement is Plato's *Phaedo*, which argues that the soul is simple, akin to the
unchanging Forms, and therefore indestructible [plato-phaedo](../sources/plato-phaedo.md). Augustine brought a Platonic
soul into Christian theology, and Descartes gave it its modern form, identifying the soul with the mind,
a thinking thing whose existence he could not doubt even while doubting that he had a body
[descartes-meditations](../sources/descartes-meditations.md). Six centuries earlier, Avicenna had argued in much the same way with
his [floating-man](../arguments/floating-man.md): a man created suspended in a void, without any sensation, would still
know that he exists while knowing nothing of his body. Among contemporary philosophers the view is
defended by Richard Swinburne [swinburne-1986](../sources/swinburne-1986.md); William Hasker's *emergent dualism* is a
variant on which the soul is generated by the brain but is a new substance once it exists
[hasker-1999](../sources/hasker-1999.md). These views are treated generally under [dualism](../positions/dualism.md).

Several Indian schools also hold that the self is a substance distinct from the body. The Nyāya and
Vaiśeṣika schools treat the self (*ātman*) as an eternal, all-pervading substance, but hold that
consciousness is not its essence, only a quality it acquires when joined to a mind and body; a liberated
self, on their view, is not conscious at all. The Dvaita school of Vedānta holds that individual souls are
eternally distinct from God and from each other. Jainism holds that there are infinitely many souls
(*jīva*), each essentially conscious, which expand and contract to fill the bodies they occupy, and that
karma is a fine kind of matter that clings to the soul and weighs it down [sep-jaina-philosophy](../sources/sep-jaina-philosophy.md).

### 2. Pure consciousness set against a material mind

The Indian school of Sāṃkhya, with the Yoga school that adopted its metaphysics, holds a distinctive
dualism ([samkhya](../positions/samkhya.md)). On one side is *puruṣa*, pure, inactive, contentless consciousness; on
the other is *prakṛti*, matter, which includes not only the physical world but the intellect, the
ego-sense and the mind. Thinking and deciding are, on this view, material processes
([cognition-belongs-to-matter](../claims/cognition-belongs-to-matter.md)), and the self proper is only the witnessing awareness in whose
light they occur. Sāṃkhya is a soul-dualism like those of the first family, and holds that there are many
such selves, but its line between soul and matter falls in a quite different place, leaving the whole of
cognition on the material side.

### 3. The soul as the form of the body

A third family holds that the soul is neither a separate thing nor mere matter but the *form* of a
living body: its organization, the principle by which it is alive and has the capacities it has
([soul-is-form-of-body](../claims/soul-is-form-of-body.md)). This is [hylomorphism](../positions/hylomorphism.md), founded by Aristotle, for whom the
soul of an axe would be its capacity to cut, and of an eye its capacity to see [aristotle-de-anima](../sources/aristotle-de-anima.md). A soul in this sense does
not ordinarily survive the body, any more than the shape of a statue survives its melting.

Thomas Aquinas adapted the view to Christian doctrine [aquinas-summa](../sources/aquinas-summa.md). The human soul, he held,
is the substantial form of the body, but because its highest activity, understanding, uses no bodily
organ, it can subsist on its own after death. Between death and the resurrection it exists as a
"separated soul," incomplete until it is reunited with a body. Thomists disagree about whether the person
survives in this interval. *Survivalists* such as Eleonore Stump hold that the person continues to exist,
constituted for a time by the soul alone [stump-2006](../sources/stump-2006.md); *corruptionists* such as Patrick Toner
hold that the person ceases to exist at death and only the soul, a part of the person, remains until the
resurrection [toner-2009](../sources/toner-2009.md). In the Islamic world, Avicenna's Aristotelian successors and the
later philosopher Mullā Ṣadrā developed related views; Ṣadrā held that the soul is "bodily in its
origination and spiritual in its survival," arising as the form of a body and becoming independent of it
through a process of inner transformation [sep-mulla-sadra](../sources/sep-mulla-sadra.md).

### 4. No enduring self

Buddhism denies that there is any enduring self at all ([no-self](../positions/no-self.md)). A person is a stream of
five kinds of momentary events (physical form, feeling, perception, volitional formations and
consciousness), and the word "I" names the stream, not a further owner of it ([no-enduring-self](../claims/no-enduring-self.md)).
The view is illustrated by the *chariot* of the *Questions of King Milinda*, which is nothing over and
above its parts suitably assembled [milindapanha](../sources/milindapanha.md). Buddhism nonetheless teaches rebirth, which
it explains as the continuity of a causal series, like a flame passed from one lamp to another, rather
than the passage of a soul. Within Buddhism, the *Pudgalavāda* schools held that the person is real
though neither identical with nor distinct from the aggregates, and were opposed by Vasubandhu
[vasubandhu-abhidharmakosa](../sources/vasubandhu-abhidharmakosa.md); the Madhyamaka school of Nāgārjuna held that the self and the
aggregates alike are "empty" of intrinsic nature. The Western parallels are Hume's bundle theory
[hume-treatise](../sources/hume-treatise.md) and Parfit's reductionism about personal identity [parfit-1984](../sources/parfit-1984.md).

### 5. One universal consciousness

A fifth family holds that the true self is not an individual thing but one consciousness shared by, or
underlying, all apparent selves. The fullest statement is [advaita-vedanta](../positions/advaita-vedanta.md), on which the self
(*ātman*) is identical with *Brahman*, the one changeless consciousness, and the plurality of selves is an
appearance produced by ignorance ([self-is-brahman](../claims/self-is-brahman.md)). The Buddhist school of
[yogacara](../positions/yogacara.md) agrees that the world of objects is nothing but appearances in consciousness, but,
true to its Buddhism, denies that there is any one self behind them, only many impersonal streams. In
contemporary philosophy, [cosmopsychism](../positions/cosmopsychism.md) and analytic idealism ([idealism](../positions/idealism.md)) defend
related views, and face the same difficulty these traditions faced: how one consciousness could appear as
many separate subjects (see [decombination-problem](decombination-problem.md)).

### 6. No soul

The last family denies that there is a soul in any of the above senses: a person is a wholly physical
being, and mental life is something the body, and in particular the brain, does
([mental-is-physical](../claims/mental-is-physical.md)). In ancient India the Cārvāka school held that consciousness arises from
the combination of material elements, as the power to intoxicate arises when certain ingredients ferment,
and that it ends with the body. In early Islamic theology, many Ashʿarite theologians treated the soul as a
subtle body or as a feature of the living body rather than an immaterial substance
[sep-arabic-islamic-mind](../sources/sep-arabic-islamic-mind.md). Among Christian
philosophers, [christian-physicalism](../positions/christian-physicalism.md) combines a physicalist view of the person with belief in
life after death, which it locates entirely in the resurrection of the body
([survival-requires-resurrection](../claims/survival-requires-resurrection.md)). Secular [physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md) belongs to this family but
draws the further conclusion that death is the end of the person.

### Other traditions

Some traditions resist this classification. Biblical Hebrew thought, as many scholars read it, treats a
human being as a single animated body (*nefesh*) rather than a soul in a body, which Christian physicalists
claim as precedent; medieval Jewish philosophers such as Maimonides, following Aristotle, held that only
the perfected intellect survives. Classical Chinese thought spoke of two souls, the *hun* and the *po*,
which separate at death, and of a vital energy (*qi*) that constitutes both body and mind, a picture that
fits neither dualism nor materialism neatly. These traditions are not yet represented by their own entries
in this web.

### In plain terms

Is there a "you" that is more than your body, and could it survive death? Traditions have answered in
roughly six ways. The soul might be a separate, non-physical thing living in the body (Plato, Descartes,
popular Christianity, Islam's Avicenna, Jainism). It might be pure awareness, separate from a mind that is
itself physical (India's Sāṃkhya). It might be the body's own life and organization, as a statue's shape
belongs to the statue (Aristotle, Aquinas). There might be no lasting self at all, just a flow of
experiences (Buddhism). Everyone's true self might be one and the same universal consciousness (Advaita
Vedānta). Or there might be no soul, the person being entirely physical, with life after death, if any,
coming only by resurrection (some modern Christians) or not at all (secular materialists).
