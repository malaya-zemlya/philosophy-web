*Multiple realizability* is the thesis that one and the same mental state, or more generally one
higher-level kind, can be *realized* (physically implemented) by very different physical
structures. A clock is a familiar non-mental case: being a clock can be realized by gears and
springs, by a vibrating quartz crystal, by sand running through a narrow neck or by a program on a
phone. What makes something a clock is what it does, not what it is made of. Applied to the mind, the
thesis says that pain, belief or the sight of red could likewise be realized in a human brain, in the
very differently organised nervous system of an octopus, and perhaps in silicon.

### The argument against identity theory

Hilary Putnam turned the idea into the leading objection to the [type-identity](../positions/type-identity.md) theory,
which holds that each type of mental state is identical to a type of brain state
([mental-states-are-brain-state-types](../claims/mental-states-are-brain-state-types.md)). If pain *is* a particular neural state, a creature
without that state cannot be in pain. Yet mammals, birds, reptiles and molluscs all seem able to feel
pain while differing widely in their neural make-up, and nothing seems to rule out pain in a Martian
or a machine. Putnam concluded that it is very unlikely that any single physical type is shared by
every possible creature in pain, and proposed instead that mental states be identified by their
functional role, by what they do in the system that has them ([putnam-1967](../sources/putnam-1967.md)). The argument
is the main historical source of [functionalism](functionalism.md).

### Multiple realizability and the special sciences

Jerry Fodor generalised the point beyond psychology ([fodor-1974](../sources/fodor-1974.md)). The kinds of economics,
such as *monetary exchange*, can be realized in coins, shells, cheques or electronic transfers, which
have nothing physically in common. A law stated in terms of monetary exchange therefore corresponds
to no law of physics, since any physical counterpart would be an open-ended disjunction of unrelated
mechanisms. Fodor took this to show that the special sciences are autonomous: their laws cannot be
replaced by physical laws without losing the generalisations they capture. This is the main argument
against classical [reduction](reduction.md), and it moved many philosophers from identity theory to
non-reductive physicalism, on which mental properties are not identical to physical ones but
supervene on them ([supervenience](supervenience.md)).

### Objections

Critics have questioned both the evidence and the inference. William Bechtel and Jennifer Mundale
argued that the case for multiple realization trades on a mismatch of grain: psychological states
are described coarsely, as "pain" across all species, while brain states are described finely, down
to particular neurons ([bechtel-mundale-1999](../sources/bechtel-mundale-1999.md)). Compared at the same grain, as neuroscience
does when it maps homologous brain areas across species, the realizers look much more alike. Others
reply that identity theory can be relativised to species, so that human pain is one brain state and
octopus pain another. A further line asks whether a kind with
physically heterogeneous realizers is a single causal kind at all, or only a label for a
disjunction of unrelated physical kinds. How fine the relevant grain of description should be is also central to
debates about whether artificial systems could share our mental states ([grain](grain.md)).

### Fibres, equivalence and quotients

Since a supervenient state is a function of its base, $y = f(x)$ ([supervenience](supervenience.md)), multiple
realizability says that $f$ is not one-to-one, and it is often more useful to ask what $f$ glues
together than whether it does. Two base states are *equivalent* when $f$ sends them to the same
value. The equivalence class of a value $y$, its *fibre* $f^{-1}(y)$, is the set of all the ways of
realizing $y$, which philosophers call its *realizers*. The higher level is then the *quotient* of
the base: what remains when equivalent base states are treated as one. Many disputes about mind can
be restated as disputes about this equivalence relation.

- **How large and how unified the fibres are.** Fodor's argument is that the fibre of an economic or
  psychological kind is physically heterogeneous, with nothing physical in common among its members.
  Bechtel and Mundale's reply is that the fibres look large only when the two levels are described at
  different grains. The worry about disjunctive kinds is that a fibre without internal unity may not
  amount to a single causal kind.
- **Where the relation lies.** [type-identity](../positions/type-identity.md) says the fibres of the brain-to-mind map
  are single brain-state types. [functionalism](functionalism.md) says two states are equivalent exactly when
  they play the same causal role, and the [grain](grain.md) of role individuation fixes how coarse
  the equivalence is. The principle of [organizational-invariance](../claims/organizational-invariance.md) says experience is
  constant on classes of fine-grained functional isomorphs, and the absent-qualia and inverted-qualia
  cases deny it.
- **What the input of the function is.** Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth shows that what a person means,
  and so what they believe, is not a function of their internal state alone: Oscar and his molecule-for-molecule
  twin are in one state, yet one thinks about H₂O and the other about XYZ ([putnam-1975b](../sources/putnam-1975b.md)).
  So-called *narrow content* is defined by the quotient construction. It is whatever all internal
  duplicates share, the most detailed content that is constant on the fibres of internal state.
- **Coarse-graining in physics.** A thermodynamic macrostate is a fibre of the map from microstates
  to macrostates, and Boltzmann's entropy $S = k \log W$, where $W$ counts the microstates in the
  fibre, measures how much the map glues together ([boltzmann-1877](../sources/boltzmann-1877.md)). Here the question of
  how multiply realized a state is has an exact numerical answer.

The equivalence also bears on mental causation. A quotient of an algebraic structure inherits the
structure only when the equivalence respects it. In the same way, a higher level has causal laws of
its own only if equivalent base states have equivalent effects, so that the dynamics passes down to
the quotient. If it does, the higher-level state is a genuine difference-maker
([mental-causes-are-difference-makers](../claims/mental-causes-are-difference-makers.md)). If not, the higher level is a mere label, which is
the conclusion Kim's [causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md) draws.

### In plain terms

Many different things can do the same job. A clock can be made of gears, a crystal or a computer
chip, and it is still a clock. Multiple realizability is the idea that minds might be like that too:
a human, an octopus and perhaps a robot could all feel pain, even though their insides are built very
differently. If that is right, pain cannot simply *be* one particular kind of brain activity, since
creatures without that activity could still have it. This is a main reason many philosophers define
mental states by what they do rather than by what they are made of.
