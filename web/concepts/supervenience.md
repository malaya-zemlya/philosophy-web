*Supervenience* is a relation of dependence between two families of properties or facts. One family
(the *supervenient* or higher-level facts) supervenes on another (the *base* or *subvenient* facts)
when there can be no difference in the first without some difference in the second. The standard
example is a picture on a screen: two screens alike in the colour of every pixel cannot differ in
what picture they show. The picture supervenes on the pixels, though talk of the picture is not talk
of pixels.

### Why the idea is useful

Supervenience lets a philosopher say that one domain is fixed by another without saying that it is
*identical* to it or *definable* in its terms. That made it the natural way to state a modest
physicalism. Donald Davidson used it to describe how the mental could depend on the physical even
though, on his view, there are no strict laws linking mental and physical kinds
([davidson-1970](../sources/davidson-1970.md), [anomalous-monism](../positions/anomalous-monism.md)). The usual formulation of
[physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md) is a supervenience thesis: any world physically exactly like ours is exactly
like ours in every respect, mental facts included. Once the physical facts are fixed, all the facts
are fixed.

Supervenience also gives the anti-physicalist a precise target. The [philosophical-zombie](philosophical-zombie.md)
is a physical duplicate of a person without any experience. If such a being is possible, the
phenomenal facts do not supervene on the physical facts, and physicalism in its supervenience form is
false ([zombie-conceivability](../arguments/zombie-conceivability.md)). [dualism](../positions/dualism.md) is, in these terms, the view that
consciousness fails to supervene on the physical, or supervenes only by the contingent laws of
nature rather than by necessity.

### Supervenience as a function

Mathematically, supervenience says that the supervenient facts are a *function* of the base facts.
For each complete base state $x$ there is exactly one supervenient state $y$, so that $y = f(x)$. To
say there is no difference in $y$ without a difference in $x$ is to say exactly this: equal inputs
cannot give unequal outputs. The function need not be one-to-one. Many base states can give the same
supervenient state, and this is how [multiple-realizability](multiple-realizability.md) fits with supervenience: pain
can be a function of brain state even though brain state is not a function of pain. What the
function glues together, the set of base states that share a supervenient state, is often more
revealing than the function itself. That set is the state's *fibre*, or set of realizers, and the
analysis is set out under multiple realizability.

### Varieties

Jaegwon Kim's analysis distinguished several strengths of the relation ([kim-1984](../sources/kim-1984.md)). In
the functional picture they differ in what the function is allowed to depend on.

- *Weak* supervenience requires only that, within any one possible world, things alike in their base
  properties are alike in their supervenient ones. The function may differ from world to world, so
  that $y = f(w, x)$: the supervenient state depends on the base state *and* on which world $w$ the
  thing is in, actual or counterfactual. Two things alike in their base properties must match only if
  they are in the same world.
- *Strong* supervenience requires one function for every world, $y = f(x)$, with no dependence on
  $w$. Anything with a given base property, in whatever world, has the corresponding supervenient
  property.
- *Global* supervenience compares whole worlds rather than individual things. The input is the
  complete base facts of a world and the output its complete supervenient facts, so that
  $Y(w) = F(X(w))$. Any two worlds alike in their total base facts are alike in their total
  supervenient facts. This allows a thing's supervenient properties to depend on its surroundings
  as well as on its own base properties. On externalist views of mental content, for instance, what
  a person believes is not a function of their brain alone, though it may still be a function of the
  whole physical world.

The functional picture shows why weak supervenience is too weak to express physicalism. The world
parameter $w$ can carry anything at all that is not part of the base, such as non-physical laws or
non-physical facts. Supervenience in the strong or global form removes that parameter, but only
across some range of worlds, which is the second dimension: the kind of necessity involved. The
mental might supervene on the physical by *nomological* necessity, with a single function holding in
every world with our laws of nature, or by *metaphysical* necessity, with a single function holding
in every possible world whatever. A property dualist can accept the first and deny the second. On
that view the psychophysical laws *are* the function $f$, and in worlds with different laws $f$
would be different. It is metaphysical supervenience that physicalism needs.

### What supervenience does not settle

Kim argued that weak and global supervenience are too weak to guarantee that the higher level
genuinely *depends* on the base ([kim-1984](../sources/kim-1984.md)). Even strong supervenience records only a
pattern of co-variation, a function from base to supervenient facts. Such a function is not
automatically asymmetric: if it happens to be one-to-one, the base facts are equally a function of the
supervenient ones that actually occur, so that the base supervenes on the supervenient facts as well, whereas dependence is meant to run in one direction only. Nor does it say why the
pattern holds, and it is compatible with views on
which the supervenient properties are something extra, attached to the base by a brute necessary
connection. For this reason many philosophers treat supervenience as a necessary condition of
physicalism rather than a full statement of it, and look for a stronger relation such as
[reduction](reduction.md), realization or grounding to explain the dependence. Supervenience without
identity also raises the question whether the supervenient properties can do any causal work of their
own, which is the issue behind Kim's [causal-exclusion](../arguments/causal-exclusion.md) and the claim that such properties
are pre-empted by their physical base ([irreducible-mental-causally-excluded](../claims/irreducible-mental-causally-excluded.md)).

### In plain terms

Saying that A "supervenes" on B means that you can't change A without changing B. The picture on
your screen can't change unless some pixel changes, so the picture supervenes on the pixels. Many
philosophers think the mind supervenes in the same way on the brain: two brains exactly alike in
every physical detail would have exactly the same thoughts and feelings. People who doubt this point
to imagined beings physically just like us but with no inner experience at all. If such beings are
possible, experience does not supervene on the physical.
