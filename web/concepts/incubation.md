*Incubation* is the period during which a problem one has stopped consciously working on seems to go
on being worked on anyway, until a solution arrives unbidden, often while the person is thinking about
something else entirely. The term names both the interval itself and the hypothesis that something
useful happens in it.

### The canonical case

The best-known report comes from the mathematician Henri Poincaré ([poincare-1908](../sources/poincare-1908.md)). After
a long stretch of work on the theory of what he called Fuchsian functions, he set it aside to join a
geological excursion. At Coutances, as he put his foot on the step of an omnibus, the idea came to
him that the transformations he had used to define those functions were identical to the
transformations of non-Euclidean geometry. Nothing in his thoughts at that moment had led up to it,
he went straight back to the conversation he was having, and he felt a perfect certainty, which he
confirmed on returning home. The pattern is familiar from ordinary life: a name that would not come
to mind surfaces an hour later, or the answer to a puzzle one gave up on presents itself in the
shower, sometimes a different answer from any one had been rehearsing.

### Wallas's four stages

The psychologist Graham Wallas built such testimony into a four-stage model of creative thought
([wallas-1926](../sources/wallas-1926.md)). In *preparation* the problem is studied consciously and in earnest; in
*incubation* it is set aside; in *illumination* the idea arrives, typically suddenly and with a sense
of rightness; in *verification* it is consciously checked and worked out. Wallas insisted that the
stages overlap in practice. His model remains the standard framework for discussing creative insight,
and illumination is now usually studied under the name *insight* (the "aha" experience).

### What happens during incubation

That incubation periods help is reasonably well supported. A meta-analysis of the experimental
literature finds a reliable if modest benefit, larger for open-ended creative problems than for
problems with a single right answer ([sio-ormerod-2009](../sources/sio-ormerod-2009.md)). *Why* it helps is disputed. On
the *unconscious work* view, which was Poincaré's own, the mind goes on searching and combining below
awareness and hands the winning result to consciousness. Deflationary rivals hold that nothing is
computed during the break: the person returns rested, or has forgotten a misleading approach that was
blocking progress (so-called *fixation*), or meets a chance cue in the environment that primes the
answer. The famous anecdotes should also be handled with care, since several were embellished in the
retelling ([sep-creativity](../sources/sep-creativity.md)).

### Significance for theories of consciousness

Incubation is a natural fit for global workspace theory ([global-workspace](../positions/global-workspace.md)), on which most
cognition is carried out by unconscious specialist processes working in parallel, and a content
becomes conscious only when it wins the competition for a limited-capacity "stage" from which it is
broadcast to the rest of the mind. The sudden arrival of the solution is, on this picture, the moment
of broadcast, while the search that produced it never entered the workspace at all. The
phenomenon also matters for the reliability of self-report. A person who reports, accurately, that a
solution "just came to them" gives an incomplete account of how they reached it, but not a false one.
That distinguishes incubation from [confabulation](confabulation.md), in which the subject supplies a fluent but
mistaken story of how the result was produced ([omission-is-not-confabulation](../claims/omission-is-not-confabulation.md)).

### In plain terms

Incubation is the "sleep on it" effect. You work hard on a problem, get stuck, give up and do
something else, and then the answer pops into your head out of nowhere, as it did for the
mathematician Poincaré stepping onto a bus. Psychologists agree that taking a break often helps.
They disagree about why: some think your brain keeps searching behind the scenes and only tells you
when it finds something, others think you simply come back fresh or have forgotten a wrong idea that
was getting in the way.
