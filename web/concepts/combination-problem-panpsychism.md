The *combination problem* is the question of how the tiny experiences that [panpsychism](panpsychism.md)
attributes to the basic constituents of matter could add up to the single, rich, unified experience of a
human being or an animal. Panpsychism holds that consciousness is present at the most fundamental level
of nature, so that an electron or a quark has some rudimentary form of experience. If that is so, the
brain does not create experience from scratch; it organizes experience that was already there. But then
something has to explain how billions of separate micro-experiences, each belonging to its own
micro-subject, come together as *your* experience of reading this sentence, which seems to be one
experience had by one subject. Proponents and critics of panpsychism agree that this is the view's
hardest problem, and most of its defenders concede that no fully satisfactory solution yet exists
[sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md).

The problem bites hardest on *constitutive* panpsychism, the version on which macro-experience is
nothing over and above micro-experience suitably related, in the way a house is nothing over and above
bricks suitably arranged. That version is also the one most often defended, because it promises to
explain human consciousness without positing any new, brute emergence. The combination problem asks
whether the promise can be kept.

This entry surveys the problem as a whole: its history, its several forms and the main families of
response. It is distinct from [combination-problem](../arguments/combination-problem.md), which records one particular strong
version of it (the subject-summing objection) as a formal argument with premises and a conclusion.

### Origins

The worry is usually traced to William James, who in *The Principles of Psychology* criticized the
"mind-stuff" theory of his day, on which the mind is compounded out of elementary units of feeling
[james-1890](../sources/james-1890.md). James argued that feelings do not compound:

> Take a hundred of them, shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can (whatever that may
> mean); still each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant
> of what the other feelings are and mean.

A "hundred-and-first feeling" belonging to the group as a whole would, in James's view, be a totally new
fact that could not be deduced from the hundred. James himself was making a broader point, since he
doubted that *anything* genuinely combines, but later writers took the passage as identifying a
difficulty peculiar to minds: bricks unproblematically compose a house, while it is hard even to
understand what it would be for many minds to compose another mind. The name "combination problem" was
introduced by William Seager [seager-1995](../sources/seager-1995.md), and the modern literature dates from the revival of
panpsychism in the early twenty-first century.

### The three faces of the problem

David Chalmers divides the problem along the three features every experience has: it belongs to a
subject, it has qualities, and it has a structure [chalmers-2016](../sources/chalmers-2016.md). Each yields its own
combination problem, and each has a particularly sharp form.

The *subject combination problem* asks how micro-subjects combine into a macro-subject. Its sharpest
form is the *subject-summing problem*. Given any group of subjects, it seems possible for exactly those
subjects to exist, having exactly their experiences, without any further subject existing. If so, no
arrangement of micro-subjects guarantees a macro-subject, and the macro-subject is not constituted by
them after all.

The *quality combination problem* asks how micro-qualities combine into the qualities we experience. Its
sharpest form is the *palette problem*. Human experience contains a vast array of qualities: colours,
tones, smells, pains, tastes, each apparently unlike the others. If micro-qualities correspond to the
handful of basic properties physics recognizes (mass, charge, spin and a few more), it is hard to see how
so small a palette could paint so large a canvas.

The *structure combination problem* asks how the structure of micro-experience yields the structure of
macro-experience. Its sharpest form is the *structural mismatch problem*. The visual field has a
spatial layout, experience is divided into sensory modalities, and so on, whereas the physical structure
of the brain looks entirely different. If micro-experience mirrors micro-physical structure, combining
it ought to yield something that mirrors the structure of the brain, not the structure of experience.
This is closely related to the older [grain-problem](grain-problem.md), which asks why experience seems smooth
and homogeneous when its neural basis is discrete and granular.

Chalmers lists further aspects as well. The *unity problem* asks how micro-experiences yield a single
unified consciousness, linking the combination problem to the wider topic of the
[unity-of-consciousness](unity-of-consciousness.md). The *boundary problem*, developed by Gregg Rosenberg, asks why
experience comes in bounded individuals at all: if experience is everywhere, what fixes where one
subject ends and another begins, so that you are a subject while the room you sit in is not
[rosenberg-2004](../sources/rosenberg-2004.md)? The *awareness problem* asks how micro-experiences give rise to a subject
that is aware of its qualities.

### The problem as an argument

The combination problem can be posed mildly or strongly. In its mild form it is a challenge: the
panpsychist owes, in the long run, an account of how combination works. In its strong form it is an
argument that combination is impossible, so that constitutive panpsychism is false. Two versions of the
strong form are prominent.

Sam Coleman argues that subject-summing is incoherent [coleman-2014](../sources/coleman-2014.md). A subject's point of
view is defined not only by the experiences it has but by its having those experiences *and no others*.
A subject composed of two others would have to enjoy each one's experiences to the exclusion of all
others, which is contradictory if the two have different experiences. That argument is recorded in
this web as [combination-problem](../arguments/combination-problem.md), whose key premise is [micro-subjects-do-not-sum](../claims/micro-subjects-do-not-sum.md).

Philip Goff gives a conceivability argument [goff-2009](../sources/goff-2009.md). It seems conceivable that any
collection of subjects exists without a further subject, and hence conceivable that there is a
*micro-experiential zombie*: a creature physically just like you, every one of whose micro-parts is
conscious, but which has no consciousness at the macro level. If conceivability is a guide to
possibility, micro-experience does not necessitate macro-experience and so cannot ground it. The
argument is especially awkward for panpsychists, because it has the same form as the zombie argument
many of them use against physicalism. If it works against physicalism it seems to work against
constitutive panpsychism too, and the explanatory gap panpsychism was meant to close reopens one step
higher, between micro-experience and macro-experience.

### Responses

Responses fall into a few families, set out in detail by Chalmers [chalmers-2016](../sources/chalmers-2016.md) and the
Stanford survey [sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md).

*Phenomenal bonding* holds that micro-subjects compose a macro-subject when they stand in a special
relation, as bricks compose a house only when they are mortared together. Goff proposes that this
relation exists but that its nature is not yet positively understood, which would explain why combination
looks impossible from where we stand [goff-2016](../sources/goff-2016.md). Others have suggested that the bonding
relation is simply *co-consciousness*, the relation two experiences bear when they are experienced
together.

*Combinationism* denies the key premise outright. Luke Roelofs argues that a whole can literally share
the experiences of its parts, much as a table has the mass of its parts, and points to split-brain
patients, conjoined twins and tightly coordinated groups as evidence that the boundaries of a subject
shift with how tightly its parts are integrated [roelofs-2019](../sources/roelofs-2019.md). This reply is developed in
[subjects-can-combine](../arguments/subjects-can-combine.md).

*Panqualityism* and *panprotopsychism* deny that the fundamental entities are subjects at all. If
micro-entities carry only unexperienced qualities, or properties that are merely precursors of
experience, there are no micro-subjects to sum. The price is that subjecthood must then be explained
somewhere further up, which threatens to reintroduce the gap.

*Emergentist* panpsychism gives up constitution. Macro-subjects are new fundamental entities that arise
from micro-experiential ones by basic laws of nature. This avoids the need for intelligible combination
but abandons the main advantage constitutive panpsychism claimed over dualism, and faces worries about how
the emergent mind can make a causal difference.

*Cosmopsychism* reverses the direction of explanation. If the fundamental subject is the whole cosmos
and individual minds are derived from it, nothing needs to be combined. The cost is a mirror-image
difficulty, the [decombination-problem](decombination-problem.md): how one cosmic subject gives rise to many bounded
subjects. Chalmers judges this reverse problem "just as hard as the combination problem."

### Relation to the binding problem

The combination problem should not be confused with the neuroscientific [binding-problem](binding-problem.md).
Binding concerns how separately processed features (the colour, shape and motion of a single ball) come
to be experienced as belonging to one object within an existing subject's experience. Combination
concerns how whole experiential perspectives could merge into a further subject. A solution to either
would not by itself solve the other.

### In plain terms

Panpsychism says every speck of matter has a tiny flicker of experience, so your mind is built out of
those flickers. The combination problem asks how the building works. Your experience right now is one
thing, had by one "you." How do countless separate little experiences, each private to its own speck,
merge into that one? William James argued that they simply cannot: put a hundred feelings together and
you still have a hundred separate feelings, not one big feeling that contains them. Later philosophers
added related puzzles, such as how a few basic ingredients could produce the huge variety of colours,
sounds and smells we experience, and why experience feels smooth when the brain underneath is a busy
crowd of separate cells. Defenders of panpsychism have proposed a special "glue" that binds small minds
into big ones, or argued that minds really do merge in cases like split brains, or flipped the picture so
that the whole universe is the one basic mind. Nobody yet claims a complete answer.
