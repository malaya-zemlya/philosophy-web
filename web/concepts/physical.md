To call something **physical** is to place it in the world that physics describes: the domain of
particles and fields, mass and charge, space, time and causal law, together with everything built
out of these. Rocks, planets, chemical bonds and nerve impulses are physical in this sense; numbers,
ghosts and (on some views) conscious experiences are candidates for being something else. The notion
seems obvious until one tries to define it, and the difficulty matters, because
[physicalism](../positions/physicalism.md), the thesis that everything is physical, can say no more than its key term
allows. A theory that says everything is ultimately made of X has told us nothing until it says what
X is.

Philosophers sometimes call this the *condition question*: what condition must an object, event or
property meet to count as physical (Stoljar, [SEP](../sources/sep-physicalism.md))? There is no agreed
answer. The main proposals are surveyed below, and the best-known obstacle to the most natural one,
[hempels-dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md), has an entry of its own.

### From matter to the physical

The older word for physicalism is *materialism*, and the older notion behind it is **matter**. For
the ancient atomists, everything was atoms moving in the void; for Descartes, body was extended
substance, defined by size, shape and motion, interacting only by contact. That picture gave a sharp
test for being material: occupy space and push or be pushed.

Physics itself dissolved the test. Newton's gravity acts at a distance through empty space, and later
physics added fields, energy, spacetime curvature, and quantum states that have no definite position
at all. None of these is matter in the old sense, yet all are paradigms of the physical. The word
*physicalism* entered philosophy in the 1930s, in the work of Otto Neurath and Rudolf Carnap, and it
came to be preferred partly because it ties the doctrine to physics rather than to a superseded idea
of stuff (Stoljar, [SEP](../sources/sep-physicalism.md)). Some authors draw a sceptical moral from the
history. Chomsky ([2000](../sources/chomsky-2000.md)) argues that since Newton there has been no fixed
concept of body at all, and Montero ([1999](../sources/montero-1999.md)) that the mind–body problem is
really a "body problem", the problem of saying what the physical side of the contrast is.

### The theory-based conception

The most common answer ties the physical to physical *theory*: a property is physical if it is the
kind of property physics tells us about, such as mass, charge or spin, or is built out of such
properties. Lewis ([1994](../sources/lewis-1994.md)) states his materialism this way, in terms of the
posits of present physics or something not too different from it. An early version is Feigl's
distinction ([1958](../sources/feigl-1958.md)) between a broad sense of "physical", meaning anything that
belongs to the spatiotemporal, causal and lawful order, and a narrow sense, meaning the kind of
concepts and laws adequate to explain inorganic nature.

The theory-based conception immediately raises the question *which* physics. Present physics is
known to be incomplete, so a physicalism indexed to it is very probably false. A future or ideal
physics would avoid that problem, but nobody can say what it will contain, so a physicalism indexed
to it seems to have no definite content. This is [hempels-dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md), set out by Hempel
([1980](../sources/hempel-1980.md)), and much of the modern literature on the physical consists of replies
to it.

### The object-based conception

A second answer starts from examples rather than theories: a property is physical if it is the kind
of property had by paradigmatic physical objects, such as tables, stones and planets, and their
constituents (Jackson, [1998](../sources/jackson-1998.md)). The two conceptions can come apart. Physics
characterizes things largely by what they do, by their dispositions and their relations to one
another, and may say nothing about the intrinsic nature that underlies those dispositions. If
ordinary objects have such an intrinsic nature, it counts as physical on the object-based conception
but not on the theory-based one. Stoljar ([2001](../sources/stoljar-2001.md)) uses this gap to distinguish
"t-physical" from "o-physical" properties, and the same thought, going back to Russell
([1927](../sources/russell-1927.md)), underlies [russellian-monism](../positions/russellian-monism.md), which locates consciousness
in the intrinsic nature that physics leaves out.

The object-based conception has its own difficulty, sometimes called the *panpsychism problem*.
Suppose the paradigm physical objects turned out to be, at bottom, made of something mental, as
[panpsychism](panpsychism.md) proposes. Then mentality would count as physical by definition, and a world
that most people would describe as one where physicalism fails would come out physicalist.

### Narrow and broad senses

Both conceptions come in a narrow and a broad form. In the narrow sense, only the properties of
fundamental physics are physical: the masses, charges and field values of elementary particles and
fields. In the broad sense, anything that is fixed by or grounded in those properties is physical
too. Being a mountain, a protein or a hurricane is not a property mentioned in fundamental physics,
but on the broad reading it is physical, because once the fundamental physical facts are settled the
facts about mountains, proteins and hurricanes are settled as well. Physicalism is usually stated
with the narrow sense in the base and the broad sense in the conclusion: everything is physical in
the broad sense because everything is fixed by what is physical in the narrow one.

### Example: entropy

Entropy shows how the standing of a quantity can seem to shift while its physicality does not.
Clausius ([1865](../sources/clausius-1865.md)) introduced it as a thermodynamic quantity defined through
heat and temperature, apparently as basic as energy. Boltzmann ([1877](../sources/boltzmann-1877.md))
recast it statistically, as a measure ($S = k \log W$) of the number of molecular arrangements
compatible with a system's large-scale state. On that analysis entropy is no longer fundamental, and
Jaynes ([1957](../sources/jaynes-1957.md)) went further, reading it as a measure of the information an
observer lacks about the system's exact state. Entropy then looked less like a property of the gas
than of our description of it. Later physics restored its weight. Landauer
([1961](../sources/landauer-1961.md)) showed that erasing information has an unavoidable thermodynamic
cost, Bekenstein ([1973](../sources/bekenstein-1973.md)) assigned black holes an entropy proportional to
the area of their horizon, and Wheeler ([1990](../sources/wheeler-1990.md)) proposed, under the slogan
"it from bit", that physical things are at bottom informational.

The distinction between narrow and broad senses shows what did and did not change. Statistical
mechanics moved entropy out of the narrow class of fundamental quantities, but not out of the
physical. Like temperature, once identified with the mean kinetic energy of molecules, it stayed
physical in the broad sense because it is fixed by the underlying mechanics. What made it look
mathematical was that Boltzmann's $W$ counts possibilities rather than describing the actual state:
it is the number of arrangements the system *could* be in, given its large-scale state, which makes
entropy seem a fact about a space of combinations rather than about the gas. But that count is fixed
by the system's physical constraints, its energy, volume and number of particles, so it remains a
physical fact even though it is stated as a number of possibilities. The informational reading raises a different question: whether entropy is an
objective feature of a system or relative to how the system is described, and, if it is relative to
what someone knows, whether something mental has entered physics. The usual answer is that it is
relative to a choice of large-scale variables, not to anyone's state of mind. "It from bit", for its
part, points toward the structuralist conception discussed below, on which the physical and the
mathematical are not sharply opposed; on that conception entropy's status never changed at all. A
definition of the physical as whatever current physics says would have given different verdicts on
entropy in 1870, 1910 and 1990, which illustrates the instability behind [hempels-dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md).

### The via negativa

A third strategy gives up on saying what the physical is and says instead what it is not. On the
*via negativa*, what physicalists really need is that the fundamental level contains nothing mental
(Montero & Papineau, [2005](../sources/montero-papineau-2005.md)). Wilson ([2006](../sources/wilson-2006.md))
combines this with the theory-based view: the physical is what current or future physics treats,
subject to the constraint that nothing fundamental is mental. The negative clause gives a future
physics a definite boundary, which is why it is the most popular reply to Hempel's dilemma.

The strategy has costs. A crude version, on which "physical" simply means "non-mental", misclassifies
cases. The vital force posited by nineteenth-century vitalists was neither mental nor physical, and
on the [type-identity](../positions/type-identity.md) of mind and brain, a property such as being in pain would be both.
The more careful "no fundamental mentality" version avoids these, but it still rules out by
definition a world whose basic properties are at once mental and physical, which some philosophers
think should be left as a substantive possibility.

### Structuralist and attitudinal accounts

Two further proposals try to sidestep the problem. *Structuralists* hold that a physical theory is
one framed in a restricted vocabulary: logical, mathematical, causal and lawlike terms, with no
reference to the intrinsic character of what is described. An idea of this kind runs from Russell
([1927](../sources/russell-1927.md)) to recent Russellian monists such as Goff ([2017](../sources/goff-2017.md)).
Because any future physics must stay within that vocabulary, physicalism keeps a definite content as
physics changes. The objection is that real physics seems to say a good deal more, about mass,
energy and particular kinds of particle, than any purely structural vocabulary can express.

Ney ([2008](../sources/ney-2008.md)) proposes instead that physicalism is not a thesis at all but an
*attitude*: the resolve to form one's ontology wholly according to what physics says exists. Critics
reply that the attitude is neither necessary nor sufficient for physicalism. Ancient atomists were
materialists before physics existed as a discipline, and a scientist who kept the attitude in a
future where physics had adopted irreducible mental quantities would not thereby be a physicalist.

### Why the definition matters

The choice among these accounts decides which views count as rivals to physicalism. If physics one
day posits fundamental quantities with an experiential character, the theory-based conception says
physicalism has been vindicated, while the via negativa says it has been refuted. The same
indeterminacy affects the principles physicalists rely on. The [completeness-of-physics](completeness-of-physics.md)
and the [causal-closure](causal-closure.md) of the physical are only as definite as the word "physical" they
contain, which is why Spurrett and Papineau ([1999](../sources/spurrett-papineau-1999.md)) restate
completeness negatively, as the claim that physical effects have sufficient causes that are not
distinctively mental. Crane and Mellor ([1990](../sources/crane-mellor-1990.md)) go further and argue that,
lacking any principled boundary for the physical, there is no real question of physicalism at all.

### In plain terms

"Physical" sounds like the easiest word in the debate about minds and brains, but it is surprisingly
hard to pin down. Once it meant solid stuff bumping into other stuff; physics has since filled the
world with forces, fields and quantum states that are nothing like that. So philosophers try other
definitions. One says the physical is whatever physics talks about, but today's physics is
unfinished and tomorrow's is unknown, a puzzle called [Hempel's dilemma](../arguments/hempels-dilemma.md).
Another says it is whatever ordinary objects like rocks are made of, whatever that turns out to be.
A third says only what it is not: nothing at the bottom of reality is a mind. Which definition you
pick changes what the claim "everything is physical" actually says, and so changes whether it is
true.
