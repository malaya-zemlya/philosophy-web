The **explanatory gap** is the apparent failure of physical science to explain why physical processes
in the brain are accompanied by conscious experience, and why by experience of one kind rather than
another. Elsewhere in science, identifying a familiar phenomenon with something physical makes the
phenomenon intelligible: once we learn what heat or water is at the molecular level, nothing about it
remains mysterious. Identifying a conscious state with a brain state does not seem to work that way.
Even if pain is the firing of certain nerve fibres, we cannot see why that firing should feel like
anything, or why it should feel like pain rather than like an itch. The term was introduced by Joseph
Levine ([1983](../sources/levine-1983.md)).

### Levine's argument

Levine's contrast is between two identity statements:

- *Heat is the motion of molecules.*
- *Pain is the firing of C-fibres.*

We pick out heat by what it does: it makes things expand, melts ice, boils water and causes
sensations of warmth. Physics shows that molecular motion does exactly these things, and so the first
identity explains heat completely. Asked why molecular motion should be heat, we can answer that
molecular motion does everything heat does, and that is all there is to being heat. This is
*reductive explanation*: the phenomenon is analysed in terms of a causal role, and the physical
process is shown to fill the role.

Pain also has a causal role: it is caused by damage and causes wincing, avoidance and complaint. A
complete neuroscience could show that C-fibre firing fills that role. But pain has something more,
the way it feels, and that does not seem to be captured by any causal role. After the explanation is
complete, the question "but why should *that* feel like *this*?" still makes sense. The second
identity, if true, is a brute fact we would have to accept without understanding. That residue is
the explanatory gap.

### An epistemological, not a metaphysical, claim

Levine presented the gap as the defensible core of Saul Kripke's modal argument against the identity
theory (Kripke, [1980](../sources/kripke-1980.md)). Kripke had argued that "pain is C-fibre firing", unlike "heat is
molecular motion", cannot be a necessary truth discovered a posteriori, and so must be false. Levine
did not accept that conclusion. What survives of Kripke's argument, he held, is a point about
knowledge: the psychophysical identity cannot be made intelligible the way the heat identity can.
This leaves open whether the identity is true.

Levine has kept to this restraint. In *Purple haze* ([2001](../sources/levine-2001.md)) he treats the gap as real
and as something a physicalist must account for, while declining to infer that physicalism is false.
Whether an explanatory gap of this kind implies that experience is something over and above the
physical is the separate question of the [metaphysical-gap](metaphysical-gap.md).

### Precursors

The thought is older than the name. Leibniz's mill ([*Monadology*](../sources/leibniz-monadology.md), §17) asks us to
imagine a thinking machine enlarged until we could walk around inside it as in a mill: we would find
only parts pushing one another, and nothing that would explain a perception. Thomas Nagel
([1974](../sources/nagel-1974.md)) argued that we do not have even the beginnings of a conception of how a
physicalist identity of experience with a brain state could be true, since physical descriptions are
objective and experience is tied to a point of view. David Chalmers's [hard-problem](hard-problem.md)
([1995](../sources/chalmers-1995.md)) is a close relative: the "easy problems" are those that reductive
explanation of causal role can solve, and the hard problem is the residue that it cannot.

### How wide is the gap?

The claim comes in versions of increasing strength (Van Gulick, [SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness.md)).

- **Practical.** With current theories and concepts, we cannot now explain experience physically.
  This is hardly disputed, and many physicalists expect it to change as neuroscience develops.
- **In principle, for us.** Human beings will never be able to close the gap, because of the kind of
  concepts available to us. This is Colin McGinn's [mysterianism](../positions/mysterianism.md) ([1989](../sources/mcginn-1989.md)).
- **In principle, for anyone.** No possible thinker could derive the facts of experience from the
  physical facts. In this strong form the explanatory gap becomes the epistemic gap of
  [materialism-types](materialism-types.md), and dualists use it as the first premise of an argument against
  physicalism.

### Responses

Responses divide according to whether they close the gap, explain it, or exploit it.

*Closing it.* Some physicalists deny that anything is left over once the causal roles are explained.
Daniel Dennett ([1991](../sources/dennett-1991.md)) holds that the sense of a residue reflects the limits of
current theory and a misleading picture of experience, not a real explanatory failure. This is the
line of [type-a-materialism](../positions/type-a-materialism.md).

*Explaining it.* Others grant the gap but argue that physicalism predicts it. On the *phenomenal
concept strategy*, we think about our experiences through special first-person concepts that have no
a priori connection to physical concepts, so no physical explanation could ever *feel* explanatory,
even if the identity is true (Loar, [1990](../sources/loar-1990.md); Papineau, [2002](../sources/papineau-2002.md)). This
is the line of [type-b-materialism](../positions/type-b-materialism.md). Block and Stalnaker ([1999](../sources/block-stalnaker-1999.md))
add that the heat and water identities are themselves not derivable a priori from physics, so the
contrast Levine draws may be less sharp than it looks.

*Exploiting it.* Dualists and Russellian monists take the gap as evidence that experience is not
wholly physical, or not wholly captured by physics. The [zombie-conceivability](../arguments/zombie-conceivability.md) and the
[knowledge-argument](../arguments/knowledge-argument.md) are attempts to make the gap rigorous. The web's
[explanatory-gap](../arguments/explanatory-gap.md) uses it as evidence in a narrower dispute, against the claim that all
mental states are substrate-independent.

### In plain terms

Science is very good at explaining things by showing what they are made of. Once you know that heat
is just jiggling molecules, nothing about heat is puzzling anymore. Brains are different. Suppose
scientists find exactly which nerve activity happens whenever you feel pain. You can still ask: why
does *that* activity hurt? Why does it feel like anything at all? The answer "it just does" is
exactly what science normally avoids. That leftover puzzle is the explanatory gap. Levine, who named
it, did not conclude that the mind is something non-physical, only that we do not understand how it
could be physical. Others go further and say the gap shows that experience really is something
extra, a stronger claim discussed under the [metaphysical-gap](metaphysical-gap.md).
