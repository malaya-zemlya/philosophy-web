The *mereological fallacy* is the error of ascribing to a part of a creature psychological attributes
that can intelligibly be ascribed only to the creature as a whole. The term is due to the
neuroscientist Maxwell Bennett and the philosopher Peter Hacker, who argued that much neuroscientific
talk commits it: saying that the brain sees, decides, believes or interprets, when it is the person
who sees and decides ([Bennett & Hacker, 2003](../sources/bennett-hacker-2003.md)). "Mereological" refers to
the theory of parts and wholes.

### In consciousness research

Matvey Sysoev identifies the fallacy as one of two methodological obstacles in the empirical study of
consciousness ([Sysoev, 2025](../sources/sysoev-2025.md)). It occurs when a theory supposes that
consciousness, or some cognitive system, "gains access" to experiential content generated by the
brain, although it is the person, not a part of their mind, to whom that content is available. It
also occurs when human-like consciousness is ascribed to a single brain region or neural network.
Sysoev distinguishes a strong and a weak use of the charge. On the strong use, statements that
ascribe conscious states to a brain are simply false. On the weak use, which he prefers, the error is
to make such an ascription *without grounds*, closing off a question that should stay open, since
ordinary language ties thinking and perceiving to persons rather than to their parts.

### Objections

Daniel Dennett and John Searle replied to Bennett and Hacker in a joint volume
([Bennett et al., 2007](../sources/bennett-dennett-hacker-searle-2007.md)). Dennett defended the
neuroscientists' psychological vocabulary as an extended use that commits no conceptual error, and
Searle held that the brain is the proper seat of conscious states. The dispute
bears directly on the [search for neural correlates](neural-correlates-of-consciousness.md),
which presupposes that conscious states can be paired with states of brain parts.

### In plain terms

People see, think and feel; their eyes and brains do not, or at least not in the same sense. The
mereological fallacy is the mistake of saying that a part of you does something only the whole of you
can do, as when a scientist says "the brain decides". Whether this is really a mistake, or just a
harmless way of speaking, is disputed.
