The distinction between *time represented* and *time of representing* separates two times that are
easy to confuse: the time an event is represented as happening, and the time at which the
representation of it occurs or arrives. A letter dated 1 March that arrives on 10 March represents an
event of 1 March, and the reader learns of it on 10 March. Nothing prevents a letter about an earlier
event from arriving after a letter about a later one, and a careful reader is not misled, because the
date travels inside the letter. Daniel Dennett and Marcel Kinsbourne
([1992](../sources/dennett-kinsbourne-1992.md)) argued that the brain faces the same problem and that much
confusion about the timing of conscious experience comes from ignoring the distinction.

### Content and vehicle

The distinction applies to time the more general difference between what a representation is
*about* (its *content*) and the physical thing that carries it (its *vehicle*), whose properties need
not match ([content-vehicle-distinction](content-vehicle-distinction.md)). A representation of a brief flash need not itself
be brief, and a representation of *A before B* need not consist of a representation of A followed by
a representation of B. What matters is the order the brain takes the events to have, not the order in
which its own signals happen to be processed.

### The Battle of New Orleans

Dennett and Kinsbourne illustrate the point with the British Empire before the telegraph. The Battle
of New Orleans was fought on 8 January 1815, about two weeks after a peace treaty had been signed in
Europe, because news of the treaty had not yet reached America. Imagine a commander in Calcutta who
receives news of the battle by a fast route before news of the treaty arrives by a slow one. Judging
by arrival times, the battle would seem to have come first. Because letters carried dates, the
commander could correct for this: the time information was part of the *content* of the messages, so
their arrival times did not matter.

### Why the brain needs it

Signals in the nervous system travel at different speeds. A touch on the foot reaches the brain later
than a touch on the face, and visual processing takes longer than auditory processing. One possible
design would hold every signal until the slowest had arrived and then process them all together, so
that the brain's own processing matched the order of events in the world. Dennett and Kinsbourne
reject this on what they call an "obvious engineering reason": it would slow everything down to a
worst-case schedule. A better design processes each signal as soon as it arrives and carries
information about timing within the content, much as a distributed computer system attaches
timestamps to messages that may arrive out of order. The brain cannot literally write the date on its
messages, but it can draw the same kind of timing information from the content of its signals.

### Consequences for consciousness

If timing can be carried in content, there need be no single moment at which a perception "enters
consciousness", and no single place, which Dennett calls the *Cartesian theater*, where all contents
arrive to be viewed together. This is the basis of his *Multiple Drafts model*. It also shapes how
Dennett and Kinsbourne read [postdictive](postdiction.md) illusions such as colour phi, in which
later input affects how an earlier moment is experienced. They argue that the question whether the
brain doctors the input before it is experienced ("Stalinesque") or rewrites the record afterwards
("Orwellian") has no answer at such short time scales.

The distinction is widely accepted as a point about representation. What is disputed is how much
follows from it. In the philosophy of temporal experience, some theorists hold that an experience of
succession must itself be spread out in time in step with what it presents. On Barry Dainton's survey
([SEP](../sources/sep-consciousness-temporal.md)), Dennett and Kinsbourne's examples are among the main
reasons that view is questioned. Critics of the Multiple Drafts model accept the distinction but
reject the step from "no evidence could decide between the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories" to
"there is no fact of the matter". On their view, a realist about experience should conclude only that
the fact is hard to discover.

### In plain terms

The time something happened is not the same as the time you hear about it. A letter posted on Monday
might arrive after one posted on Wednesday, but you are not fooled about the order because each letter
has a date on it. The brain has the same problem: signals from your toes take longer to arrive than
signals from your nose. Dennett and Kinsbourne argued that the brain does not wait for everything to
arrive and then play it back in order. It works on each signal as it comes in and keeps track of
timing inside the information itself. If they are right, there may be no exact moment when something
"becomes conscious".
