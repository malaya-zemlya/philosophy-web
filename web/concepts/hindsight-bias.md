*Hindsight bias*, also called the *knew-it-all-along effect*, is the tendency, once one knows how an
event turned out, to overestimate how predictable that outcome was, and in particular to misremember
one's own earlier expectations as having been closer to the actual outcome than they were. After an
election, a market crash or a war, many people sincerely recall having seen it coming, and forget the
doubts and the rival scenarios they took seriously at the time.

### Two forms of the bias

The effect was established experimentally by the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff, who distinguished two
forms of it. In the *judgment* form, people who are told the outcome of an unfamiliar historical
episode rate that outcome as having been more likely than people who are told nothing, and they
largely fail to notice that the outcome knowledge has influenced them. Fischhoff called this
*creeping determinism*: what happened comes to look as though it had to happen
([fischhoff-1975](../sources/fischhoff-1975.md)). In the *memory* form, people misremember their own earlier forecasts. Before
President Nixon's 1972 visits to China and the Soviet Union, subjects in a study by Fischhoff and Ruth
Beyth gave probabilities for a range of possible outcomes. Asked afterwards to recall those
probabilities, they remembered having given higher ones to the events that had in fact occurred
([fischhoff-beyth-1975](../sources/fischhoff-beyth-1975.md)). The two forms are related but distinct: the first distorts an
estimate made now, the second distorts the record of a belief held then.

### Why it matters

Hindsight bias is philosophically interesting less as an error of prediction than as an error of
*self-knowledge*. The person who says "I always knew" is not lying. Their present report of their past
belief has been rebuilt from what they now know, and the rebuilt version feels exactly like a memory.
This makes the bias a close cousin of [confabulation](confabulation.md), in which people supply a sincere but
mistaken account of *why* they acted or judged as they did. The two misdescribe different things,
hindsight bias the content of one's earlier beliefs and confabulation the causes of one's present
behaviour, but both show self-reports being *reconstructed* from plausibility rather than read off a
record of what actually occurred ([introspection-is-fallible-representation](../claims/introspection-is-fallible-representation.md)). The bias also has
practical consequences: it makes past decisions look more foolish, and past forecasters more
negligent, than they were, which is why it is studied in law, medicine and accident investigation.

### In plain terms

Hindsight bias is the "I knew it all along" feeling. After something happens, it seems obvious that it
was going to happen, and people genuinely remember having predicted it, even when at the time they
were unsure or expected something else. Experiments show this clearly: ask people for their
predictions, wait until the event happens, then ask them what they had predicted, and their memories
shift toward the real outcome. They are not lying; their memory has quietly been rewritten.
