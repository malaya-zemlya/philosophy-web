The *grain problem* is the difficulty that experience and the brain processes supposed to underlie it seem
to have completely different textures. A patch of colour in your visual field looks smooth and
homogeneous, the same red through and through. The neural activity that accompanies it is anything but:
it is a vast, discontinuous swarm of separate neurons firing, ions crossing membranes and molecules
diffusing across synapses. If the experience simply *is* that brain activity, or is made out of it, why
does none of that grain show up in the experience? The problem was first pressed by Wilfrid Sellars
against the mind-brain identity theory, given its clearest statement by Grover Maxwell, and developed at
length by Michael Lockwood [lockwood-1993](../sources/lockwood-1993.md). It now figures as one aspect of the
[combination-problem-panpsychism](combination-problem-panpsychism.md).

### Sellars and the pink ice cube

Sellars's starting point was a pink ice cube [sellars-1962](../sources/sellars-1962.md). In ordinary perception the ice
cube presents itself "as something which is pink through and through, as a pink continuum, all the
regions of which, however small, are pink." It presents itself, in Sellars's phrase, as *ultimately
homogeneous*. Science, by contrast, tells us that the ice cube is a system of colourless particles. A
property of a system is normally made up of the properties and relations of its parts, the way being a
ladder is made up of the rungs being cylindrical and the frame being rectangular. But pinkness does not
seem to be made up of anything in that way; there is no arrangement of colourless particles that adds up
to a homogeneous pink [sep-sellars](../sources/sep-sellars.md).

Science responds by relocating the pinkness from the ice cube to the perceiver, as a sensation. Sellars
argued that this only moves the problem. If sensations are processes in the brain, and the brain too is a
system of particles, then the homogeneous pink sensation must somehow be composed of particulate neural
events, which is just as puzzling as the pink ice cube being composed of particles. He pressed this
against the identity theory of mind directly [sellars-1965](../sources/sellars-1965.md), and concluded that a completed
science would have to recognize new, non-particulate basic entities to accommodate sensory qualities.

### Maxwell, Lockwood and the Russellian setting

The problem gained new force within *Russellian monism*, the view that physics describes only the
structure of matter and leaves its intrinsic nature open, so that experience might be the intrinsic
nature of certain brain processes [intrinsic-structural-distinction](intrinsic-structural-distinction.md). Maxwell defended an
identity theory of this Russellian kind and accepted that it faces the grain problem
[maxwell-1978](../sources/maxwell-1978.md). On such a view, introspection reveals brain events *as they are in themselves*.
But then experience ought to have the structure physics attributes to those events, and it plainly does
not appear to.

Lockwood broke the vague worry into three distinct difficulties [lockwood-1993](../sources/lockwood-1993.md):

1. *Coarse-graining.* Experience is far less finely structured than its physical basis. A flawless
   violin note is accompanied by a messy concatenation of electrical changes in billions of neurons. How
   are all these discontinuities glossed over to yield the smooth phenomenal tone?
2. *Structural mismatch.* Even at a coarse level, the structure of experience does not match the
   structure of the brain. Three-dimensional spatial arrangement is central to all physical structure,
   yet experience has no global spatial layout corresponding to the layout of the brain, only separate
   modality-specific fields that represent the outside world. In John Foster's image, the phenomenal pegs
   seem the wrong shape for the holes that the physical description leaves open.
3. *Qualitative diversity.* Experience contains an enormous variety of qualities, sounds wholly unlike
   flashes of light, while its physical ingredients are qualitatively uniform. Neurons in the auditory
   cortex and in the visual cortex fire in much the same way, and at the level of fundamental physics a
   handful of particle types make up everything.

The second and third of these reappear in David Chalmers's anatomy of the combination problem as the
*structural mismatch problem* and the *palette problem*. Chalmers reserves the name "grain problem" for
the first: how micro-experiences could combine "to yield homogeneous macroexperiences, such as a
homogeneous experience of red, instead of an enormous jagged array of distinct qualities"
[chalmers-2016](../sources/chalmers-2016.md).

### Proposed solutions

Maxwell suggested that the physical structure matching experience might be found at an unfamiliar
"middle-sized" level, between quantum microstructure and the classical structure of everyday objects
[maxwell-1978](../sources/maxwell-1978.md). Lockwood objected that it is unclear what such structure would be, unless it is
simply emergent structure, which would give up the identity theory's ambitions.

Lockwood's own proposal draws on quantum mechanics [lockwood-1993](../sources/lockwood-1993.md). A quantum system can be
described in any of infinitely many bases, each corresponding to a set of questions one could ask of it,
and none is privileged by the theory. Lockwood suggested that a conscious subject views its own brain
"through the eyes" of one preferred set of compatible observables, as an observer in relativity views the
world from one frame of reference. Since quantum theory recognizes no rock-bottom level of structure,
there is no fundamental grain that experience is obliged to reproduce, and the smoothness of experience
reflects the viewpoint from which the brain is given to consciousness.

Other responses do not depend on physics. Daniel Stoljar has suggested that the problem may confuse the
structure *of* experience with the structure of what experience *represents*: the visual field presents
a smooth red surface, but that need not mean the experience itself has a smooth, unstructured nature
[stoljar-2001](../sources/stoljar-2001.md), [sep-panpsychism](../sources/sep-panpsychism.md). Philip Goff argues that the brain does contain
structure isomorphic to experience once one looks at less basic levels of neural organization
[goff-2017](../sources/goff-2017.md). Luke Roelofs, echoing Leibniz, suggests that experience may have more structure
than introspection reveals [roelofs-2019](../sources/roelofs-2019.md). Finally, cosmopsychists argue that the problem eases
if experience is grounded top-down in the whole rather than built up from micro-parts, since the
structure of a macro-experience is then no longer required to be inherited from micro-structure
[nagasawa-wager-2017](../sources/nagasawa-wager-2017.md); that move invites the reverse difficulty discussed under
[decombination-problem](decombination-problem.md).

### Scope

The grain problem is not specific to panpsychism. It confronts any view that identifies experience with,
or builds it out of, physical processes while also taking introspection to reveal the character of
experience as it really is. Strict functionalists largely escape it, since for them experience relates to
neural processes as software relates to hardware, and the fine physical details are irrelevant to the
mental description. Lockwood argued that this escape is bought at the cost of failing to explain why any
functional state should feel like anything at all. The problem is sharpest for Russellian monists and
constitutive panpsychists, because they insist that experience is the very nature of the physical
process, not merely a high-level pattern it implements.

The grain problem is distinct from the [binding-problem](binding-problem.md), which asks how separately processed
features come to be experienced together rather than why experience lacks the brain's granularity. It
is also unrelated to [grain](grain.md), the level of detail at which a functional role is individuated.

### In plain terms

When you look at a red apple, the redness looks perfectly smooth: red everywhere, with no gaps or
graininess. But scientists tell us the brain activity going on while you see it is a crackling swarm of
billions of separate cells firing on and off. If your experience simply *is* that activity, why does it
feel so smooth and simple? That is the grain problem. The philosopher Wilfrid Sellars first raised it
with the example of a pink ice cube that looks pink "through and through," even though it is made of
colourless particles. Proposed answers include the idea that consciousness views the brain from a
special angle, as a picture made of tiny dots looks smooth from across the room, and the idea that we
confuse how the apple looks with how the experience itself is built.
