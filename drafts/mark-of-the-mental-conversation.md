## 1. How philosophers separate the mental from the physical

**Q.** How do philosophers separate mental from physical?

**A.** There are three broad ways of drawing the line. Each proposed criterion has well-known
counterexamples, which is a large part of why the mind–body problem is still open.

### Find a "mark of the mental"

One feature that every mental state has and no physical state has.

- **Non-extension (Descartes).** Body is extended: it has size, shape and position. Mind is thinking
  substance and has no spatial extent. It seems odd to ask how many centimetres wide a thought is.
  This is the basis of substance dualism.
- **Intentionality (Brentano, 1874).** Mental states are *about* something: a belief is about Paris,
  a fear is fear *of* something; a rock is about nothing. Objections run in both directions. Pains
  and moods seem to be about nothing, and maps, sentences and thermostats seem to carry content
  without having minds (hence Searle's distinction between intrinsic and derived intentionality).
- **Privileged access or incorrigibility.** You know your own mental states directly and without
  inference; physical facts are publicly checkable. Rorty (1970) argued that incorrigibility is the
  mark of the mental, and also that it is a social practice neuroscience could erode. Confabulation
  research shows how fallible introspection is.
- **Phenomenal character.** There is something it is like to be in a conscious state. This leaves
  out unconscious beliefs, so many philosophers split the mental into an intentional part and a
  phenomenal part rather than look for a single mark.

### Define "physical" first, then ask what is left over

The physical side is just as hard to define.

- **Theory-based:** physical is whatever physics describes. This runs into Hempel's dilemma: current
  physics is incomplete, and a future physics has no definite content yet.
- **Object-based:** physical is the kind of thing tables and stones are made of. If panpsychism is
  true, minds would count as physical by definition.
- **Via negativa:** physical means *not* fundamentally mental, which makes the distinction circular.

Montero and Chomsky push this to the conclusion that the real puzzle is a "body problem".

### Separate them through a relation, not an essence

- **Supervenience:** no mental difference without a physical difference.
- **Multiple realizability:** the same mental kind could be realized in very different physical
  systems, the usual argument that mental kinds are not physical kinds.
- **Reduction and emergence:** whether the mental can be derived from the physical, or adds
  something new.
- **Gap arguments** (knowledge argument, zombies, explanatory gap) rely on differences in what we can
  know or conceive to argue for a distinction in reality; much of the debate is whether that step is
  legitimate.

Where you stand determines the distinction you end up with. A dualist treats it as a real division in
what exists, a physicalist as a difference in concepts or levels of description, and a neutral or
dual-aspect monist as two aspects of one underlying stuff.

## 2. Writing the "mark of the mental" entry

**Q.** Mark of the mental seems like a good article to make.

**A.** Created `concept-mark-of-the-mental` (encyclopedia style, author `generic`, status
`contested`), placed in chapter 02 (mind and body) after the mind–body problem. It covers, in order:

- non-extension (Descartes), and why it assumes dualism from the start;
- intentionality (Brentano; Chisholm's restatement in terms of how sentences about the mind behave),
  with the counterexamples of pains and moods (answered by Crane and the representationalists) and
  of maps and sentences (answered by intrinsic vs. derived intentionality), and the naturalist who
  keeps intentionality as the mark but treats it as physical;
- consciousness, with the problem of unconscious beliefs, Searle's connection principle and
  phenomenal intentionality;
- privileged access (Rorty on incorrigibility), against confabulation and unreliable introspection;
- location in the head, defeated by the extended-mind thesis;
- whether there is any single mark: Chalmers's psychological vs. phenomenal concepts of mind,
  Rorty 1979, Ryle, and the cluster-concept view; each answer yields a different mind–body problem.

New sources: Chisholm 1957 (*Perceiving*), Crane 1998 ("Intentionality as the mark of the mental"),
Rorty 1979 (*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*), Jacob's SEP entry "Intentionality".

## 3. Objection: thoughts are not always about something

**Q.** I definitely disagree that thoughts are always about something. Thoughts have to be formed and
focused well enough to be about something, and they not always are, especially if one is tired,
drunk, meditating, etc.

**A.** This is a stronger objection than the pains-and-moods one, because it targets thought itself,
the case Brentano's thesis seems to fit best. Added to the entry:

- **The objection.** Drowsy, exhausted, drunk or slack-minded thinking can be fragmentary:
  half-images, the rhythm of a sentence with nothing asserted, an urge that never settles on an
  object. Contemplative traditions report wakeful awareness with no object at all: Forman's *pure
  consciousness event* (1990) and Metzinger's *minimal phenomenal experience* (2020).
- **Two intentionalist replies, each costly.**
  - The state still has content, only vague content, as a blurred photo is still a photo *of*
    something. This must say what a content is when no one, the thinker included, could say what it
    is.
  - The state is not a thought but bare awareness. This concedes that some mental states lack
    aboutness, giving up half of Brentano's thesis.
- **Upshot.** These states are plainly conscious, so they favour consciousness over intentionality as
  the mark. They suggest aboutness is something thought achieves to a greater or lesser degree,
  rather than a property every mental state has.

New sources: Forman 1990, Metzinger 2020.

## 4. The yogi who thinks "aum"

**Q.** If a yogi meditates and thinks *aum* (not about aum, just thinks it), what is it about?

**A.** It depends on what "about" means. There are two senses, and the aum case separates them.

1. **As a thought *that* something is so, it is about nothing.** Thinking "aum" asserts, asks and
   wants nothing. It has no topic; it is closer to humming than to judging. The word has a
   conventional meaning, but the yogi is not using it to mean anything, any more than a parrot refers
   when it says "Polly". It is a vehicle without content in use. On this reading the case is a clean
   counterexample to "all thought is about something".

2. **As inner hearing, it arguably has an object: the syllable itself, as it sounds.** This is
   Brentano's answer, and his own examples were of this kind: in hearing, something is heard; in
   imagining a sound, a sound is imagined. The object need not be a topic one thinks *about*; it is
   whatever is presented in the act. Silently voicing "aum" presents a sound pattern with pitch,
   length and resonance. "Not about aum, just thinks it" marks exactly the difference between being
   a topic and being a presented object. The intentionalist says the second is enough for aboutness;
   one can reasonably say it is not.

3. **The tradition says it is about something definite, but only when used that way.** *Yoga Sūtra*
   1.27–28 calls *praṇava* (Om) the word expressing Īśvara and prescribes repeating it *and* dwelling
   on its meaning (*taj-japas tad-artha-bhāvanam*). The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad maps A-U-M onto waking,
   dreaming and deep sleep, and the silence after it onto *turīya*, a fourth state beyond all three.
   The tradition itself separates repetition from repetition-with-meaning.

4. **In practice the mantra works more as a tool than as a representation.** It anchors attention and
   crowds out other content; its role is causal. Many traditions describe the syllable thinning out
   and dissolving into the silence after it. That end point is the strong version of the case: no
   syllable, no image, no topic, only wakeful awareness (Forman's pure consciousness event,
   Metzinger's minimal phenomenal experience).

**View.** While the syllable still sounds inwardly, the intentionalist can hold on by appeal to the
presented sound, but that is aboutness at its thinnest, the sense in which a ringing in the ears is
"about" a tone. Once the syllable fades, nothing is left to be about, and the intentionalist must deny
either that the state is mental or that it is real. "About" covers two things, having a topic and
presenting an object; thought can lose the first while keeping the second, and eventually lose both.
