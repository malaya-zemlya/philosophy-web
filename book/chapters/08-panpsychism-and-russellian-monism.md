[panpsychism](../../web/concepts/panpsychism.md) is the view that consciousness is fundamental and widespread, present in some
form even in the simplest physical things. Once regarded as a curiosity, it has returned as a serious
response to the hard problem. This chapter presents the case for it, its most developed form, and the
problems it faces.

The case begins with the distinction between [intrinsic and
relational properties](../../web/concepts/intrinsic-structural-distinction.md). Physics describes matter only by its structure and dynamics, by what it
does. The intrinsic-nature argument proposes that what matter is in itself is experiential, and
Russellian monism builds a metaphysics on that idea. Further arguments say that experience cannot
emerge brutely from the wholly non-experiential, and that there is no non-arbitrary line at which
experience could switch on. Against them stands the reply that strong emergence is acceptable.

The main difficulty is the combination problem: how micro-level experiences or subjects could make up
a unified human mind. Related problems concern the grain of experience and the "decombination" of a
conscious cosmos into individual minds. Cosmopsychism and panqualityism are presented as attempts to
avoid these problems.
