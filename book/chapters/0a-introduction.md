This book is an encyclopedia of the philosophy of mind and its neighbouring fields, centred on the
problem of consciousness. Its entries are not written as freestanding essays. They are distilled
from a *web*: a network of small, precisely defined philosophical objects linked by explicit
relations. A **concept** is a term with its definition. A **claim** is a single proposition that is
true or false. An **argument** derives a claim from premises, or attacks or supports another claim or
argument. A **position** is a named view, defined by the claims it accepts and rejects. A **question**
is an open problem. Each entry in this book is one such object, so every argument names its premises
and every reply names its target.

The web grows through debates. Its claims and arguments come from the philosophical literature, from
the editor, and from structured debates between AI characters who hold different views. The ideas are
always attributed to their real sources, never to the characters, and every attribution is backed by
a reference in the Sources at the back of the book.

The book is arranged in thematic chapters. Each chapter opens with an introduction and then presents
its entries roughly from the basic to the advanced, so that a chapter can be read straight through.
Claims that belong to a particular argument are printed as compact sub-entries directly under it.
Similar chapters are kept together, and the order is chosen so that the book can be read from the
start with few references forward. Chapters 1 to 4 lay the foundations: what consciousness is, the
mind–body problem, the explanatory gap and our access to our own minds, and the main theories of
consciousness. Chapters 5 to 9 examine particular views in depth: functionalism, computation,
illusionism and panpsychism, followed by the unity of consciousness. Chapters 10 to 13 are devoted to
the great thought experiments: zombies, Mary, the puzzle cases for functionalism and the Chinese room.
Chapters 14 to 16 turn to artificial minds, moral status and AI risk, and the book ends with free
will, personal identity and the theory of knowledge (17 to 19). An appendix collects the tools of
argument.

The book can also be used as a work of reference. The index lists every entry and sub-entry
alphabetically, and every cross-reference in the text is a link to the entry it names.
