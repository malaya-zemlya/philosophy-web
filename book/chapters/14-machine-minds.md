This chapter applies the book's questions to present-day artificial intelligence, and especially to
large language models. It opens with the evidential problem. A model can match human behaviour,
including talk about its own experience, so the behavioural-parity argument asks why the evidence
should count for less in a model than in a person. The replies are that such behaviour is trained
mimicry, that the model's reports are contaminated by human text, and that a near-perfect actor
proves nothing. A further group of entries asks what interpretability research on models' internal
representations adds to this evidence.

The middle of the chapter applies global workspace theory to models. Some entries argue that a
model's verbalisable internal workspace, or its chain of thought, meets the functional signature of
conscious access. Others ask what follows if it does.

The last part treats the AI version of the hard problem. A model may face its own explanatory gap when
it tries to account for its states, and the entries ask whether that gap arises from its
self-modelling architecture alone. They also consider whether the AI case could serve as a "model
organism" for studying the human one.
