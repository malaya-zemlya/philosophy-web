Much of the interest in consciousness is practical: it is widely held that whether a being is
conscious bears on how it may be treated. A being with experiences, and especially one that can
suffer, has interests that can be set back. This is usually taken to be the basis of *moral status*,
the property of counting morally for one's own sake. This chapter applies that idea to its most
pressing new case, the [moral status of language models](../../web/questions/llm-moral-status.md).

The entries follow one sustained dispute. On one side is [proteocentrism](../../web/positions/proteocentrism.md), the view that
only protein-based life can be conscious and so only it can have moral status. A more cautious
version holds that biology should serve as the criterion for now, because it is currently the one
sign of consciousness that cannot be forged. On the other side are arguments that this asymmetry is
unfounded. The first-person-access argument and its rebuttal concern whether we know our own
consciousness in a way that justifies treating machines differently. The arguments about forgeable
signs ask what makes a criterion for rights hard to game, and whether biology is the only such
criterion. The two-sided precaution objection observes that caution cuts both ways: wrongly denying
status to a conscious being is also a moral risk.

The general question of why consciousness should matter morally at all is not yet well represented
in the web. The chapter's current entries assume an answer to it.
