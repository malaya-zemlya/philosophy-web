Even if consciousness is physical, there seems to be an [explanatory-gap](../../web/concepts/explanatory-gap.md): no account of
neural processing makes it intelligible why that processing should feel like anything. This chapter
asks what the gap shows. The first group of entries treats it as evidence, as in the gap argument
and McGinn's case for mysterianism, the view that human minds are constitutionally unable to solve
the problem. The phenomenal-concept strategy replies that the gap is just what physicalism predicts,
given the special concepts we use to think about our own experience.

The second group turns to our knowledge of our own minds. [first-person-access](../../web/concepts/first-person-access.md) has
traditionally been taken to be direct and authoritative. The entries ask whether introspective
acquaintance is a datum or a representation, how private experience can be known at all, and what
the psychology of [confabulation](../../web/concepts/confabulation.md) does to the authority of self-report.

The chapter ends with a family of arguments that trace the gap to the limits of self-modelling. No
finite system can represent its entire current state within itself, so any sufficiently complex
system, human or artificial, may face a gap when it tries to explain itself. Whether that
architectural gap is the same as the phenomenal one is the point in dispute.
