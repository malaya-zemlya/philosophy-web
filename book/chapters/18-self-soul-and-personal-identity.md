What makes a person at one time the same person as someone at another time? The [problem of
personal identity](../../web/questions/personal-identity.md) is the subject of the first half of this chapter. On the psychological continuity
view, a person persists through overlapping chains of memory, intention and character. On animalism,
a person is a human animal and persists as that organism does. Each view faces thought experiments:
brain transplants, fission into two equally good successors, and Parfit's spectrum of gradual
rewiring. Parfit concluded that identity is not what matters in survival.

The second half concerns the [soul](../../web/concepts/soul.md), the idea of a non-physical self. It begins with Avicenna's
floating man, who is aware of himself while aware of no body. The positions that follow range from
Aristotelian hylomorphism, on which the soul is the form of the body, to Christian physicalism, which
affirms resurrection without an immaterial soul. The chapter ends with two Indian answers that point
in opposite directions: the Buddhist doctrine that there is no enduring self, and Advaita Vedānta's
identification of the self with Brahman.
