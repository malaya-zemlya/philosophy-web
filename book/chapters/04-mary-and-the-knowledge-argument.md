Frank Jackson's Mary knows every physical fact about colour vision but has lived her whole life in a
black-and-white room. When she first sees red, she seems to learn something new. If she does, the
[knowledge-argument](../../web/concepts/knowledge-argument.md) concludes that some facts are not physical. This chapter presents the
argument and the replies to it, from the most widely held to the most recent.

Everything turns on [what-does-mary-learn](../../web/questions/what-does-mary-learn.md). On the ability hypothesis she gains know-how,
such as the abilities to recognise and imagine red, and no new fact. On the acquaintance hypothesis
she meets the experience directly. The old-fact-new-guise reply says that she grasps an old fact
under a new phenomenal concept, and the indexical reply says that she learns where she is in the
world. Further replies question the setup: whether the argument equivocates on "learning", whether
it refutes only a priori physicalism, and whether "all the physical facts" can even be imagined, or
known by someone who is part of the world they describe.

The chapter ends with LLMary, a version of the thought experiment in which a language model has read
everything about a token it has never processed. The case is wholly physical, and it is used both
against the argument's central inference and to suggest that what Mary gains is relational.
