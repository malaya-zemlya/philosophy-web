A [philosophical-zombie](../../web/concepts/philosophical-zombie.md) is a being physically identical to a conscious human but with no
experience at all. If such a being is even coherently conceivable, the [zombie-conceivability](../../web/arguments/zombie-conceivability.md)
concludes that consciousness is something over and above the physical. This chapter is about that
argument and about the bridge it needs from what can be conceived to what is possible.

The chapter starts with the cases that test the link between behaviour and experience: the perfect
actor, who shows pain without feeling it, and the super-spartans, who feel it without showing it. It
then sets out the argument and the main physicalist responses, classified as type-A, type-B and
type-C materialism. These differ over whether there is an epistemic gap between the physical and the
phenomenal and, if there is, whether it marks a gap in reality.

The second half concerns the modal machinery. The objection from a posteriori necessity says that
conceivability is a poor guide to possibility. Two-dimensional semantics, with its primary and
secondary intensions, is the standard attempt to rescue the bridge, and modal rationalism is the
general thesis that ideal conceivability does imply possibility. The chapter ends with two
challenges: an anti-zombie, whose conceivability would prove too much, and the objection that the
zombie premise cannot be cogently asserted at all.
