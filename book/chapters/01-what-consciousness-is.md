Most of this book turns on one phenomenon: there is something it is like to see a colour, feel a pain
or hear a chord. This chapter fixes the vocabulary that the rest of the book uses for it. It opens
with [philosophy-of-mind](../../web/concepts/philosophy-of-mind.md) as a field and with [consciousness](../../web/concepts/consciousness.md) in its several
senses, then separates the two senses that matter most. [phenomenal-consciousness](../../web/concepts/phenomenal-consciousness.md) is the
felt character of experience. [access-consciousness](../../web/concepts/access-consciousness.md) is the availability of information
for report, reasoning and the control of action. Whether these come apart is itself disputed, and
[access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness](../../web/concepts/access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness.md) sets out what turns on the distinction.

The felt qualities of experience are usually called [qualia](../../web/concepts/qualia.md). Explaining why any physical
process should be accompanied by them is the [hard-problem](../../web/concepts/hard-problem.md), which the chapter states first
as a concept and then as an open question. The empirical side of the subject follows: the search for
[neural-correlates-of-consciousness](../../web/concepts/neural-correlates-of-consciousness.md), a criticism of how that search has been run, and the
question whether experimental science could ever study consciousness exhaustively. The chapter
closes with two clinical cases, pain asymbolia and Jo Cameron, in which the felt and the functional
aspects of pain seem to separate.

Every later chapter takes these terms as given. The two that follow ask what kind of thing
consciousness could be, and why many philosophers think it resists physical explanation.
