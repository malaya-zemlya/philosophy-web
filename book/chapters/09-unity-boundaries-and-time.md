At any moment a person's experiences seem to belong together, as parts of one experience of one
subject. This chapter is about that unity: what it consists in, whether it is necessary, and what
happens at its limits. It begins with the [unity-of-consciousness](../../web/concepts/unity-of-consciousness.md) and the unity thesis,
which says that a subject's simultaneous experiences are necessarily unified. Split-brain cases,
reports from fatigue and anxiety, and the distributed-brain argument all suggest instead that unity
is graded and costly, and that it holds only while the brain functions well.

The [binding-problem](../../web/concepts/binding-problem.md), how separately processed features come to be experienced as one
object, is the narrower perceptual version of the same issue. Temporal unity raises its own
puzzles: the distinction between the time an experience represents and the time at which it occurs,
and postdiction, in which later input shapes what was experienced earlier.

The chapter then turns to boundaries. The boundary problem asks what fixes the edges of a conscious
subject, and the extended-consciousness entries ask whether those edges could reach beyond the skull.
It ends with two questions of origin: how consciousness arose in evolution, and when it begins in the
development of an individual.
