The earlier chapters ask whether consciousness can be explained. This chapter collects attempts to
explain it. It opens with the minimal requirements a proposal must meet to count as a theory of
consciousness, and with the claim that a theory built by conceptual analysis rather than experiment
need not be idle speculation.

The theories differ first over the relation between a conscious state and the mind's awareness of
it, so the distinction between [first-order and higher-order states](../../web/concepts/order-of-representation.md)
comes next. Higher-order theories hold that a state is conscious when the subject is suitably aware
of being in it. [global-workspace](../../web/positions/global-workspace.md) theory holds that a content is conscious when it is
broadcast to many specialised processes at once, and its functional signature is stated as a separate
claim because later chapters apply it to language models. Integrated information theory identifies
consciousness with a measure of a system's integration. Representationalism identifies the felt
character of experience with what the experience represents. Intermediate-level theory locates
consciousness at a middle stage of perceptual processing.

These theories reappear throughout the book, most of all in the chapter on machine minds, where the
question is whether any of them would count an artificial system as conscious. The chapters that
follow examine at greater length the broader views in which such theories are set: functionalism,
computationalism, illusionism and panpsychism.
